Learning goals

The long-run model of Chapters 3–8 tells us where the economy is heading on average. This chapter looks at the ups and downs around that path. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • split actual output into potential output \(\bar{Y}_t\) and short-run output \(\tilde{Y}_t\), and compute \(\tilde{Y}_t\) from data;
  • describe U.S. recessions with data on output, employment and inflation, and measure what they cost;
  • explain the Phillips curve: a booming economy raises inflation, a slump lowers it;
  • use Okun’s law to move between short-run output and unemployment;
  • in Python: shade recessions on a chart, fit a straight line with np.polyfit, compare two vintages of the same data, and simulate inflation year by year with a loop.

The model in code

The short-run model takes the long run as given. Potential output \(\bar{Y}_t\) and the long-run inflation rate \(\bar{\pi}\) come from the long-run model, so they carry bars: they are exogenous here. The model explains how actual output \(Y_t\) and inflation \(\pi_t\) move around them.

Actual output is the long-run trend plus short-run fluctuations (equation 9.1). We measure the fluctuations in percent of potential output and call them short-run output:

\[\tilde{Y}_t \equiv \frac{Y_t - \bar{Y}_t}{\bar{Y}_t} \qquad (9.2)\]

When the economy booms, \(Y_t > \bar{Y}_t\) and \(\tilde{Y}_t > 0\). In a recession, \(\tilde{Y}_t < 0\). Two more relationships complete this first look at the short run:

  • the Phillips curve: inflation rises when output is above potential and falls when it is below, \(\Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\,\tilde{Y}_t\). Chapter 12 calls the slope \(\bar{\nu}\) (the Greek letter nu);
  • Okun’s law: unemployment is above its natural rate \(\bar{u}\) when output is below potential, \(u_t - \bar{u} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\tilde{Y}_t\) (equation 9.3).

In the code, \(\tilde{Y}\), \(\Delta\pi\) and \(u-\bar{u}\) are all measured in percent (or percentage points), as in the textbook’s figures.

One baseline for every diagram. The textbook’s diagrams 9.1 and 9.6 print no numbers, so all the model figures of this notebook share one illustrative baseline:

  • potential output starts at \(\bar{Y}_0 = 100\) and grows by \(\bar{g} = 2\) percent a year;
  • short-run output swings between \(+2\) and \(-2\) percent (the \(\pm 2\%\) marked in Figure 9.1), in a cycle that repeats every 8 years;
  • the slope of the Phillips curve is \(\bar{\nu} = 0.33\), the textbook’s “about 1/3” for the United States;
  • long-run inflation is \(\bar{\pi} = 2\) percent, and Okun’s slope is \(-1/2\).

Figure 9 is the worked answer to a textbook exercise, which gives its own numbers (\(\bar{\nu} = 1\), \(\tilde{Y} = +3\%\)), and we use those there.

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. Writing g=2.0 gives the argument a default value, used whenever we do not pass one. The text in triple quotes is a short description of what the function does. ** means “to the power of”, and np.sin is the sine function, which draws a smooth wave (np.pi is the number π).

def potential_output(t, g=2.0, Y0=100.0):
    """Potential output Ybar_t: a smooth trend that grows g percent a year, from Y0 in year 0."""
    return Y0 * (1 + g / 100) ** t


def actual_output(t, A=2.0, g=2.0, cycle=8):
    """Actual output Y_t: potential output times (1 + a wave of +-A percent that repeats every `cycle` years)."""
    return potential_output(t, g) * (1 + A / 100 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / cycle))


def short_run_output(Y, Ybar):
    """Short-run output in percent: 100 (Y - Ybar) / Ybar   (equation 9.2)."""
    return 100 * (Y - Ybar) / Ybar
def phillips_curve(Y_tilde, nu=0.33):
    """Change in inflation, percentage points: delta pi = nu * Y_tilde   (the Phillips curve, Figure 9.6)."""
    return nu * Y_tilde


def okun_law(Y_tilde, slope=-0.5):
    """Cyclical unemployment u - u_bar, percentage points: slope * Y_tilde   (Okun's law, equation 9.3)."""
    return slope * Y_tilde

Figure 1: Economic fluctuations and short-run output

Goal. See how removing the trend from actual output leaves the ups and downs of the business cycle.

Panel (a) draws actual output \(Y_t\) around a smooth potential output \(\bar{Y}_t\). Panel (b) draws short-run output \(\tilde{Y}_t\) from equation (9.2): the same wiggle, with the trend taken out. The gray bars in panel (a) are the gaps \(Y - \bar{Y}\) at the top of the boom and at the bottom of the slump.

Python note. np.linspace(0, 8, 200) creates 200 evenly spaced numbers from 0 to 8: the years at which we draw the curves. make_subplots(rows=1, cols=2) makes a figure with two panels, and row=..., col=... tells add_trace where to draw. We fix the axis ranges so that you can see the curves move when a parameter changes.

def fluctuations(A=2.0, g=2.0):
    """Figure 9.1: actual and potential output (a) and short-run output (b), over one 8-year cycle."""
    t = np.linspace(0, 8, 200)                          # time, in years
    Y, Ybar = actual_output(t, A, g), potential_output(t, g)
    t_gap = np.array([2.0, 6.0])                        # the top of the boom and the bottom of the slump
    Y_gap, Ybar_gap = actual_output(t_gap, A, g), potential_output(t_gap, g)
    fig = make_subplots(rows=1, cols=2, subplot_titles=("(a) Actual and potential output",
                                                        "(b) Short-run output, Ỹ (percent)"))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=Y, name="Actual output, Y", line=dict(color=BLUE, width=3)), row=1, col=1)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=Ybar, name="Potential output, Ȳ", line=dict(color=GREEN, width=3, dash="dash")),
                  row=1, col=1)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[2, 2, None, 6, 6], y=[Ybar_gap[0], Y_gap[0], None, Ybar_gap[1], Y_gap[1]],
                             name="Gap, Y − Ȳ", mode="lines", line=dict(color=GRAY, width=4)), row=1, col=1)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=t, y=short_run_output(Y, Ybar), name="Short-run output, Ỹ",
                             line=dict(color=TEAL, width=3)), row=1, col=2)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[0, 8], y=[0, 0], name="Ỹ = 0: output at potential", mode="lines",
                             line=dict(color=GREEN, width=2, dash="dash")), row=1, col=2)
    fig.add_annotation(x=2, y=A, xref="x2", yref="y2", text="Boom: Ỹ > 0", showarrow=False, yshift=14)
    fig.add_annotation(x=6, y=-A, xref="x2", yref="y2", text="Slump: Ỹ < 0", showarrow=False, yshift=-14)
    fig.update_xaxes(title_text="Time (years)", range=[0, 8])
    fig.update_yaxes(title_text="Output", range=[94, 145], row=1, col=1)
    fig.update_yaxes(range=[-5.2, 5.2], row=1, col=2)
    fig.update_layout(title=f"Boom: Ỹ = +{A:.1f}%   Slump: Ỹ = −{A:.1f}%   |   potential grows {g:.1f}% a year",
                      legend=dict(orientation="h", y=-0.2, yanchor="top"))
    return fig

Figure 6: The Phillips curve

Goal. Draw the Phillips curve and see how short-run output moves inflation.

Think of a hot holiday toy. When demand is high, the firm that makes it runs overtime and pays its workers more, and the toy is scarce. So its price rises faster than usual. In a booming economy, it is as if every firm made the hot toy: all of them raise prices faster than the current inflation rate, so inflation goes up. In a slump the opposite happens. The Phillips curve puts this in one line:

\[\Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\,\tilde{Y}_t\]

When output is at potential (\(\tilde{Y} = 0\)), inflation is steady. Our function phillips_curve from “The model in code” draws the line. The black dot is the economy, at the short-run output you choose.

Python note. fig.add_shape(type="line", ...) draws a straight segment, here the dashed guides from the dot to the axes. An if ... elif ... else block picks one of three cases.

Figure 9: The Phillips curve and the booming economy

Goal. Answer the textbook’s exercise “Overstimulating the economy”: what happens if policymakers keep output 3 percent above potential every year?

The economy starts at potential with inflation at its long-run rate, \(\bar{\pi} = 2\%\). Then output is held at \(\tilde{Y} = +3\%\). The Phillips curve says inflation rises by \(\Delta\pi = \bar{\nu}\tilde{Y}\) every year:

\[\pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + \bar{\nu}\,\tilde{Y}_t\]

With the textbook’s slope \(\bar{\nu} = 1\), inflation goes from 2 to 5, 8 and 11 percent in three years. A computer can apply this equation year after year: a simulation.

Python note. A for loop repeats the indented lines once for each year. pi_path[-1] is the last number in the list, last year’s inflation, and pi_path.append(...) adds this year’s at the end. range(1, years + 1) counts 1, 2, …, years.

def simulate_inflation(pi_bar=2.0, nu=1.0, Y_tilde=3.0, years=5):
    """Inflation year by year when output is held at Y_tilde: pi_t = pi_(t-1) + nu * Y_tilde."""
    pi_path = [pi_bar]                                    # year 0: inflation at its long-run rate
    for year in range(1, years + 1):
        pi_path.append(pi_path[-1] + phillips_curve(Y_tilde, nu))
    return np.arange(years + 1), np.array(pi_path)

What to notice.

  • Holding output above potential does not raise inflation once: it raises it every year. The cost of a permanent boom is ever-rising inflation.
  • The steeper the Phillips curve, the faster inflation climbs. With the U.S. slope of about 0.33, inflation rises by about 1 point a year instead of 3, slower but without end.
  • Set \(\tilde{Y}\) below zero and the same mechanism brings inflation down: that is how a recession fights inflation.

Summary

  • Actual output is potential output plus short-run fluctuations. Short-run output \(\tilde{Y}_t = (Y_t - \bar{Y}_t)/\bar{Y}_t\) is positive in a boom and negative in a recession.
  • U.S. recessions push output a few percent below potential, cost millions of jobs and, in total, several percent of a year’s GDP. Potential output is only estimated, so the measured gaps change when the data are revised.
  • Inflation usually falls during a recession. The Phillips curve \(\Delta\pi = \bar{\nu}\tilde{Y}\) captures this, with a U.S. slope of about 1/3; oil and other shocks move inflation too.
  • Okun’s law links output and unemployment: each point of output below potential adds about half a point of unemployment.
