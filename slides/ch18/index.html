U.S. federal debt is back where it stood at the end of World War II

99.5% Federal debt held by the public, as a share of GDP, at the end of fiscal year 2025

In 1946 the ratio was 106.1%. By 1974 it was down to 23.2%, and yet the government never paid the war debt back. How can a debt shrink without being repaid, and could it happen again?

Open with the number. It is the last point of the notebook’s Fig. 2: debt held by the public (bonds owned outside the federal government) was 99.5 percent of GDP in FY2025, close to the record of 106.1 percent in FY1946.

The puzzle that runs through the deck: from 1946 to 1974 the debt ratio fell by more than 80 points, while the budget was close to balance in most years. Act II solves it with one line of algebra: when nominal GDP grows faster than the interest rate on the debt, the ratio falls on its own.

Ask the room: how big is 99.5 percent of GDP? (About one year of everything the U.S. economy produces.) Is that a lot? Compared with what: a household mortgage, Japan, the U.K. in 1946?

The epigraph of the textbook chapter jokes that federal deficits have overtaken the number of stars in the galaxy. The chapter’s job is to say when such numbers are a problem and when they are not.

Today: three questions

  • How big are government budgets and debts? U.S. spending, revenue and debt since the 1930s, and other rich countries.
  • What rules tie deficits to debt? The flow and intertemporal budget constraints, the debt-GDP ratio and crowding out.
  • Why is the future the problem? Entitlements, health spending and the debt projections, and the policy choices they force.

These are the three acts of the deck and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide: the facts, the framework, and its uses.

This chapter has no behavioral model to solve for an equilibrium. It rests on accounting relations that hold every year, in every country, whatever voters and politicians decide. That makes them powerful: every policy proposal has to satisfy them.

The headline result: a government’s budget must balance in present value. When GDP grows faster than the interest rate, the debt ratio falls without repayment; rising health spending is the problem that growth will not solve.

The budget arithmetic

Act II

Every year, what the government uses must equal what it raises

\[\underset{\text{uses}}{G_t + Tr_t + iB_t} \;=\; \underset{\text{sources}}{T_t + \Delta B_{t+1} + \Delta M_{t+1}}\]

Set \(\Delta M_{t+1} = 0\) and fold transfers into \(G_t\):

\[B_{t+1} = (1+i)\,B_t + \underset{\text{primary deficit}}{G_t - T_t}\]

Deficit measure formula counts interest?
primary \(G_t - T_t\) no
total (the headline) \(G_t + iB_t - T_t\) yes

The debt rolls forward like a credit-card balance: last year’s balance plus interest plus this year’s overspending.

The flow budget constraint, equations (1) and (2) of the book chapter’s Model overview. \(B_t\) is the debt at the start of year t; \(\Delta B_{t+1} = B_{t+1} - B_t\) is new borrowing; \(\Delta M_{t+1}\) is new money. The U.S. Treasury does not pay its bills by printing money (the fiscal side of inflation was Chapter 8), so we drop it.

Derivation in two steps: borrowing covers the total deficit, \(\Delta B_{t+1} = G_t + iB_t - T_t\); then add \(B_t\) to both sides and group \(B_t + iB_t = (1+i)B_t\).

Dividing by \(B_t\): the debt grows at the rate \(i + (G_t - T_t)/B_t\). A balanced primary budget means the debt grows at the rate i; a balanced total budget means it stays put; to hold the dollar debt constant, the primary surplus must equal the interest bill.

U.S. numbers (notebook, Fig. 2 key numbers): in FY1999 the total surplus was 1.3 percent of GDP but the primary surplus 3.8 percent, because 2.5 percent went to interest. In FY2025 the total deficit of 5.9 percent was 3.3 percent interest and only 2.6 percent primary deficit.

The notebook’s Check 1, based on Exercise 5 of the textbook: a government that owes 5 trillion dollars at 5 percent interest, with GDP of 20 trillion growing at 4 percent a year.

Case (a), primary budget balanced: the total deficit equals the interest bill, 0.250, then 0.263, then 0.276 trillion. The ratio grows by 0.96 percent a year, exactly 1.05/1.04 − 1.

Case (b), total budget balanced: taxes pay spending and interest, a primary surplus of 0.25 trillion. The ratio falls by 3.85 percent a year, about the growth rate of GDP.

Ask: which of the two would a politician call “a balanced budget”? Both, which is why the next slide matters.

“Balanced” depends on whether interest is counted

If the government balances its budget, its debt stops growing.

Only if the total budget balances. With a balanced primary budget the debt still grows at the interest rate, because the interest is borrowed. In FY2025 the U.S. primary deficit was 2.6% of GDP; the total deficit, with interest, was 5.9%.

A common confusion in news coverage and in student answers. Fiscal plans often target the primary balance because it measures what current policy adds; the interest bill is the cost of past borrowing.

The distinction becomes more important as the debt grows: at a debt of 100 percent of GDP, every point of interest is a point of GDP in the total deficit. In FY2025 net interest (3.2 percent of GDP in Table 18.1) exceeded national defense (3.0 percent).

Over time, the budget must balance in present value

\[\underset{\text{pdv of spending}}{G_1 + \frac{G_2}{1+i}} \;+\; \underset{\text{initial debt}}{(1+i)B_1} \;=\; \underset{\text{pdv of taxes}}{T_1 + \frac{T_2}{1+i}}\]

A one-dollar rise in … changes \(T_2\) by why
\(T_1\) (a tax cut is a fall) \(-(1+i)\) borrowed today, repaid with interest
\(G_1\) \(+(1+i)\) the same: financed by borrowing
\(G_2\) \(+1\) paid in the same period
the initial debt \(B_1\) \(+(1+i)^2\) interest accumulates for two periods

With \(i = 5\%\), a tax cut of 100 today means 105 of extra taxes tomorrow.

The intertemporal budget constraint, equation (3). Derivation: write equation (2) for periods 1 and 2; nobody lends after period 2, so \(B_3 = 0\); solve the period-2 constraint for \(B_2 = (T_2 - G_2)/(1+i)\) and substitute into the period-1 constraint. With more periods each extra term is discounted once more. The full derivation is in the book chapter’s Model overview.

Equivalent form, equation (4): the present value of the budget balances equals \((1+i)B_1\). A deficit in one period must be matched by a surplus of equal present value in another, and a government that starts in debt needs surpluses large enough to pay it.

The notebook’s Check 2: initial debt 25 trillion, spending 7 trillion in each period, taxes 5 trillion in period 1. Then \(B_2\) = 28.25 trillion and period-2 taxes must be 36.66 trillion: both sides of equation (3) equal 39.917. Check 3: cutting period-1 taxes by 1 trillion raises period-2 taxes by 1.05 trillion.

“Period 2” can stand for the whole future. The constraint does not say when, only that the present values must add up.

The timing of taxes can change; their present value cannot

  • Once spending and the initial debt are set, the constraint fixes the present value of all taxes. A tax cut today is a tax rise later.
  • Ricardian equivalence: households that see the later taxes save the whole tax cut, so their consumption does not move (Chapter 16).
  • Generational accounting: when “later” is decades away, the people who pay are not the people who benefited.

Debt moves burdens across time and generations. Whether that is fair depends on who gains from the spending.

Ricardian equivalence goes back to Barro (1974, Journal of Political Economy). It needs households that look far ahead, can borrow and lend freely, and care about the heirs who will pay the later taxes. Most evidence finds that private saving offsets only part of a deficit; the textbook says at most about half. We test it with Fig. 5.

Generational accounting is due to Auerbach, Gokhale and Kotlikoff (1994, Journal of Economic Perspectives). It computes the lifetime net taxes that current policy implies for each birth cohort, and the extra taxes future cohorts would need to satisfy the intertemporal constraint.

The textbook’s example of a fair transfer: World War II. The generation that fought it bore the casualties and the rationing; borrowing let later generations, who also benefited from the victory, share the cost.

Ask: is borrowing to fight climate change more like the war, or more like borrowing for current consumption?

What matters is the debt relative to GDP

\[b_t = \frac{1+i}{1+g}\,b_{t-1} + d_t \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad b_t - b_{t-1} \;\approx\; (i-g)\,b_{t-1} + d_t\]

  • \(b_t\) is the debt-GDP ratio, \(d_t\) the primary deficit in % of GDP, \(g\) the growth of nominal GDP.
  • Two forces: new primary deficits add to the ratio; old debt grows with interest at \(i\) while GDP grows at \(g\).
  • When \(g > i\), the economy outgrows its debt, even with a balanced primary budget.

GDP is the tax base: a debt that grows more slowly than GDP becomes easier to carry every year.

Equations (5) and (6). Derivation: divide equation (2) by \(Y_t\); write \(B_t/Y_t = b_{t-1}/(1+g)\); subtract \(b_{t-1}\) and use \((1+i)/(1+g) - 1 = (i-g)/(1+g)\). The approximation drops the division by 1 + g, close to 1 when g is a few percent.

The same result from the growth rules: with a balanced primary budget the debt grows at i and GDP at g, so the ratio grows at about i − g. With a balanced total budget the debt is constant, so the ratio shrinks at about g. That is Check 1 again: +0.96 percent and −3.85 percent a year.

Both i and g are nominal: both include inflation. The textbook’s analogy: lenders lend more to someone whose income is high and rising.

Notation reminder: the statistical tables report the ratio as end-of-year debt over that year’s GDP, which is why \(b_t = B_{t+1}/Y_t\) in the book chapter.

At a debt near 100% of GDP, a point of interest weighs as much as a point of deficit

A one-point rise in … changes \(b_t - b_{t-1}\) by about at \(b_{t-1} = 99.5\%\)
the primary deficit \(d_t\) \(+1\) point \(+1\) point
the interest rate \(i\) \(+b_{t-1}/100\) points +1 point
nominal GDP growth \(g\) \(-b_{t-1}/100\) points −1 point

The higher the debt, the more the ratio reacts to interest rates and growth, and the less it is under the control of current policy.

Comparative statics of equation (6), holding last year’s ratio fixed. The notebook’s Check 6: one point more interest at a ratio of 99.5 percent raises the yearly change by 0.957 points, that is \(b/(1+g)/100\), about \(b/100\).

The policy meaning: a government with a debt of 100 percent of GDP is exposed to the bond market. If interest rates rise by two points, the deficit would have to fall by about two points of GDP just to keep the ratio on its old path. When the U.S. debt was 30 percent of GDP, the same rise in rates mattered a third as much.

Whether a debt is sustainable depends on \(i\) versus \(g\)

\[-d = \frac{i-g}{1+g}\,b \qquad\qquad b_t = b^* + \left(\frac{1+i}{1+g}\right)^{t}(b_0 - b^*), \quad b^* = \frac{1+g}{g-i}\,d\]

Case what happens to the ratio example, \(b_0 = 99.5\%\), \(g = 4\%\)
\(i < g\) converges to \(b^*\) from any start, even with permanent deficits \(i = 3\%\), \(d = 2\%\): \(b^* = 208\%\)
\(i = g\) adds up all primary deficits no surplus needed to hold 99.5%
\(i > g\) \(b^*\) is a tipping point; only surpluses hold the ratio \(i = 5\%\): surplus 0.96% a year

When \(i > g\), a government needs a primary surplus just to stand still, and the surplus grows with the debt.

Equation (7) is the primary surplus that holds the ratio constant; equation (8) is the closed-form path for a constant primary deficit d, with \(a = (1+i)/(1+g)\). Each year the gap to \(b^*\) is multiplied by a.

The notebook’s Check 5:

  • i = 5, g = 4: the stabilizing surplus is 0.957 percent of GDP; with it the ratio stays at 99.50 percent for 31 years.
  • i = 3, d = 2: \(b^*\) = 208 percent, reached only slowly: after 31 years the ratio is 127.58 percent (simulated and closed form agree). The gap shrinks by about 1 percent a year.
  • i = 5 with a primary surplus of 1 percent: the tipping point is 104.0 percent. Starting at 99.5 percent the ratio drifts down to 97.9 after 31 years; starting at 108.5 it climbs to 110.1.

Confidence and default: if lenders start to doubt repayment, they ask for a higher i, which raises a and the required surplus, and can tip a stable ratio into an explosive one. The fear can fulfill itself.

Growth, not repayment, paid for most of the war debt

After the war, three quarters of the fall came from GDP growing faster than the interest rate. Since 2007, primary deficits did the work, in the other direction.

The notebook’s debt_change_parts: the change in the ratio minus the sum of primary deficits is the term \((i-g)\,b/(1+g)\) of equation (6), summed over the years. Primary balances come from revenue and spending excluding interest, available from FY1950.

FY1950–1974: the ratio fell by 55.4 points; primary surpluses paid for 14.1 of them, and 41.3 came from growth (plus inflation) outpacing the interest rate. FY2007–2025: primary deficits added 80.5 points, but low interest rates and the inflation of 2021–23 offset 16.2 of them.

Hall and Sargent (2011, American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics) do a finer decomposition of the post-war decline into growth, inflation and the returns paid to bondholders, and reach the same conclusion: the U.S. grew and inflated its way out of most of the war debt.

The textbook calls growth one of the “magic elixirs” of budget problems.

The U.S. did not repay its war debt

After World War II, the U.S. paid down its debt with years of budget surpluses.

From FY1950 to FY1974 the dollar debt was rolled over, not repaid. The ratio fell 55.4 points, and primary surpluses explain only 14.1 of them; the rest came from GDP outgrowing the interest rate.

The myth is attractive because it fits the household analogy: a family that borrowed in bad times pays it back in good times. The government did run modest primary surpluses after the war, but the decisive force was the denominator.

Ask: why did nominal GDP grow faster than the interest rate in those years? Strong real growth (the golden age of the 1950s and 1960s), inflation at times above what bondholders expected, and interest rates on government debt kept low by regulation and, until 1951, by the Federal Reserve.

That last point links to the next slide: only unexpected inflation helps.

Only surprise inflation erodes the debt

Inflation shrinks the debt ratio, so a heavily indebted government can just let prices rise.

Expected inflation is already in the interest rate: \(\frac{1+i}{1+g} = \frac{1+r}{1+g_{real}}\). With \(r = 1\%\) and real growth 2%, the ratio after 10 years is 99.735% whether inflation is 0% or 4%. Only inflation that surprises lenders after they bought the bonds lowers the real burden.

The Fisher equation (Chapter 8): \(1 + i = (1+r)(1+\pi)\) and \(1 + g = (1+g_{real})(1+\pi)\). The inflation factor cancels in the ratio that drives equation (5).

The notebook’s Check 6: with π = 0, i = 1.00 and g = 2.00 percent; with π = 4 percent, i = 5.04 and g = 6.08 percent. The debt ratio after 10 years is 99.735 percent in both cases.

The surprise inflation of 2021–23 is part of the −16.2 points of the previous table, and part of the fall in measured net debt in 2022 (Fig. 4). But a government cannot surprise lenders forever: once they expect inflation, they demand a higher i. This is why very high debts raise fears of inflation, and why central bank independence matters (Chapter 8).

Crowding out

Let students commit. The identity of the previous slide says a deficit must come out of investment unless private or foreign saving rises. Which happened?

Deficits nearly doubled after 1980, yet the investment rate did not fall

The textbook’s summary: the U.S. debt ratio is not especially large by international standards, and foreign saving has kept investment close to its post-war average. The worry is the future (Act III).

A correlation between deficits and investment does not prove crowding out

Deficits and investment fall together, so deficits must be crowding out investment.

In a recession investment falls, tax revenue falls and spending rises: both lines drop for the same reason. The correlation of the yearly changes is positive in all 36 OECD countries with data (median 0.41), from 0.01 in Colombia to 0.75 in Japan.

Numbers: the notebook’s cross-country version of Fig. 5 (fig. 5b in the book chapter), with the general government budget balance (net lending) and private investment from the OECD. The U.S. correlation with this measure is 0.62, close to the 0.55 of the federal measure.

A third variable, the business cycle, drives both series. To measure crowding out we would need changes in deficits that are not caused by the cycle, for example tax laws passed for long-run reasons. This is the identification problem every policy student meets again in empirical work.

Ireland, in the same chart: the budget balance fell to −32.2 percent of GDP in 2010, when the government rescued its banks; investment jumped to 51.5 percent of GDP in 2019 for an accounting reason (multinationals moving intellectual property, Chapter 17).

  • Social Security: 4.0% of GDP in 2000, 5.6% in 2030, 6.0% in 2056, then flat. Some mix of higher payroll taxes and lower benefits must cover it.
  • Medicare and Medicaid: 3.1% in 2000, 6.0% in 2030, 8.1% in 2056, still rising when pensions have stopped.
  • Everything else, defense included, shrinks as a share of GDP.

Numbers: the notebook’s Fig. 7 key numbers. The textbook’s case study on Social Security: spending rises “from about 4.2 percent of GDP to about 6 percent”. The new data: 4.0 → 5.6 → 6.0 percent, close to it.

Social Security is pay-as-you-go: current workers’ payroll taxes, split between workers and employers, pay current retirees. As the ratio of workers to retirees falls, either the tax rate rises or benefits fall. Once the baby boom has retired, the ratio stabilizes, and so does spending.

Health is different: the 2002 projection had health at 7.7 percent of GDP by 2030 and 14.9 percent by 2075. The new projection is slower (6.0 percent in 2030), but still rising. The next slide splits the reasons.

Aging and costs that outpace income both raise the health share

\[h = \frac{H}{Y} = \frac{N_r}{N}\times\frac{c}{y} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad g_h = \underset{\text{aging}}{g_{N_r/N}} + \underset{\text{excess cost growth}}{(g_c - g_y)}\]

  • If the cost per recipient grows 3% a year and income per person 2%, the share grows about 1% a year and doubles in about 70 years.
  • Add 1% a year of aging and it doubles in about 35 years.
  • With revenue at a fixed share of GDP, a rising \(h\) raises \(d_t\) every year, and the debt ratio has no resting point.

Taxes must rise or spending must fall: the arithmetic leaves no third option.

Equation (11). Health spending H is the number of recipients \(N_r\) times the cost per recipient c; GDP is the population N times GDP per person y. The growth rate of a product is the sum of the growth rates; that of a ratio is the difference.

The notebook’s Check 8: with costs growing 3 percent and income 2 percent a year, the share after 70 years is 9.90 percent of GDP, 1.98 times its start; the exact yearly growth is 0.980 percent. The rule of 70 at work.

The textbook notes that CBO’s long-run projections assume costs per recipient grow one point faster than GDP per person, slower than in recent decades.

Why might costs grow faster than income? New medical technology (Newhouse 1992, Journal of Economic Perspectives), and the value of life itself (next figure and the policy slide).

Should a law cap government debt?

The EU’s rules set reference values of 3% of GDP for deficits and 60% for debt. Should countries tie their hands with debt limits?

What the framework says

  • No ratio is dangerous on its own: the U.K. carried 270% in 1946.
  • What matters is \(i - g\), the debt and the primary balance.
  • A rule on \(d\) is clearer than a rule on \(b\).

What is debated

  • Is there a growth “cliff” near 90% of GDP?
  • The cliff shrank once errors were fixed.
  • Strict rules can force cuts in a slump.

The left column is Act II. Equation (7) says the surplus needed to hold the ratio depends on (i − g) b; equation (8) says that with i < g the ratio converges whatever its start. So a single ceiling for every country and every interest rate has no basis in the arithmetic. A rule on the primary balance, or on spending growth, targets the variable the government controls.

The research. Reinhart and Rogoff (2010, “Growth in a Time of Debt”, American Economic Review Papers and Proceedings) reported that growth falls sharply in countries whose debt exceeds about 90 percent of GDP. Herndon, Ash and Pollin (2014, Cambridge Journal of Economics) replicated the study, found a spreadsheet error and debatable weighting choices, and found no sharp threshold. Debt and growth are negatively correlated, but causality runs both ways: slow growth also raises debt ratios. Contested.

The EU’s reference values date from the Maastricht Treaty of 1992 and the Stability and Growth Pact. They were often broken and were reformed in 2024 toward country-specific paths for net spending.

Ask the policy students: should a debt rule be suspended in a recession or a pandemic? Who decides when the emergency is over?

Raise taxes or cut spending?

Closing a primary deficit of about 2% of GDP holds the U.S. debt ratio near today’s level. Should it be done with taxes, spending cuts, or both?

What the accounting says

  • Either works for the debt: a point is a point.
  • Revenue has stayed between 14% and 20% of GDP.
  • Health and pensions are most of the spending.

What is debated

  • Spending cuts may cost less output than tax rises.
  • That finding depends on how plans are measured.
  • Timing: a cut in a slump deepens it.

The left column: equation (5) does not care whether d falls because T rises or G falls. Fig. 6b: a cut of 2 points of GDP every year keeps the ratio near 102 percent. Table 18.1: health, Social Security and interest take 14.9 of the 23.1 points of spending; interest cannot be cut directly.

The research. Alesina, Favero and Giavazzi (2019, Austerity: When It Works and When It Doesn’t, Princeton University Press) study fiscal plans in 16 OECD countries and find that consolidations based on spending cuts cost much less output than those based on tax increases. Guajardo, Leigh and Pescatori (2014, Journal of the European Economic Association), using IMF narrative data on consolidations, find that consolidations are contractionary on average, and that earlier evidence of “expansionary austerity” came partly from how fiscal changes were measured. The size of the difference between tax- and spending-based plans is contested.

Timing links to the short-run model of Chapters 11–13: a consolidation in a recession lowers demand when output is already below potential.

Ask the policy students: which would you cut first, and which group would bear the cost?

Who should pay for rising health spending?

If richer people rationally want to spend more of their income on health, how should a government pay for it?

The case it is optimal

  • Extra consumption adds less as we get richer.
  • An extra year of life does not lose value.
  • New treatments drive costs; people want them.

The case for restraint

  • U.S. regions spend very differently, with similar results.
  • Higher taxes have their own costs.
  • Each generation’s bill is set by the last.

The left column is the textbook’s argument, from Hall and Jones (2007, “The Value of Life and the Rise in Health Spending”, Quarterly Journal of Economics): because the marginal utility of consumption falls as income rises while the value of an extra year of life does not, the optimal health share rises with income. Their simulations suggest the optimal U.S. share could exceed 30 percent by mid-century. Newhouse (1992, Journal of Economic Perspectives) found that new medical technology, not aging or insurance alone, explains most of the long-run rise in costs.

The right column. Fisher and coauthors (2003, Annals of Internal Medicine) found that U.S. regions with much higher Medicare spending per person did not deliver better quality or survival, evidence that part of U.S. spending is waste. Financing a rising share through taxes raises tax rates, with their own effects on work and saving. Generational accounting (Auerbach, Gokhale and Kotlikoff 1994) shows how current policy shifts the bill to later cohorts.

Options the textbook mentions besides taxes: private insurance and mandated health-saving accounts, each with its own problems.

Ask the policy students: should the government pay for every new treatment that works, whatever it costs?

“The debt never has to be repaid”

A skeptic says: “The U.S. has never repaid its debt; it just rolls it over. Japan’s debt is above 230% of GDP and it borrows cheaply. The budget constraint is a textbook fiction.”

Reply: rolling over is fine, and the arithmetic shows when. If \(i < g\), the ratio settles even with deficits; that is how the war debt shrank. But the constraint binds in present value: if \(i\) rises above \(g\), only primary surpluses hold the ratio, and interest already costs more than defense.

Take the objection seriously. The skeptic is right that governments almost never repay debt in the household sense, and that Japan (gross debt 237 percent of GDP in 2024) has not had a crisis. Blanchard (2019, “Public Debt and Low Interest Rates”, American Economic Review) argued that when the safe interest rate is below the growth rate, as it was for most of U.S. history, debt may have no fiscal cost and a limited welfare cost. Mian, Straub and Sufi (2022, “A Goldilocks Theory of Fiscal Deficits”, NBER Working Paper 29707) model how the room for deficits depends on how much interest rates rise as debt grows.

The reply uses Act II. Equation (8): with i < g the ratio converges to b, even with a permanent primary deficit (i = 3, g = 4, d = 2 gives b = 208 percent). With i > g, b* becomes a tipping point and equation (7) asks for a surplus proportional to the debt (0.96 percent of GDP at i = 5, g = 4). Fig. 6b: one point of i above g turns 164 percent into 209 percent in 2056.

Since 2022, interest rates have risen: U.S. net interest was 3.2 percent of GDP in FY2025, more than defense (3.0). And a higher debt makes i itself sensitive to lenders’ confidence.

Ask: what would have to be true for the U.S. to keep rolling over forever?

What to remember from this chapter

  • The U.S. federal government spends about 23% of GDP and collects about 17%; its debt, 99.5% of GDP, is back near the 1946 record.
  • Each year, the debt rolls forward with interest plus the primary deficit; over time, the budget must balance in present value.
  • What matters is debt over GDP: when \(g > i\) it falls without repayment, as after World War II; when \(i > g\), only primary surpluses hold it.
  • Deficits must be financed by private or foreign saving, or they crowd out investment.
  • The fiscal problem of this century is health spending, rising faster than income in almost every rich country.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: how much can a government borrow, and why does growth matter more than repayment? A good answer: as much as lenders believe it can service out of future taxes; what matters is the debt relative to GDP, which falls on its own when GDP grows faster than the interest rate, and rises unless the primary balance is in surplus when it does not.

For the policy students, three habits: ask whether a deficit is primary or total; compare i with g before judging a debt; and look at the projections, not only today’s numbers.
