Learning goals

This chapter is about measurement: how economists turn millions of transactions into a few numbers that describe a whole economy. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • explain why production = expenditure = income, and split GDP with the national income identity \(Y = C + I + G + NX\);
  • describe how the composition of U.S. GDP and labor’s share of income have changed since 1929;
  • separate nominal from real GDP with Laspeyres, Paasche and chain-weighted indexes, and measure inflation with the GDP deflator;
  • compare GDP across countries with exchange rates and purchasing power parity;
  • read CSV files with pandas, compute new columns, work with dates, and draw area and line charts with Plotly Express.

def slider_figure(make_figure, name, values, start, label=None):
    """Precompute make_figure(name=value) for every value and join the results with one slider.

    The website cannot run Python, so each interactive figure also gets this precomputed version.
    """
    figures = [make_figure(**{name: v}) for v in values]
    first = values.index(start)
    fig = go.Figure(layout=figures[first].layout)
    owner = []                                  # which precomputed figure each trace comes from
    for i, f in enumerate(figures):
        for trace in f.data:
            trace.visible = (i == first)
            fig.add_trace(trace)
            owner.append(i)
    steps = []
    for i, f in enumerate(figures):
        layout = {"title": f.layout.title.to_plotly_json(),
                  "annotations": [a.to_plotly_json() for a in f.layout.annotations],
                  "shapes": [s.to_plotly_json() for s in f.layout.shapes]}
        visible = [o == i for o in owner]
        steps.append(dict(method="update", label=str(values[i]), args=[{"visible": visible}, layout]))
    fig.update_layout(sliders=[dict(active=first, steps=steps, pad=dict(t=60),
                                    currentvalue=dict(prefix=(label or name) + " = "))])
    return fig

The model in code

This chapter has no economic model yet. Instead it has a few accounting rules that we will use in every later chapter.

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the market value of the final goods and services produced in an economy in a given period. It can be measured in three ways that give the same number: as total production, as total spending, and as total income. On the spending side, the national income identity splits GDP by who buys the goods:

\[Y = C + I + G + NX \qquad (2.1)\]

where \(Y\) is GDP, \(C\) consumption, \(I\) investment, \(G\) government purchases, and \(NX\) net exports (exports minus imports). To compare the parts, we divide each one by GDP and get its share.

GDP measured at today’s prices is nominal GDP. It can rise because prices rise or because the economy produces more. Real GDP values production at a fixed set of prices, so it only changes when quantities change:

\[\text{nominal GDP} = \text{price level} \times \text{real GDP} \qquad (2.2)\]

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. The text in triple quotes is a short description of what the function does. .sum() adds up all the values of a column or a row.

def share(part, total):
    """Share of a part in a total, in percent (Tables 2.1 and 2.2)."""
    return 100 * part / total


def gdp_value(prices, quantities):
    """Value of production: price times quantity, added over all goods (nominal or real GDP)."""
    return (prices * quantities).sum()


def chain_growth(g_laspeyres, g_paasche):
    """Chain-weighted growth: the average of the Laspeyres and Paasche growth rates (Section 2.3)."""
    return (g_laspeyres + g_paasche) / 2

Nominal GDP uses each year’s own prices. Real GDP holds the prices fixed. The Laspeyres index measures the change from 2026 to 2027 with the initial (2026) prices, and the Paasche index with the final (2027) prices. Chain weighting averages the two growth rates.

Python note. An f-string such as f"{x:,.0f}" inserts the value of a variable into a piece of text: , adds thousands separators, .0f shows no decimals and .1% shows a share as a percentage with one decimal.

Comparing countries: exchange rates and purchasing power parity

To compare GDP across countries we first convert it to a common currency with the exchange rate. But prices also differ across countries, so we then value production at common prices, which is called purchasing power parity (PPP):

\[\text{real GDP}^{\text{U.S. prices}}_{\text{China}} = \frac{\text{price level}_{\text{U.S.}}}{\text{price level}_{\text{China}}}\times\text{nominal GDP}^{\text{dollars}}_{\text{China}} \qquad (2.6)\]

def to_dollars(gdp_local, exchange_rate):
    """Convert GDP in local currency into dollars at the market exchange rate (Section 2.4)."""
    return gdp_local / exchange_rate


def at_common_prices(gdp_dollars, relative_price_level):
    """Real GDP at U.S. prices: dollar GDP divided by the price level relative to the U.S. (equation 2.6)."""
    return gdp_dollars / relative_price_level


china_dollars = to_dollars(95.1, 6.91)                  # 95.1 trillion yuan, 6.91 yuan per dollar (2019)
china_ppp = at_common_prices(china_dollars, 0.684)      # goods in China cost 68.4% of U.S. prices
print(f"China at the exchange rate: ${china_dollars:.1f} trillion, {china_dollars / 20.6:.0%} of U.S. GDP")
print(f"China at common prices:     ${china_ppp:.1f} trillion, {china_ppp / 20.6:.0%} of U.S. GDP")
China at the exchange rate: $13.8 trillion, 67% of U.S. GDP
China at common prices:     $20.1 trillion, 98% of U.S. GDP

With the textbook’s 2019 numbers, China’s economy looks like two-thirds of the U.S. economy at the exchange rate, but almost exactly as large at common prices.

The textbook stacks the shares on top of each other: net exports at the bottom, then consumption, investment, and government purchases up to 100 percent. px.area stacks areas in the order of category_orders. Because net exports are negative in recent decades, consumption starts below zero in those years.

ORDER = ["Net exports", "Consumption", "Investment", "Government purchases"]
COLORS = {"Net exports": "#e0a33b", "Consumption": "#5aa0d8", "Investment": GREEN, "Government purchases": TEAL}
LABELS = {"year": "Year", "share": "Percent of GDP", "component": "Component"}


def composition_chart(style="stacked areas"):
    """Figure 2.1: the expenditure shares of U.S. GDP, 1929-2025, stacked or as separate lines."""
    if style == "stacked areas":
        fig = px.area(long, x="year", y="share", color="component", color_discrete_map=COLORS,
                      category_orders={"component": ORDER}, labels=LABELS)
    else:
        fig = px.line(long, x="year", y="share", color="component", color_discrete_map=COLORS,
                      category_orders={"component": ORDER}, labels=LABELS)
    fig.add_hline(y=0, line_dash="dash", line_color="black", line_width=1)
    fig.update_xaxes(range=[1929, 2025])
    fig.update_yaxes(range=[-10, 100])
    fig.update_layout(title="Composition of U.S. GDP: stable shares, except during World War II")
    return fig

  • From 1950 on, the shares hardly move: consumption averages about 64 percent, government purchases about 20 percent and investment about 17 percent (see the numbers below).

The function below builds the table for any year. Population is in thousands, so GDP per person in dollars is billions × 1,000,000 / population.

EXPENDITURE = {"gdp": "Gross domestic product", "consumption": "Personal consumption expenditures",
               "motor_vehicles": "   Motor vehicles and parts", "food": "   Food", "housing": "   Housing",
               "medical": "   Medical care", "investment": "Gross private domestic investment",
               "structures": "   Structures (nonresidential)", "equipment": "   Equipment",
               "ipp": "   Intellectual property products", "residential": "   Residential",
               "government": "Government purchases", "defense": "   National defense",
               "net_exports": "Net exports of goods and services", "exports": "   Exports", "imports": "   Imports"}


def expenditure_table(year):
    """Table 2.1: the expenditure approach to U.S. GDP in one year."""
    row = nipa.loc[year, list(EXPENDITURE)]
    table = pd.DataFrame({"Trillions of dollars": row / 1000,
                          "Share of GDP (percent)": share(row, row["gdp"]),
                          "Per person (dollars)": row * 1_000_000 / nipa.loc[year, "population"]})
    table.index = list(EXPENDITURE.values())
    return table.round(1)

Because the four shares always add up to 100, the four changes must add up to zero: a rise in one share is always matched by falls in others. The chart below shows the changes directly.

Python note. wide.loc[base_year:] - wide.loc[base_year] subtracts one row from every row of the table, column by column. The result is the change of each share since the base year, in percentage points.

CHANGE_COLORS = {"Consumption": BLUE, "Investment": GREEN, "Government purchases": TEAL, "Net exports": "#e0a33b"}


def changes_chart(base_year=1970):
    """Figure 2.2, second view: change in each expenditure share since base_year (percentage points)."""
    changes = (wide.loc[base_year:] - wide.loc[base_year]).reset_index()
    long_changes = changes.melt(id_vars="year", var_name="component", value_name="change")

Figure 3: Labor’s share of GDP

Goal. Check one of the classic “stylized facts” of macroeconomics: that labor earns about two-thirds of GDP, year after year.

The income approach splits GDP into the income earned by workers (compensation: wages, salaries and benefits) and the income earned by the owners of capital. Two parts need care. Taxes on production (such as sales taxes) are income of neither, so they are left out. And the income of the self-employed (proprietors’ income) mixes both, so it is split between labor and capital in the same proportion as in the rest of the economy. The result is

\[\text{labor share} = \frac{\text{compensation}}{\text{GDI} - \text{taxes on production} - \text{proprietors' income}}\]

where GDI is gross domestic income, the income-side measure of GDP.

Figure 5: The annual inflation rate using the GDP deflator

Goal. Turn the price level into an inflation rate, the percentage change of the price level.

FRED’s “Percent Change from Year Ago” compares each quarter with the same quarter one year earlier:

\[\pi_t = 100 \times \left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-4}} - 1\right)\]

where \(P_t\) is the GDP deflator in quarter \(t\). A second common measure compares each quarter with the quarter before and turns the change into an annual rate: \(100 \times \left[(P_t/P_{t-1})^4 - 1\right]\).

Python note. .shift(4) moves a column down by four rows, so that each quarter sits next to the value from one year earlier. The first four rows get NaN, because there is nothing to compare them with.

year_ago = 100 * (level / level.shift(4) - 1)
quarterly = 100 * ((level / level.shift(1)) ** 4 - 1)

Summary

  • GDP is the market value of final goods and services. Production, expenditure and income are three ways to measure it, and they give the same number (up to a small statistical discrepancy).
  • The national income identity \(Y = C + I + G + NX\) splits spending. In the United States, consumption is about two-thirds of GDP, and since the 1970s its rise has been matched by falling government purchases and a trade deficit.
  • Labor earns roughly two-thirds of income, but its share has drifted down since 1970 and is now below 60 percent.
  • Real GDP values production at common prices. Chain weighting averages the Laspeyres and Paasche growth rates; nominal growth is about inflation plus real growth. Comparing countries also needs common prices (purchasing power parity), which shrinks the measured gap between rich and poor countries.
  • The next chapter uses real GDP per person to study long-run economic growth.
