Lesson 1: Capital, skills, hours and ideas set living standards

Goal. Recall what makes some countries rich and others poor, and measure how much of the gap capital explains.

In the long run, a country’s income per person depends on four things: how much it invests in machines and in people’s skills, how many hours people work, the stock of ideas and technology it can use, and how efficiently it combines all of these. You built this in the production model of Chapter 4, where output per person is

\[y = \bar{A}\,k^{1/3}.\]

Measured relative to the United States, a country’s income is its total factor productivity (TFP) \(\bar{A}\) times the part that capital alone explains, \(k^{1/3}\). See Chapter 4, Figure 5 (the prediction from capital alone) and Chapter 4, Figure 7 (the TFP that makes the model fit). The Solow model of Chapter 5, Figure 4 then explained capital by the investment rate, and the Romer model of Chapter 6, Figure 2 showed that sustained growth comes from new ideas. Because an idea can be used by everyone at once, income per person grows with the whole stock of knowledge.

Python note. a.merge(b, on=[...]) joins two tables row by row, matching the columns listed in on. Here both tables have one row per country, so the result does too.

Lesson 2: Transition dynamics explain differences in growth rates

Goal. See why countries far below their steady state grow fast, and check it with Japan’s catch-up.

A country on its balanced growth path grows at the rate set by the growth of world knowledge. A country below its steady state grows faster than that, and the gap shrinks; a country above it grows more slowly. This is the principle of transition dynamics. You first saw it in the Solow model and then in the cross-country data of Chapter 5, Figure 8 and Chapter 5, Figure 9, and in the combined Solow–Romer model of Chapter 6, Figure 8. The long series for each country are in Chapter 3, Figure 6.

Python note. pivot(index="year", columns="country", values="gdppc") reshapes a long table (one row per country and year) into a wide one: one row per year and one column per country.

We measure growth as the average annual growth rate in percent, \(g = 100 \times \ln(y_{end}/y_{start})/(end - start)\), as in Chapter 3.

def growth_rate(y, start, end):
    """Average annual growth rate, percent: 100 ln(y_end / y_start) / years (Chapter 3)."""
    return 100 * np.log(y.loc[end] / y.loc[start]) / (end - start)

What to notice.

  • In 1950 Japan earned only a fifth of U.S. income (0.20). Far below its steady state, it grew more than twice as fast as the United States for four decades.
  • The ratio peaked at 0.85 in 1991. Since then Japan has grown more slowly than the United States, and the ratio has drifted down to 0.65 in 2022, a little below the textbook’s guess of 0.75.
  • Fast growth that fades as the gap closes is exactly what transition dynamics predicts. The same logic suggests that China’s fast growth will slow as it nears its own steady state.

Lesson 3: In the long run, money drives inflation and real forces drive output

Goal. Recall the classical dichotomy and test the quantity theory across 165 economies.

In the long run, the real side of the economy (output, employment, wages) is set by real forces: the inputs and ideas of Lesson 1. The nominal side (the price level and inflation) is set by money. With a stable velocity of money, the quantity theory says that inflation equals money growth minus real GDP growth,

\[\pi = g_M - g_Y.\]

This split between the real and the nominal sides is the classical dichotomy. It means that, in the long run, there is no trade-off between inflation and real GDP. You tested it in Chapter 8, Figure 3 (U.S. decades) and Chapter 8, Figure 4 (countries). Behind fast money growth there is usually a government that cannot borrow and prints money to pay its bills. Long-run unemployment, by contrast, depends on labor-market institutions such as hiring and firing costs and unemployment insurance, as in Chapter 7, Figure 7.

Lesson 4: In the short run, there is a trade-off between inflation and output

Goal. Recall the Phillips curve and estimate its slope from U.S. data.

In the short run, inflation is sticky: firms set prices partly by looking at past inflation, so inflation changes only gradually. What moves it is the state of the economy. The Phillips curve of Chapter 12 says

\[\Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\,\tilde{Y}_t + \bar{o},\]

where \(\tilde{Y}_t\) is short-run output, the percent gap between actual and potential GDP, and \(\bar{o}\) is a price shock. A boom raises inflation and a slump lowers it. Sticky inflation also breaks the classical dichotomy in the short run: when the central bank raises the nominal interest rate, the real interest rate rises too, which lowers investment and output. See Chapter 9, Figure 7, Chapter 12, Figure 4, Chapter 12, Figure 7 and the inflation-output loops of Chapter 13, Figure 17.

Python note. .diff() subtracts the previous row from each row, so it turns inflation into the change in inflation (the first year has no previous year and becomes empty, NaN). np.polyfit(x, y, 1) fits a straight line \(y = a + bx\) by least squares and returns the slope \(b\) first.

us = pd.read_csv(DATA_URL + "fig13-17.csv")            # U.S. output gap and CPI inflation, percent
us["change"] = us.inflation.diff()                      # Δπ_t = π_t − π_(t−1)
us = us.dropna()                                        # drop 1960, which has no previous year
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(us.output_gap, us.change, 1)
print(f"Estimated slope ν = {slope:.2f}, intercept {intercept:.2f}, from {len(us)} years ({us.year.min()}–{us.year.max()})")
Estimated slope ν = 0.35, intercept 0.10, from 65 years (1961–2025)
fig = px.scatter(us, x="output_gap", y="change", hover_name="year", trendline="ols",
                 labels={"output_gap": "Short-run output, Ỹ (percent)", "change": "Change in inflation, Δπ (points)"},
                 title=f"Inflation tends to rise in booms and fall in slumps (slope ν = {slope:.2f})")
fig.update_traces(marker=dict(color=TEAL, size=8))
fig.update_traces(line=dict(color=BLUE, width=3), selector=dict(mode="lines"))
fig.show()

What to notice.

  • The fitted slope is about 0.35: each percent of output above potential goes with inflation about a third of a point higher than the year before. That is close to the “about 1/3” you measured in Chapter 9.
  • The points are widely scattered. Price shocks \(\bar{o}\), such as the oil shocks of the 1970s and the 2021–2022 surge, move inflation far off the line (hover over the outliers).
  • The trade-off is short-run only. Over decades, Lesson 3’s classical dichotomy takes over again.

Lesson 5: A credible central bank stabilizes output as well as inflation

Goal. Measure what the Volcker disinflation cost in lost output, and see why credibility lowers that cost.

The Phillips curve gives a central bank one sure way to bring inflation down: a recession. But if firms and workers believe that the bank will do whatever it takes, they lower their expected inflation on their own, and a smaller recession, or none, does the job. That is why credibility, transparency and explicit inflation targets matter so much. See the disinflation in Chapter 12, Figure 12, the costless disinflation of Chapter 13, Figure 20, and the low, stable inflation of the inflation-targeting era in Chapter 13, Figure 19.

One way to measure the cost of a disinflation is the sacrifice ratio: the output lost, added up over the years of the recession (the sum of the negative values of \(\tilde{Y}\)), divided by the fall in inflation.

Python note. .clip(upper=0) replaces every positive number by 0, so that only the years below potential count as losses. A function can return several values at once, separated by commas.

annual = pd.read_csv(DATA_URL + "fig13-17.csv").set_index("year")    # the same file, with the year as the row label

def sacrifice_ratio(start, end):
    """Output lost (sum of negative Ỹ) per point of lower inflation between two years (Chapter 13)."""
    lost = -annual.loc[start:end, "output_gap"].clip(upper=0).sum()
    fall = annual.loc[start, "inflation"] - annual.loc[end, "inflation"]
    return lost, fall, lost / fall

lost, fall, ratio_v = sacrifice_ratio(1980, 1985)
print(f"1980–1985: inflation fell by {fall:.1f} points; output lost {lost:.1f} percent of a year's potential GDP")
print(f"Sacrifice ratio: {ratio_v:.1f} percent of output per point of inflation")
print(f"Average inflation 1970–1989: {annual.loc[1970:1989, 'inflation'].mean():.1f}%;  1993–2019: {annual.loc[1993:2019, 'inflation'].mean():.1f}%")
1980–1985: inflation fell by 10.0 points; output lost 16.4 percent of a year's potential GDP
Sacrifice ratio: 1.6 percent of output per point of inflation
Average inflation 1970–1989: 6.3%;  1993–2019: 2.3%

What to notice.

  • Between 1980 and 1985, U.S. inflation fell from 13.5 to 3.5 percent. The price was a deep recession: over the six years, output summed to 16.4 percent of a year’s potential GDP below potential, about 1.6 percent for each point of disinflation.
  • A fully credible announcement could, in the model of Chapter 13, have lowered inflation with much smaller losses. The Fed paid for its lost credibility of the 1970s.
  • Once credibility was rebuilt, inflation stayed low: it averaged 6.3 percent in 1970–1989 and 2.3 percent in 1993–2019.

Open questions at the frontier

Goal. Name the big questions macroeconomics has not yet answered, and measure one of them.

The textbook closes with problems that are still open. In short:

  • Why are investment and TFP so low in poor countries? Lesson 1 showed that TFP explains most of the income gap. Weak property rights and the rule of law are part of the answer, but why are they weak?
  • Which institutions work best? For producing and spreading ideas (patents, prizes, research funding), for sharing the gains from trade and capital flows, and for running monetary systems.
  • How do we measure potential output in real time? Mistaking a slowdown in potential for a recession leads to too much stimulus, as in Chapter 12, Figure 13.
  • Why do bubbles form, and what should policymakers do about them? See the housing boom of Chapter 14, Figure 8.
  • How will governments pay for aging populations and rising health spending? See the long-term projections of Chapter 18, Figure 6 and health spending in Chapter 18, Figure 8.

The third question has a recent twist: since about 2003, U.S. productivity has grown much more slowly than around 2000. Is the slowdown temporary or permanent? You met it in Chapter 6, Figure 6 and Chapter 14, Figure 11. We measure it with the BLS growth-accounting indexes of Chapter 6 and our growth_rate function from Lesson 2.

What to notice.

  • Growth of output per hour fell from 3.2 percent a year in 1995–2003 to 1.8 percent since 2003, and TFP growth roughly halved, from 1.5 to 0.8 percent.
  • A difference of 1.4 points a year adds up: over 22 years it is a gap of about a third in output per hour.
  • Nobody knows yet whether this is a long pause between big inventions or a lasting change. It is one of the questions you might help answer.

Summary

  • Long run, levels: income per person depends on capital, skills, hours, ideas and TFP, and TFP explains most of the gap between rich and poor countries. Long run, growth: sustained growth comes from new ideas.
  • Transition dynamics: countries far below their steady state grow fast, as Japan did after 1950, and slow down as they catch up.
  • Classical dichotomy: in the long run, money growth drives inflation and real forces drive output, so there is no long-run trade-off between them.
  • Short run: sticky inflation creates a Phillips curve trade-off and lets monetary policy move real output; a credible central bank can lower inflation at a smaller cost.
  • This is the last chapter. Many big questions remain open, from low TFP in poor countries to the productivity slowdown, bubbles and aging. Go back to any notebook, change a parameter or a data window, and start asking your own questions.
