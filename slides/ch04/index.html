Today: three questions

  • The production model. How do machines and workers turn into output, and who gets paid what?
  • The model meets the data. If countries differed only in capital per person, how rich would each one be?
  • TFP and institutions. How much of the gap is productivity, and what could explain it?

These are the three acts and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide. Act I builds and solves the model: one good, a fixed number of machines and workers, competitive firms. Act II takes its per-person prediction to data on 110 countries. Act III measures productivity as what the model cannot explain, and ends with four policy questions: capital flows, institutions, misallocation and the speed of reform.

This is the first behavioral model of the course. The growth facts of Chapter 3 are what it, and the Solow model of Chapter 5, try to explain.

The production model

Act I

A model is a small economy we can solve

  • One good, measured in tons a year. Its price is 1, so every price is in units of the good.
  • Given inputs: \(\bar{L}\) workers and \(\bar{K}\) machines. A bar marks a parameter, set outside the model.
  • Technology: a production function \(Y = F(K, L)\).
  • Competitive firms take the wage \(w\) and the rental price \(r\) as given and maximize profit.
  • Markets clear: \(w\) and \(r\) adjust until firms want exactly the workers and machines that exist.

Five assumptions, five equations, five unknowns: \(Y\), \(K\), \(L\), \(r\) and \(w\).

The textbook’s good is ice cream; any single good works. One good means no relative prices between goods: the good is the numéraire, so the wage and the rental price are measured in tons of the good.

Why so simple? A model is a laboratory: we decide what the toy economy contains, then switch it on and see what it does. If the toy world reproduces some facts, we have learned which ingredients matter for them. If it fails, the way it fails is informative too, which is exactly what happens in Act II.

Notation: letters with a bar are parameters (given), letters without a bar are endogenous (solved for), and an asterisk marks equilibrium values.

Cobb-Douglas: double both inputs and output doubles

\[Y = F(K, L) = \bar{A}\,K^{1/3}L^{2/3}\]

\[F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \bar{A}(\lambda K)^{1/3}(\lambda L)^{2/3} = \lambda^{1/3+2/3}\,F(K, L) = \lambda\,F(K, L)\]

Constant returns to scale: the exponents add up to one. A firm can always build a copy of itself.

Ā is productivity: with the same machines and workers, a larger Ā gives more output. It is the parameter Act III is about.

The derivation uses two rules for exponents: (ab)^c = a^c b^c and a^c a^d = a^(c+d). With exponents that sum to more than one we would have increasing returns, below one decreasing returns.

The replication argument: a second plant with the same staff and equipment makes the same output again, so doubling everything doubles output. Ask: why might it fail in practice? (Land and natural resources cannot be copied; coordination in a very large firm.) We set those aside here.

Why 1/3 on capital? Act I ends with the answer: it is the share of income that capital earns.

Experiment: add machines, keep the workers

A plant has 64 workers. With one machine it makes 16 tons of ice cream a year. How much does it make with eight machines and the same 64 workers? Write down a number.

Collect guesses before revealing anything. Many students say 128 tons (“eight times the machines”). Some will say “less than 128” without a number.

The numbers: with Ā = 1 and L = 64, one machine gives 1^(1/3) × 64^(2/3) = 16 tons. We come back to the guesses on the interpretation slide.

\[MPK = \frac{\partial F}{\partial K} = \frac{1}{3}\,\bar{A}\left(\frac{L}{K}\right)^{2/3} = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{Y}{K}\]

Diminishing returns: with the workforce fixed, the marginal product of capital falls as machines are added.

Reveal: 8^(1/3) = 2, so eight machines make 2 × 16 = 32 tons, not 128. The gains are the title of Fig. 1.

The formula: the marginal product of capital is the slope of the curve, the extra output from one more machine with L fixed. Apply the power rule to K^(1/3): the derivative is (1/3) K^(−2/3). Rewriting K^(−2/3) L^(2/3) as (L/K)^(2/3) shows the diminishing returns: more K lowers L/K and so the MPK. Rewriting it as Y/K shows a Cobb-Douglas property: each marginal product is the exponent times the average product.

By the same steps, MPL = (2/3) Y/L, which falls as workers are added and rises with more machines.

Doubling the machines does not double output

If a firm doubles its machines, it doubles its output.

Only if it doubles its workers too. Capital alone multiplies output by \(2^{1/3} \approx\) 1.26: each new machine has fewer hands to run it. Constant returns apply to all inputs at once.

The two properties sit side by side in the same function: constant returns to scale (all inputs) and diminishing returns to each input alone. Students often mix them up.

Ask: a company buys twice as many delivery vans but hires no new drivers. What happens to deliveries? (Little, once every driver has a van.)

This is the most important property for Act II: large differences in capital per person will turn into much smaller differences in income.

Firms hire each input until its marginal product equals its price

\[\max_{K,L}\; F(K, L) - rK - wL \quad\Longrightarrow\quad MPK = r, \qquad MPL = w\]

\[r = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{Y}{K} \qquad\qquad w = \frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{Y}{L}\]

Because the MPK falls as \(K\) rises, the hiring rule for capital is a downward-sloping demand curve. The same holds for labor.

The logic without calculus: if one more machine adds more output than its rent (MPK > r), renting it raises profit, so the firm keeps renting and the MPK falls. If MPK < r, returning a machine raises profit. Only at MPK = r is there nothing left to gain. Same for workers and the wage.

The supplies are fixed at K̄ and L̄, so the supply curves are vertical lines. With the two hiring rules, the production function and the two market-clearing conditions, we have five equations for Y, K, L, r and w.

Because both markets clear at once, this is a general equilibrium, not a single supply-and-demand diagram.

Experiment: the economy gets more machines

The economy doubles its stock of machines; the number of workers does not change. What happens to the rental price of capital, \(r\)? And to the wage, \(w\)? Up, down or unchanged?

Ask for a show of hands for each price. Most students get r right (more supply, lower price) and are split on w. Some think the wage falls because “machines replace workers”.

We will start from K̄ = L̄ = Ā = 1 and move to K̄ = 2.

Reveal the predictions. The rental price falls by about 37 percent: (1/2)^(2/3) ≈ 0.63. The wage rises by about 26 percent: 2^(1/3) ≈ 1.26. Both numbers are in the notebook’s Model overview.

The “machines replace workers” intuition fails here because there is only one good and every worker is employed. In this model, capital and labor are complements: more of one raises the marginal product of the other. Whether new kinds of machines (robots, AI) substitute for some workers is a question for a richer model with different kinds of labor.

A policy reading: in this model, investment raises wages. That is one reason capital per person matters for living standards.

The solution: every input is employed, and each is paid its marginal product

\[K^* = \bar{K}, \quad L^* = \bar{L}, \quad Y^* = \bar{A}\bar{K}^{1/3}\bar{L}^{2/3}\]

\[r^* = \frac{1}{3}\,\bar{A}\left(\frac{\bar{L}}{\bar{K}}\right)^{2/3} \qquad w^* = \frac{2}{3}\,\bar{A}\left(\frac{\bar{K}}{\bar{L}}\right)^{1/3}\]

Each unknown on the left, only parameters on the right: the model is solved.

The steps: market clearing gives K* and L* directly. Substitute them into the production function for Y. Substitute Y into the hiring rules: divide by K̄ for r* (K̄^(1/3)/K̄ = K̄^(−2/3)), divide by L̄ for w* (L̄^(2/3)/L̄ = L̄^(−1/3)).

Read the prices: r* depends on L̄/K̄, the number of workers per machine; w* on K̄/L̄, machines per worker. Neither depends on the size of the economy, only on the ratio. We return to this in Act II.

With the values of Fig. 2, K̄ = L̄ = Ā = 1, the solution is Y* = 1, r* = 1/3 and w* = 2/3.

Labor gets two‑thirds, capital one‑third, and nothing is left over

\[\frac{w^*L^*}{Y^*} = \frac{2}{3} \qquad \frac{r^*K^*}{Y^*} = \frac{1}{3} \qquad w^*L^* + r^*K^* = Y^*\]

With \(\bar{K} = \bar{L} = \bar{A} = 1\)
Output \(Y^*\) 1.000
Capital income \(r^*K^*\) 0.333 (33%)
Labor income \(w^*L^*\) 0.667 (67%)
Economic profit 0.000

The table is the notebook’s key numbers for Fig. 2. The shares come straight from the hiring rules: w* L/Y = (2/3)(Y/L)(L/Y) = 2/3, whatever Ā, K̄ and L̄ are. Only the exponent can change the split.

This is why the exponent on capital is 1/3: in U.S. data, capital receives roughly a third of GDP and labor roughly two-thirds, and the split has been fairly stable (Chapter 2 showed the labor share). The model is built to match that fact.

The last equation says income equals production, one of the national-accounts identities of Chapter 2. Profit is zero, which is what perfect competition leads us to expect.

Zero profit does not mean that firms earn nothing

If economic profit is zero, firm owners and shareholders get nothing.

Economic profit is what is left after paying all inputs, including capital. Accounting profit is revenue minus labor costs: the payment to capital, \(r^*K^*\), which is one-third of output. Shareholders own claims to that flow.

The two words “profit” mean different things. In the model, firms rent machines from their owners; in reality, corporations own their machines, and the owners of the corporations (shareholders) receive the capital income as accounting profit, dividends and retained earnings.

So the stock market puts a price on capital income: a firm’s market value is the value of its current and future accounting profits. That is the next figure.

Ask: if accounting profit is a third of output, why would economic profit be zero? (Because investors could have earned the same return elsewhere: r is the opportunity cost of capital.)

Share prices value future capital income, so they swing with expectations

  • Over 1920–2026 the real index rose by a factor of 49.8: about 3.7% a year.
  • The falls are large and can last a decade or more: 1929–32, the late 1960s to 1982, 2000–09.
  • A share is a claim to a firm’s future accounting profits, its part of \(r^*K^*\). Its price moves with expected profits and with the rate used to discount them.

The stock market puts a value on capital income, the third of output that the model pays to capital.

Why is the index so volatile when GDP is not? Because the price reflects expectations about the whole future stream of profits. A small change in expected growth, or in the interest rate used to discount the future, changes the value of the whole stream. The production model is static and says nothing about expectations; Chapter 17 (investment) returns to asset prices.

The model meets the data

Act II

Per person, income depends on productivity and capital per person

\[y^* \equiv \frac{Y^*}{L^*} = \bar{A}\,\bar{k}^{1/3}, \qquad \bar{k} \equiv \frac{\bar{K}}{\bar{L}}\]

  • Productivity \(\bar{A}\) raises income one for one.
  • Capital per person raises it with strong diminishing returns: doubling \(\bar{k}\) multiplies \(y^*\) by \(2^{1/3} \approx 1.26\).
  • The wage moves with income per person: \(w^* = \frac{2}{3}\,y^*\).

Divide Y* by L̄ and use L̄^(2/3)/L̄ = L̄^(−1/3). Everyone works in this model, so “per person” and “per worker” are the same thing.

The second bullet is the key prediction to take to the data: large differences in capital per person should show up as much smaller differences in income.

The wage formula will matter in Act III: if schooling raises wages by some percentage, it raises income per person by the same percentage, which lets us size the role of human capital.

Productivity is the only parameter that raises every outcome

A 1% rise in … \(Y^*\) \(y^*\) \(w^*\) \(r^*\)
productivity \(\bar{A}\) +1% +1% +1% +1%
capital \(\bar{K}\) +⅓% +⅓% +⅓% −⅔%
labor \(\bar{L}\) +⅔% −⅓% −⅓% +⅔%
both \(\bar{K}\) and \(\bar{L}\) +1% 0 0 0

Each entry is an elasticity: the exponent of the parameter in the log of the solution.

Take logs of the solution: ln Y* = ln Ā + (1/3) ln K̄ + (2/3) ln L̄, and so on. The coefficient on each parameter is the percent change in the outcome when the parameter rises 1 percent.

Read the rows:

  • Productivity shifts both demand curves up, so every outcome rises 1 for 1.
  • More capital lowers its own price (down the demand curve) and raises the wage (labor demand shifts up). More labor does the opposite.
  • For y* the elasticity with respect to L̄ is 2/3 − 1 = −1/3: the same output shared by more people.
  • The last row is next slide’s misconception.

The factor shares are 1/3 and 2/3 in every row.

A bigger economy is not a richer one, per person

Countries with more workers and more machines are richer per person.

Scale both \(\bar{K}\) and \(\bar{L}\) up by the same percentage: total output rises in proportion, but \(y^*\), \(w^*\) and \(r^*\) do not change. Only the ratio \(\bar{K}/\bar{L}\), and productivity, matter for income per person.

Constant returns to scale is what makes size irrelevant per person. Small countries are among the richest: the five richest countries of the opening slide include Luxembourg, Singapore and Ireland.

Size can matter in richer models: through the number of ideas a large population produces (Chapter 6), or the size of the market for new products. In this model, it does not.

Ask: India and the United States have very different populations. Which number would you compare to say who is richer? (GDP per person, not total GDP.)

TFP and institutions

Act III

TFP is the productivity that makes the model fit exactly

\[y^* = \bar{A}\,\bar{k}^{1/3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar{A} = \frac{y^*}{\bar{k}^{1/3}}\]

A country with 1/8 of U.S. capital per person is predicted to have 1/2 of U.S. income. If it actually has 1/8, its implied TFP is \(\frac{1/8}{1/2} = \frac{1}{4}\).

Total factor productivity (TFP) is a residual: everything that raises income other than physical capital.

We observe y and k̄ for every country, but there is no direct way to count Ā: no census of efficiency. So we assume the model is right and solve it for the one term we cannot observe.

Because it is chosen to make the model fit, TFP measures how much the model leaves unexplained. That is both its strength (it is a precise number) and its weakness (it is a name for what we do not explain), the subject of the Devil’s advocate at the end.

The example is the notebook’s: with income 1/8 and capital 1/8, capital explains a factor of 2 and TFP a factor of 4.

Is low TFP the exception or the rule?

Is low productivity a feature of a few poor countries, or a pattern across the whole world? And has it changed since 1980?

Most of the gap between rich and poor is TFP

\[\underbrace{\frac{y_{rich}}{y_{poor}}}_{82} = \underbrace{\frac{\bar{A}_{rich}}{\bar{A}_{poor}}}_{15.9\ (\text{TFP})} \times \underbrace{\left(\frac{\bar{k}_{rich}}{\bar{k}_{poor}}\right)^{1/3}}_{5.2\ (\text{capital})}\]

  • Five richest against five poorest in 2023: TFP explains about two-thirds of the gap in logs, capital about one-third (\(\ln 5.2/\ln 82 \approx 0.37\)).
  • Burundi against the U.S.: actual income 1/74; with capital differences only, 1/6.0; with TFP differences only, 1/12.2. Capital explains 42% of the log gap.

The numbers are the notebook’s key numbers “Capital or TFP?”. The textbook, for 2019, finds 75 = 5 × 15; our 2023 split is very close.

Why logs? The decomposition is a product, so the parts add up only in logs: ln 82 = ln 15.9 + ln 5.2 (up to rounding). “TFP explains two-thirds” means ln 15.9 / ln 82 ≈ 0.63.

Policy reading: even if a poor country matched rich-country capital per person, it would close only a third or so of the log gap. Raising productivity is where most of the gain lies, which is why the rest of the lecture asks what TFP is.

TFP is more than technology

TFP measures technology: poor countries are poor because they use older machines and methods.

TFP is a residual. It collects everything except physical capital: skills and schooling, technology, management, institutions, how inputs are spread across firms, natural resources, and errors in measuring capital and output.

Technology is one candidate, and Chapter 6 develops it. But the residual does not label itself.

Human capital can be sized with w* = (2/3) y*: if extra schooling raises wages by some percentage, it raises income per person by the same percentage. The textbook’s rough calculation: if U.S. adults have about 9 more years of school than adults in the poorest countries, at about 10 percent more wages a year of school, education alone would make U.S. income about 1.9 times as high. That is a large effect, but far from the factor of 15.9 that TFP accounts for in the rich-poor comparison.

Measurement: our capital stock is built from past investment, with a guess for the starting value. Errors in that guess, or in the price data, end up in TFP. Venezuela in Act II is an extreme example.

Why doesn’t capital flow from rich to poor countries?

With equal productivity, a country with 1/8 of U.S. capital per person would have a marginal product of capital \(8^{2/3} = 4\) times as high. Why don’t investors move their capital there?

What the evidence suggests

  • With TFP of 1/4, the two effects cancel: capital earns the same return.
  • Measured carefully, returns to capital differ little across countries.
  • Weak institutions raise the risk of losing an investment.

Why it is contested

  • Capital often flows out of fast-growing poor countries.
  • Default risk and thin financial markets also matter.
  • Measured returns depend on how capital is priced.

The question is the title of Lucas (1990, American Economic Review): with the production model and equal productivity, the return to capital in India should be many times that in the United States, and capital should rush there. It does not.

Left column: per person, MPK = (1/3) Ā k̄^(−2/3). With 1/8 of the capital, k̄^(−2/3) is 4 times higher; with TFP of 1/4, the two cancel exactly (the notebook’s Model overview). Caselli and Feyrer (2007, Quarterly Journal of Economics) measure marginal products directly and find them similar across rich and poor countries, once differences in natural resources and in the price of capital goods are accounted for. Alfaro, Kalemli-Ozcan and Volosovych (2008, Review of Economics and Statistics) find that institutional quality is the main explanation for the paradox.

Right column: Gourinchas and Jeanne (2013, Review of Economic Studies) document an “allocation puzzle”: among developing countries, those with faster productivity growth attract less foreign capital, not more. China, a fast grower, exports capital to the United States (Chapter 2’s trade balances).

Ask the policy students: what would make you invest your own savings in a factory abroad?

Institutions: what natural experiments suggest

When one country is split in two under different rules, what happens to the two incomes over a few decades?

What the evidence suggests

  • Korea and Germany were split in two: same history and culture, different rules, very different incomes.
  • At night, South Korea is full of light; the North is dark except its capital.
  • Secure property and contracts make investment pay.

Why it is contested

  • Which institutions matter, and how much, is hard to pin down.
  • Schooling and income may improve institutions, not only the reverse.
  • Rules that work in one country often fail elsewhere.

Olson (1996, Journal of Economic Perspectives, “Big Bills Left on the Sidewalk”) uses the divided countries as natural experiments: the pairs shared geography, culture and history until the split, so the large income gaps that opened afterwards point to institutions and policies. The textbook’s Fig. 4.8 is a night-time satellite photo of the Korean peninsula; there are no reliable income data for North Korea, so we cannot rebuild it from data. NASA’s “The Koreas at Night” shows a similar image (link in the book chapter).

Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson (2001, American Economic Review) use the mortality of European settlers to isolate the effect of institutions on income, and find it large. Hall and Jones (1999, Quarterly Journal of Economics) link output per worker, and TFP, to “social infrastructure”.

Contested: Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes and Shleifer (2004, Journal of Economic Growth) argue that human capital is the more basic cause, and that poor countries often grow first and improve institutions later.

Ask: name one institution you would change first in a poor country, and how you would know it worked.

Misallocation: the same inputs, used in the wrong firms

Can a country raise its TFP without new machines, more workers or new technology, just by moving inputs between firms?

What the evidence suggests

  • Well-allocated inputs have equal marginal products in every firm.
  • In China and India they differ a lot: some firms get cheap credit or favors.
  • Moving inputs to the most productive firms would raise TFP substantially.

Why it is contested

  • Part of the measured gaps is error in firm data.
  • Some gaps reflect adjustment costs and risk, not bad policy.
  • Removing distortions creates losers who resist it.

The idea follows from the hiring rule MPK = r: in an efficient economy, every firm faces the same prices, so marginal products are equalized. Taxes, subsidies, cheap credit for state firms, or favors for a politically connected firm break that rule. A connected but unproductive firm keeps workers and machines that a more productive rival would use better, and aggregate TFP falls.

Evidence: Hsieh and Klenow (2009, Quarterly Journal of Economics) measure marginal products across manufacturing plants in China and India and estimate large TFP gains from equalizing them. Restuccia and Rogerson (2008, Review of Economic Dynamics) show in a model that policies favoring some firms over others can lower TFP considerably.

Contested: Bils, Klenow and Ruane (2021, Journal of Monetary Economics) argue that measurement error inflates the measured dispersion. Asker, Collard-Wexler and De Loecker (2014, Journal of Political Economy) show that adjustment costs alone can create large measured gaps.

For the policy students: misallocation is attractive because it does not need more saving; it needs reforms of credit, competition and regulation.

Big bang or gradualism?

Should a country leaving central planning reform everything at once, or step by step?

The case for gradual reform

  • China kept the planned sector while letting a market sector grow beside it, so few people lost at first.
  • Reforms in sequence let new institutions form before old ones disappear.

Why it is contested

  • China started as a poor, rural economy; Russia was industrial. Starting points differ.
  • Part of Russia’s measured fall was output nobody wanted.
  • Some fast reformers, such as Poland, did much better than Russia.

The case for gradualism: Lau, Qian and Roland (2000, Journal of Political Economy) explain China’s “dual-track” reform: firms delivered planned quotas at plan prices and sold anything extra at market prices, so reform created winners without immediate losers. Roland (2000, “Transition and Economics”, MIT Press) argues that reform sequencing matters when institutions take time to build.

Contested: Sachs and Woo (1994, Economic Policy) argue that China’s success came from its starting point (a mostly rural labor force moving into industry), not from gradualism. Shleifer and Treisman (2005, Journal of Economic Perspectives) argue that Russia’s fall is overstated, because much Soviet output had little value, and that Russia became a fairly normal middle-income country. Stiglitz (2002, “Globalization and Its Discontents”) criticizes the speed of Russia’s privatization.

Ask the policy students: with Fig. 8 open, add Poland and Ukraine. What would you conclude about the speed of reform, and what else differs between the countries?

“TFP is just a measure of our ignorance”

A skeptic says: “You explained a third of the gap with capital and called the rest TFP. That is a name for what you cannot explain. The model has not explained why poor countries are poor.”

Reply: the size of the residual is the finding. It rules out the idea that poor countries only need more machines, and it tells us where to look: skills, technology, institutions and misallocation. Each of these can then be measured and tested, as the last slides showed.

Take the objection seriously. Abramovitz (1956, American Economic Review) called the growth residual “a measure of our ignorance”, and the label stuck.

The reply has three parts. First, a negative result is valuable: before this kind of accounting, many development policies were built on raising investment. The model says that equal capital would close only about a third of the log gap. Second, the residual can be broken down: human capital through wages (w* = (2/3) y*), misallocation through firm data, institutions through natural experiments. Third, the residual is a target for theory: Chapter 6 models technology as ideas.

But the skeptic has a point: TFP also absorbs measurement errors, and the model does not say why TFP differs. Ask: what would convince you that a particular cause, say institutions, explains a large part of TFP?

What to remember from this chapter

  • \(Y = \bar{A}K^{1/3}L^{2/3}\): constant returns to scale, but diminishing returns to each input alone.
  • Firms hire until \(MPK = r\) and \(MPL = w\). Capital earns one-third of output and labor two-thirds; profit is zero.
  • Per person, \(y^* = \bar{A}\bar{k}^{1/3}\). With equal productivity, the model predicts income gaps far smaller than in the data.
  • TFP, \(\bar{A} = y^*/\bar{k}^{1/3}\), is the residual. It explains about two-thirds of the log income gap between rich and poor.
  • Institutions, misallocation, skills and technology are candidates for TFP, and how to change them is contested.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: are poor countries poor because of fewer machines or because of lower productivity? Then ask what the model cannot tell us: where capital per person comes from. That is the next chapter.
