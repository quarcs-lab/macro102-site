The long-run model explains where the economy is heading on average: capital, ideas, the natural rate of unemployment, money growth and long-run inflation. Keynes’s famous complaint was that a theory of the calm sea after the storm is no help during the storm. This chapter starts the short run: the storms.

Ask the room: was April 2020 a recession like the others, or something different? Keep the question open; Chapter 10 returns to it.

Today: three questions

  • How do we measure the cycle? Potential output, short-run output, and four U.S. facts: GDP, the gap, jobs and inflation.
  • What does the cycle do to inflation? The Phillips curve, its fit in U.S. data, a boom that lasts, and the cost of bringing inflation down.
  • What does it do to jobs, and what can policy do? Okun’s law, the chain from interest rates to output, and steering by a gap nobody observes.

These are the three acts of the deck and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide.

The headline result: output swings around potential. A boom raises inflation year after year and pushes unemployment down, so bringing inflation down takes a slump, and the slope of the Phillips curve fixes how big that slump must be in total.

This chapter is a framework, not yet a full model. It lets policymakers choose short-run output directly. Chapters 11 to 13 explain how monetary and fiscal policy actually move output.

Measuring the cycle

Act I

The long-run model sets the trend; the short-run model explains the swings

Model What it determines Where it comes from
long-run model potential output \(\bar{Y}_t\) and long-run inflation \(\bar{\pi}\) Chapters 3 to 8
short-run model actual output \(Y_t\) and inflation \(\pi_t\), year by year Chapters 9 to 15
  • Potential output is what the economy produces when capital, labor and ideas are used at rates it can sustain. It is not a ceiling: for a while output can run above it.
  • Shocks push output away from potential: oil prices, financial panics, tax changes, new technologies, disasters, booms abroad.
  • The short run is how long such deviations last, typically about two years.

In the short-run model, \(\bar{Y}_t\) and \(\bar{\pi}\) are given from outside: that is what the bars mean.

The bars are the book’s notation for exogenous variables. The short-run model takes the long-run model’s answers as given and explains the deviations from them. The endogenous variables of the short run are actual output and actual inflation.

Long-run inflation has no time subscript: in the end it is a stable target chosen by policymakers (Chapter 13).

Firms can produce more than potential for a while: overtime shifts, deferred maintenance, extra temporary workers. They cannot do it for ever, and that is exactly why a boom raises inflation (Act II).

Ask: name a shock from the last five years. (The pandemic; the energy price surge of 2021–22; supply-chain disruptions; interest-rate hikes.)

Short-run output is the percent gap between actual and potential output

\[\widetilde{Y}\!_t \equiv \frac{Y_t - \bar{Y}_t}{\bar{Y}_t} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Y_t = \bar{Y}_t\,(1 + \widetilde{Y}\!_t)\]

\[\widetilde{Y}\!_t \approx \ln Y_t - \ln\bar{Y}_t\]

  • Boom: \(\widetilde{Y} > 0\). Slump: \(\widetilde{Y} < 0\). At potential: \(\widetilde{Y} = 0\).
  • Year 2 of our baseline: potential 104.04, actual 106.12, so \(\widetilde{Y} = +2\%\).
  • A recession starts when \(\widetilde{Y}\) turns negative and is dated over at its low point, while output is still below potential.

Measured in percent, a gap means the same thing in a small economy and a large one.

Left top: the definition, and the same equation solved for actual output. Actual output is the trend times a cycle factor slightly above or below one.

Left bottom: take logs of Y = Ȳ(1 + Ỹ) and use ln(1 + x) ≈ x for small x. The approximation is very good for usual gaps: the notebook’s Check 2 gives an error of 0.02 points at ±2 percent, 0.13 at −5 percent and 0.54 at −10 percent. Agencies often report gaps in logs.

The baseline of every model figure: potential output starts at 100 and grows 2 percent a year; the gap follows a wave between +2 and −2 percent that repeats every 8 years. In year 6, the bottom of the slump: potential 112.62, actual 110.36, gap −2 percent.

Other names: the output gap, detrended output. In the United States, a committee of the National Bureau of Economic Research dates recessions from many indicators, not only GDP.

Actual growth is trend growth plus the change in the gap

\[g_{Y,t} \approx \bar{g} + \Delta\widetilde{Y}\!_t\]

  • Year 1 → 2: the gap rises from +1.41 to +2, so output grows by about \(2 + 0.59 = 2.59\%\).
  • Year 4 → 5: the gap falls from 0 to −1.41, so output grows only \(0.59\%\): still growing, yet sliding into a slump.

Growth of 5% fits fast potential growth with a steady gap, or slow potential growth with a widening gap. Growth alone cannot tell them apart.

Write the log equation for year t and year t − 1 and subtract. The change in a log is approximately a growth rate, so actual growth is potential growth plus the change in the gap. The notebook’s Check 3 compares the exact growth rates with the approximation: they agree within 0.05 points.

Quarterly data: GDP growth is reported at annualized rates. A quarter with 1 percent growth is reported as 1.01⁴ − 1 = 4.06 percent.

The takeaway is the policymaker’s problem. We observe actual growth; we do not observe potential growth or the change in the gap. Potential output must be estimated: with a smooth trend, an average of surrounding quarters, or, like the Congressional Budget Office, a production function with labor and capital at sustainable levels. We return to this in Act III.

Bridge to the misconception on the next slide.

A recession does not require output to fall

A recession is when GDP shrinks.

In our baseline, output never shrinks: its slowest growth is 0.56% a year. Yet the economy spends 4 years of every 8 below potential. A slump is output below potential, whether or not it is falling.

The popular rule “two quarters of falling GDP” is a news shorthand, not the definition economists use. The NBER dating committee looks at employment, income, sales and industrial production, and the book’s definition is about the gap.

Why it matters for policy: in an economy whose potential grows 2 percent a year, growth of 1 percent means unemployment is rising (Okun’s law, Act III). An economy can be in a slump while its GDP sets a record every quarter.

Ask: if potential output grew 0.5 percent a year, as in Japan since 1995, what growth rate would be “normal”? (Half a percent. The same 1 percent that is weak in the United States would be a boom there.)

Ending the business cycle would not save the cost of a recession

If policy could abolish booms and busts, we would gain the output lost in every recession.

In our baseline, the slump years cost −4.83% of a year’s potential output, but the boom years add +4.83%: over a whole cycle the gaps sum to zero. Abolishing the cycle would also abolish the booms.

The numbers are the notebook’s Check 4: the gaps of years 5 to 7 add up to −4.83 percent; the whole cycle (years 1 to 8) sums to 0.00.

The real gain from stability is smaller than the cost of a recession, but not zero. In the data, booms and slumps are not symmetric: long slumps like 1980–87 and 2008–18 cost far more than the booms that preceded them. And the losses fall on the people who lose their jobs, while the gains of a boom are spread thinly.

For the policy students: the case for stabilization policy rests on that asymmetry and on who bears the losses, not on recovering the whole output lost in recessions.

The Phillips curve

Act II

The short-run model rests on three premises

  1. Shocks never stop. Something is always pushing output away from potential, or inflation away from \(\bar{\pi}\).
  2. Policy can move output in the short run. Monetary and fiscal policy raise or lower spending for a while. In this chapter, policymakers simply choose \(\widetilde{Y}\!_t\).
  3. Output and inflation trade off over time. A hot economy speeds up inflation; a cold one slows it down.

If policy can raise output, why not keep it high? The third premise is the answer.

These are the three premises of the short-run model in our words.

Premise 2 is a shortcut for this chapter only. Chapter 11 shows how the real interest rate moves spending (the IS curve), and Chapter 12 how the central bank sets the real interest rate (the MP curve).

Premise 3 is the Phillips curve, the subject of this act. The question in the takeaway is the one the textbook poses: a government that can raise output would want to keep it as high as possible, unless doing so has a cost. The cost is rising inflation.

The Phillips curve: a hot economy speeds up inflation

\[\Delta\pi_t \equiv \pi_t - \pi_{t-1} = \bar{\nu}\,\widetilde{Y}\!_t, \qquad \bar{\nu} > 0\]

  • \(\bar{\nu}\) (nu) is the slope: the points by which inflation rises in a year for each percent of output above potential. For the United States, about 1/3.
  • Why: when demand runs hot, firms pay overtime, run machines without a break, and goods are scarce. Each firm raises prices faster than the inflation everyone expected, so inflation itself goes up.
  • In a slump, firms with idle capacity hold back their price increases to win customers.

The curve is about the change in inflation, not its level.

The textbook’s image is the hot holiday toy: its maker runs overtime and the toy is scarce, so its price rises faster than usual. In a boom, it is as if every firm made the hot toy and every month were December.

The level of inflation that persists when output is at potential can be anything: 2 percent, 5 percent, 10 percent. That level comes from the long run and from policy.

A full version in Chapter 12 adds a term for price shocks, such as oil. Ask: what would a jump in oil prices do to this line? (Shift it up: inflation rises even at potential.)

Experiment: policymakers push output above potential

Inflation is steady at 2%, and the slope is \(\bar{\nu} = 0.33\). Policymakers push output 2% above potential this year. What happens to inflation? And if instead they push it 3% below potential?

Ask for numbers, not only directions. Students should get +0.66 points and −0.99 points from the equation.

A frequent wrong answer: “inflation rises to 2.66 percent and stays there”. Ask what happens next year if output is still 2 percent above potential. (It rises again.)

Comparative statics are the habit of the book: which line moves, and in which direction. Here: the slope parameter rotates the line, a price shock shifts it, and long-run inflation leaves it in place because the curve is about changes.

Solved forward, inflation is a running total of past gaps

\[\pi_t = \pi_0 + \bar{\nu}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\widetilde{Y}\!_s\]

  • A boom that is over still leaves inflation higher, until a slump of the same total size brings it back.
  • Inflation peaks where the gap crosses zero from above: at the start of a recession.

Add π_{t−1} to both sides of the Phillips curve: π_t = π_{t−1} + ν̄Ỹ_t, a difference equation. Apply it year after year from π_0 and the gaps pile up in a sum. The notebook’s Check 4 computes inflation both year by year and with the running-total formula: they agree to three decimals.

Inflation is steady when, and only when, Ỹ = 0. That is why the Phillips curve does not pin down the level of long-run inflation.

In the baseline cycle, inflation rises in years 1 to 3, stays at 3.59 percent in year 4 where the gap is zero, and falls back to 2 percent by year 7, because the gaps of a full cycle add up to zero.

Lowering inflation takes a slump whose total size the slope fixes

\[\sum_{s=1}^{T}\widetilde{Y}\!_s = \frac{\pi_T - \pi_0}{\bar{\nu}}\]

  • With \(\bar{\nu} = 0.33\), cutting inflation by 1 point takes −3.03 percent-years of output.
  • \(1/\bar{\nu}\) is the sacrifice ratio.

By Okun’s law, the same disinflation costs about 1.52 point-years of extra unemployment.

Policy chooses how to spread the pain over time, not how much pain there is.

Set t = T in the running-total equation, subtract π_0 and divide by ν̄. The left side is the cumulative gap, in percent-years.

The notebook’s Check 5 runs both paths: one sharp year or three mild years both end at exactly 1.00 percent. A cold turkey disinflation and a gradual one cost the same total output in this model.

The Okun number previews Act III: half a point of unemployment per percent of output, so ½ × 3.03 ≈ 1.52 point-years.

A steeper curve makes disinflation cheaper; a flat curve makes it very expensive. That is why the slope matters so much for central banks, and why its measurement on the next slides matters.

Should a central bank cause a recession to end high inflation?

Inflation was 11.25% in 1979. Volcker’s Fed raised interest rates sharply; output fell 6.22% below potential in 1982, and inflation was 3.16% by 1983. Was the slump worth it?

What the model says

  • Lowering inflation requires a slump.
  • Right direction in 1981–83; inflation fell even faster than predicted.
  • The early-1980s slump cost 17.2% of a year’s GDP.

What is debated

  • Credibility: if people believe the bank, the slump can be smaller.
  • Speed: cold turkey or gradual? Here the total is the same.
  • Who pays: the jobless, not the savers.

Ball (1994, in Mankiw, ed., Monetary Policy, University of Chicago Press) measures sacrifice ratios across disinflations in OECD countries and finds that fast disinflations had smaller ratios. These are contested findings; the simple Phillips curve of this chapter has no role for credibility, which Chapter 12 adds through expected inflation.

Ask the policy students: if you could promise a smaller slump by being believed, how would you make a central bank believable?

Overstimulating the economy

The economy starts at potential with inflation at 2%. Policymakers then hold output 3% above potential every year, with a slope of \(\bar{\nu} = 1\). Where is inflation after three years? Does it settle at a new, higher rate?

This is the textbook’s exercise “Overstimulating the economy”. Ask for the three-year number and for a sketch of the path. Many students expect inflation to jump to a higher level and stay there, because they read the Phillips curve as a curve in the level of inflation.

Running the economy hot cannot last

If policymakers keep the economy a little above potential, they get more output and jobs for good, at the price of slightly higher inflation.

A gap held at \(+3\%\) with \(\bar{\nu} = 1\) takes inflation from 2% to 11% in three years and 17% in five. The price is not a higher inflation rate but an inflation rate that rises every year.

The myth is the old reading of the Phillips curve as a menu of inflation levels and unemployment rates: choose a little more inflation and get a little less unemployment, for ever. The curve of this chapter is in the change of inflation, so there is no such menu.

Running hot for a while can make sense: after a deep slump, or when policymakers are unsure where potential output is. Running hot for ever cannot.

Bridge to the policy slide on the 1970s.

Can policy buy lasting low unemployment with a little inflation?

One reading of the 1970s: policymakers held output above potential year after year, thinking inflation cheap or the trade-off permanent. What should a government that wants more jobs learn from that?

The lesson of the model

  • A lasting boom raises inflation every year.
  • It then takes a slump to undo, as in 1981–83.
  • The boom is borrowed, not bought.

Why it was hard to see

  • Potential was overestimated in real time, so stimulus looked right.
  • Oil shocks raised inflation too.
  • Once people expect inflation, the trade-off is gone.

The model lesson is Fig. 9.

Right column, the research. Friedman (1968, American Economic Review) and Phelps (1967, Economica) argued, before the 1970s, that no permanent trade-off between inflation and unemployment exists once expectations adjust: the natural-rate hypothesis. Orphanides (2003, Journal of Monetary Economics) uses the Fed’s real-time data and argues that 1970s policy looked reasonable given the output gaps believed at the time, which later turned out to be far too negative. Romer and Romer (2002, Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, Jackson Hole symposium) argue instead that policymakers’ beliefs about the economy, including an optimistic view of the natural rate of unemployment, drove the policy. The weight of each explanation is debated.

The slide adds no numbers beyond the book’s: the oil shocks show up in Figs. 5 and 7.

Ask: today’s version of the question is “running the economy hot” after a deep slump to pull people back into work. When is that a good idea? (When output is clearly below potential; the risk is that potential is lower than thought.)

Okun’s law and policy

Act III

Okun’s law: each point of output gap is half a point of unemployment

\[u_t - \bar{u} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\widetilde{Y}\!_t\]

Short-run output \(\widetilde{Y}\) +2% 0 −2% −4%
Cyclical unemployment \(u - \bar{u}\) (points) −1 0 +1 +2
  • \(\bar{u}\) is the natural rate of unemployment (Chapter 7), the labor-market cousin of potential output.
  • The slope is less than 1 in size: in a slump firms also cut hours and keep some workers, and some job losers stop looking for work.

Okun’s law lets us work with output alone and translate to unemployment whenever we need to.

The table is the notebook’s “What the law predicts”. Okun’s law is a rule of thumb fitted to U.S. data, named after Arthur Okun.

Going backwards: multiply by −2. Output 2 percent below potential means unemployment 1 point above the natural rate; unemployment 1 point below the natural rate signals output 2 percent above potential.

Why less than one-for-one: labor hoarding (firms keep workers they will need later), shorter hours, and discouraged workers who leave the labor force and so are not counted as unemployed. Japan’s very small slope, on the next slides, is the extreme case.

Unemployment rises whenever output grows more slowly than potential

\[\Delta u_t \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}\left(g_{Y,t} - \bar{g}\right)\]

  • Baseline, year 4 to 5: output grows 0.56%, so unemployment rises by about +0.7 points.
  • Steady growth at the trend rate only holds unemployment where it is.

\[\Delta\pi_t = -2\bar{\nu}\,(u_t - \bar{u})\]

  • The Phillips curve in terms of unemployment.
  • With \(\bar{\nu} = 0.33\): each point of unemployment above the natural rate lowers inflation by 0.66 points a year.

Growth below potential growth, not negative growth, is what raises unemployment.

Left: write Okun’s law for two years and subtract; the natural rate cancels. Then replace ΔỸ by g_Y − ḡ from the growth slide of Act I. The notebook’s Check 6 gets +0.71 points from the levels and +0.72 from the growth form; the difference is the approximation.

Right: substitute Ỹ = −2(u − ū) into the Phillips curve. This is the form in which the trade-off was first found in the data, with unemployment on the horizontal axis: the history is in Chapter 12.

Ask: potential output grows 2 percent a year and GDP grows 1 percent. What happens to unemployment? (It rises by about half a point.)

The full short-run model: from the interest rate to output, inflation and jobs

\[\underbrace{R_t}_{\text{real interest rate}} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{IS curve}\ }\; \widetilde{Y}\!_t \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{Phillips curve}\ }\; \Delta\pi_t, \qquad \widetilde{Y}\!_t \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{Okun's law}\ }\; u_t - \bar{u}\]

  • IS curve (Chapter 11): a higher real interest rate lowers investment and other spending, and so output.
  • MP curve (Chapter 12): the central bank sets a short-term nominal rate, and with it the real rate.
  • Signs: a higher \(R_t\) → lower \(\widetilde{Y}\!_t\) → falling inflation and higher unemployment.

This chapter skipped the first link by letting policymakers pick \(\widetilde{Y}\!_t\) directly.

The road map of the next chapters. Chapter 10 applies the framework of this chapter to the Great Recession and the Covid-19 pandemic. Chapters 11 to 13 build the full model: the IS curve, the MP curve and the Phillips curve together. Chapter 14 returns to the financial crisis, and Chapter 15 to modern DSGE models.

What the full model predicts, in four lines: policy can lean against shocks and keep the gap near zero, which keeps inflation steady; it cannot keep output above potential without inflation climbing; high inflation comes down only through a slump; and the job is harder than in the model because potential output is not observed.

The output gap is an estimate, not a measurement

The output gap is a statistic, like GDP or unemployment.

Potential output is never observed. The 2020 trough moved from −10.42% to −8.83% between two vintages of the data. The CBO and the OECD agree on every big swing, but their U.S. gaps correlate only 0.80, and in 2025 they disagree on the sign: −0.06% for the OECD, +1.40% for the CBO.

Numbers: the notebook’s Fig. 3 key numbers (vintages) and “two agencies, two estimates” (correlation 0.80 for 1985–2025, largest difference 2.0 points in 1986).

GDP is measured, with errors and revisions. Potential output is the output of a model: a smooth trend, an average of nearby quarters, or a production function. Different models give different gaps, and the same model gives a new gap every time the data are revised or a new year is added at the end of the sample.

For other countries every gap in the book is the OECD’s estimate, from its own model.

Should policy steer by an output gap it cannot see?

Central banks set interest rates partly on the size of the output gap. The gap is estimated, revised for years, and differs across agencies. How much weight should policy put on it?

Lean on the gap

  • Inflation responds to the gap: without an estimate, policy flies blind.
  • The direction of big swings is clear in every vintage.
  • Unemployment, which is observed, cross-checks it through Okun’s law.

Lean less on it

  • Real-time gaps have been revised by as much as the gaps themselves.
  • An overestimate of potential calls for too much stimulus, as in the 1970s.
  • Respond more to observed inflation and unemployment.

The research. Orphanides and van Norden (2002, Review of Economics and Statistics) show that real-time estimates of the U.S. output gap are unreliable, and that most of the problem is not data revisions but the difficulty of knowing the trend at the end of the sample. Orphanides (2003, Journal of Monetary Economics) connects that to the 1970s. Staiger, Stock and Watson (1997, Journal of Economic Perspectives) show that the natural rate of unemployment is also measured with wide uncertainty, so the Okun cross-check is not free of the same problem.

The left column’s claims are the book’s: the vintages agree on direction (the trough table of Act I); Okun’s law links the gap to unemployment.

The right column’s first claim is the research above; the slide adds no outside numbers.

Ask the policy students: if you had to set interest rates tomorrow, which three numbers would you look at? (Inflation, unemployment, and some estimate of the gap; and you would ask how often each has been revised.)

“The Phillips curve is dead”

A skeptic says: “Look at the cloud. 1974, 1979 and 2021–22 sit far above the line. In 2023–2025 inflation fell while output was above potential. Even the best line explains less than a fifth of the variation. A curve that misses this often cannot guide policy.”

Reply: over 65 years the cloud still slopes up, about 0.35, and the big misses are price shocks that the full model adds as a shift of the curve. Every large disinflation in the data came with a slump, 1981–83 above all. A flat, noisy curve is a reason to expect costly disinflations, not a reason to ignore the gap.

Take the objection seriously. The simple curve of this chapter has no price shocks and no expected inflation, and a flat curve with large shocks is hard to see in a scatter plot.

The research. Blanchard (2016, American Economic Review Papers and Proceedings) finds that the U.S. Phillips curve has become flatter but is still there, with inflation now anchored by expectations. Hooper, Mishkin and Sufi (2020, Research in Economics) ask whether the curve is dead or “hibernating” and find a stronger relation in city- and state-level data and in periods of high labor-market pressure. Hazell, Herreño, Nakamura and Steinsson (2022, Quarterly Journal of Economics) estimate the slope from U.S. states and find it small but positive, and argue that the fall of inflation in the 1980s owed more to changing expectations than to the slope. These are contested readings of a hard identification problem.

The book’s own numbers for the reply: OLS slope 0.35 for 1961–2025; squared misses fall from 199.7 to 163.6; the Volcker years all moved in the model’s direction.

Ask: if the curve is flat, is that good or bad news for a central bank fighting inflation? (Bad: the sacrifice ratio 1/ν̄ is large. Good: a boom raises inflation only slowly.)

What to remember from this chapter

  • Short-run output is the percent gap from potential, \(\widetilde{Y} = (Y - \bar{Y})/\bar{Y}\). Actual growth is potential growth plus the change in the gap.
  • U.S. recessions push output a few percent below potential, cost millions of jobs and up to a fifth of a year’s GDP. The measured gap changes when the data are revised.
  • Inflation usually falls in a recession. The Phillips curve, \(\Delta\pi = \bar{\nu}\widetilde{Y}\) with a U.S. slope near 1/3, makes inflation a running total of past gaps.
  • A lasting boom raises inflation every year; cutting inflation by a point costs \(1/\bar{\nu}\) percent-years of output.
  • Okun’s law, \(u - \bar{u} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\widetilde{Y}\): unemployment rises whenever growth falls short of potential growth.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: why does output swing around its trend, and what do booms and slumps do to inflation and jobs? A good answer: shocks push output away from potential; a boom pushes inflation up year after year and unemployment down; a slump does the opposite, and bringing inflation down requires one.

For the policy students, three habits: ask whether output is growing faster or slower than potential, not only whether it is growing; ask which vintage and which agency a gap comes from; and treat any promise of a lasting boom as a promise of rising inflation.
