Learning goals

Inflation is the growth rate of the overall price level. By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  • measure inflation from a price index, and convert a price from one year into the dollars of another year with the consumer price index (CPI);
  • solve the quantity theory of money for the price level, \(P^* = \bar{M}\bar{V}/\bar{Y}\), and for inflation, \(\pi^* = \bar{g}_M - \bar{g}_Y\);
  • test the quantity theory with 150 years of U.S. data and with 165 economies;
  • compute real interest rates with the Fisher equation, \(i = R + \pi\);
  • explain why very high inflation is usually a fiscal problem: governments print money to pay their bills;
  • rebuild a price index from inflation rates, compute average growth rates over periods, use ratio scales with clear tick labels, and spot suspicious numbers in a data set.

The model in code

The chapter’s model is the quantity theory of money. Let \(M_t\) be the amount of money, \(V_t\) the velocity of money (how many times a year each dollar is used to buy goods), \(P_t\) the price level and \(Y_t\) real GDP. The theory has four equations (Table 8.3 of the textbook):

Equation Meaning
\(M_t V_t = P_t Y_t\) the quantity equation (8.1): money spent = nominal GDP
\(Y_t = \bar{Y}_t\) real GDP comes from the growth model (the classical dichotomy)
\(V_t = \bar{V}\) velocity is constant
\(M_t = \bar{M}_t\) the central bank chooses the money supply

Only the price level is left for the quantity equation to pin down:

\[P_t^* = \frac{\bar{M}_t \bar{V}}{\bar{Y}_t} \qquad (8.2)\]

In growth rates, the growth of a product is the sum of the growth rates, so \(\bar{g}_M + \bar{g}_V = g_P + \bar{g}_Y\) (8.3). With constant velocity, \(\bar{g}_V = 0\), and inflation \(\pi = g_P\) is

\[\pi^* = \bar{g}_M - \bar{g}_Y \qquad (8.4)\]

Inflation also links the two kinds of interest rates. The nominal rate \(i\) is paid in dollars, the real rate \(R\) in goods, and the Fisher equation says

\[i = R + \pi \qquad (8.5) \qquad\qquad R = i - \pi \qquad (8.6)\]

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. Writing g_V=0.0 gives an argument a default value, used whenever we do not pass one. All rates here are in percent.

def price_level(M, V, Y):
    """Equation (8.2): the price level that solves the quantity equation M V = P Y."""
    return M * V / Y


def qt_inflation(g_M, g_Y, g_V=0.0):
    """Equations (8.3)-(8.4): inflation = money growth + velocity growth - real GDP growth (percent)."""
    return g_M + g_V - g_Y


def nominal_rate(R, pi):
    """Equation (8.5), the Fisher equation: the nominal interest rate i = R + pi (percent)."""
    return R + pi


def real_rate(i, pi):
    """Equation (8.6): the real interest rate R = i - pi (percent)."""
    return i - pi

Equation (8.4) is a rule for growth rates, and it is exact only for small changes. To see how close it is, we let money and real GDP grow year by year and solve equation (8.2) in every year.

Python note. for t in range(years + 1) repeats the indented lines for t = 0, 1, …, years. rows.append([...]) adds one row to a list, and pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=[...]) turns the list into a table. .pct_change() gives the change from the previous row as a fraction.

def simulate_prices(g_M=7.0, g_Y=4.0, V=5.0, years=5):
    """Equation (8.2) year by year: money grows at g_M percent and real GDP at g_Y percent."""
    M, Y = 200.0, 500.0
    rows = []
    for t in range(years + 1):
        rows.append([t, M, Y, price_level(M, V, Y)])
        M = M * (1 + g_M / 100)
        Y = Y * (1 + g_Y / 100)
    path = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["year", "M", "Y", "P"])
    path["inflation"] = 100 * path.P.pct_change()
    return path

def high_inflation_share(threshold=25):
    """Figure 8.2: the percentage of reporting economies with inflation above `threshold` percent."""
    above = wdi.inflation > threshold
    share = (100 * above.groupby(wdi.year).mean()).reset_index(name="share")
    peak = share.loc[share.share.idxmax()]
    fig = px.area(share, x="year", y="share", hover_name="year",
                  labels={"year": "Year", "share": "Fraction of countries (percent)"},
                  title=f"Inflation above {threshold}%: the share of countries peaked at {peak.share:.1f}% in {peak.year:.0f}")
    fig.update_traces(line=dict(color=TEAL, width=2))
    fig.update_yaxes(range=[0, 90])
    return fig

Python note. range(1870, 2025, years) counts 1870, 1870 + years, … up to 2024: the first year of each period. min(a, b) is the smaller number, so the last period stops in 2025. x ** (1 / n) is the n-th root.

money = pd.read_csv(DATA_URL + "us_money_prices.csv").set_index("year")


def growth(x, start, end):
    """Average annual growth of x from start to end, in percent."""
    return 100 * ((x[end] / x[start]) ** (1 / (end - start)) - 1)


def periods(years=10):
    """Figure 8.3: average money growth and inflation in periods of `years` years since 1870."""
    rows = []
    for start in range(1870, 2025, years):
        end = min(start + years, 2025)
        if years == 10 and end - start == 10:
            label = f"{start}s"
        else:
            label = f"{start}–{end}"
        kind = "full period" if end - start == years else "unfinished period"
        rows.append([label, start, end, growth(money.m2, start, end), growth(money.cpi, start, end), kind])
    return pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["period", "start", "end", "money_growth", "inflation", "kind"])

The inflation tax

Why would a government let money grow so fast? Because printing money is one way to pay its bills. The government budget constraint says that spending \(G\) is paid with taxes \(T\), new borrowing \(\Delta B\), or new money \(\Delta M\):

\[G = T + \Delta B + \Delta M \qquad (8.7)\]

The revenue from new money is called seignorage, or the inflation tax. It is paid by everyone who holds money: when the price level doubles, a 200-dollar bill buys only what 100 dollars bought before.

def money_printed(G, T, dB):
    """Equation (8.7): the new money the government must issue, Delta M = G - T - Delta B."""
    return G - T - dB

Summary

  • Inflation is the growth rate of the price level. U.S. inflation peaked at 13.5 percent in 1980, stayed near 2 percent for two decades, and jumped to 8 percent in 2022. A price index turns past prices into today’s dollars.
  • The quantity theory of money gives \(P^* = \bar{M}\bar{V}/\bar{Y}\) and \(\pi^* = \bar{g}_M - \bar{g}_Y\): in the long run, faster money growth means higher inflation, one for one. U.S. decades and 165 economies line up as predicted, although velocity is not really constant.
  • The Fisher equation \(i = R + \pi\) turns nominal interest rates into real ones. U.S. real rates were negative in the 1970s and in most years since 2008.
  • Hyperinflations happen when governments cannot pay their bills with taxes or borrowing and print money instead: persistent high inflation is a fiscal problem. They can end as quickly as they start.
