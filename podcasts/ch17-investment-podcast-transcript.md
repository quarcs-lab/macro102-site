## Part 1: Investment swings

HOST: Part one of the map: Investment swings. First, what do economists mean by investment? To me, investing means buying shares.

ECONOMIST: That's the everyday meaning, but not ours. Buying a share only moves ownership from one saver to another. Here, investment means spending on new capital, new things that help us produce more in the future.

HOST: And the first leaf says it has three parts.

ECONOMIST: Right. Business investment is the equipment, buildings, software and research of firms. Housing is the building of new homes. And inventories are the change in the goods that firms keep in store, made but not yet sold.

HOST: Next leaf: investment in 2006 and 2009. How big was the fall?

ECONOMIST: We measure investment as a share of GDP, a country's total output in a year. In the United States, private investment was 19.6 percent of GDP in 2006. By 2009 it was down to 13.3 percent.

HOST: So it fell by about 6 points of GDP. Let me guess which part fell most. Firms stop building factories in a recession, so my guess is business investment.

ECONOMIST: That's a natural guess, and it was right in the dot-com bust of 2000 to 2002. But this time it's wrong. These are percentage points, the plain difference between two percentages. Of the fall of about 6 points, housing alone explains about 3.4, more than half. Inventories and business investment share the rest.

HOST: Then the next leaf, housing, is the big story.

ECONOMIST: It is. Housing investment peaked at 6.6 percent of GDP in 2005. By 2011 it was down to 2.4 percent, less than half. That was the housing bust, a sharp fall after a boom, in the Great Recession. Part three shows why expected price gains matter so much for house prices.

HOST: Next leaf: inventories. In a recession, shops can't sell their goods. So unsold goods build up in the warehouses, and inventories rise.

ECONOMIST: A lot of students think that, but the data say the opposite. Inventory investment turned negative in recession years such as 1982, 2009 and 2020. Negative means firms took more goods out of store than they put in, so their inventories shrank.

HOST: Why would firms do that?

ECONOMIST: Because they cut production by more than their sales fall. Many inventories are parts and materials inside the production line. Less production means fewer parts in the line. So inventory investment rises in booms and falls in recessions.

HOST: Last leaf of this part: Ireland's housing. Was the housing boom only an American story?

ECONOMIST: No, and some booms were much bigger. In Ireland, housing investment reached 13.5 percent of GDP in 2006, about twice the U.S. peak. By 2011 it had fallen to 2 percent.

HOST: So in part one we saw that investment is the part of spending that swings most. In the Great Recession, housing and inventories fell hardest, and Ireland's housing fell even more. But why does investment swing so much?

ECONOMIST: Because every investment is a bet on the future. Next branch: how a firm decides how much capital to own.

## Part 2: The user cost of capital

HOST: Part two of the map: The user cost of capital. Where do we start?

ECONOMIST: On Islandia, our imaginary island country. Harbor Boatworks builds the island's fishing boats in a workshop. It has some spare cash. It can leave the money in a savings account at the bank in town, or buy one more power saw.

HOST: And the first leaf, the arbitrage equation, compares those two choices?

ECONOMIST: Exactly. Put the same dollars into each choice for a year. The bank pays interest. The saw pays the extra output it makes, plus any change in its price when the firm sells it. Economists call that extra output the marginal product of capital. When the firm has chosen well, both choices pay the same.

HOST: Why must they pay the same?

ECONOMIST: Suppose the saw paid more than the bank. Then the firm buys another saw. But each extra saw has less work to do, so its extra output is smaller. The firm keeps buying until the last saw pays just what the bank pays. Moving money toward the better choice until no gain is left is called arbitrage.

HOST: Next leaf: the user cost. What does a saw really cost the firm each year?

ECONOMIST: Three things. First, the interest the firm gives up by not leaving the money in the bank. Second, wear and tear, the part of the saw that is used up during the year. Third, take away any rise in the saw's price, because the firm gets that back when it sells. Together, that's the user cost of capital.

HOST: Give me Islandia's numbers.

ECONOMIST: The bank pays 2 percent a year, after inflation. Each year, 8 percent of a saw wears out. Saw prices stay the same. Add them up, and the user cost is 10 percent a year. Each dollar of saw must add at least 10 cents of output a year.

HOST: Now the tax leaf. The Islandia Treasury taxes Harbor Boatworks' profits. How does that change the user cost?

ECONOMIST: The Treasury takes a slice of what every saw produces. But it pays none of the saw's costs. So the saw must produce more before tax, to leave the firm the same return after tax.

HOST: How much more?

ECONOMIST: Let's build that number step by step. Say the tax rate is 35 percent. Then the firm keeps only 65 cents of every dollar a saw produces. After tax, it still needs 10 cents per dollar of saw.

HOST: So before tax, the saw must produce 10 cents divided by 0.65?

ECONOMIST: Yes, about 15.4 cents. So the user cost rises from 10 to 15.4 percent. In cents, 15.4 is about 54 percent more than 10.

HOST: Next leaf: desired capital, the number of saws the firm wants. A 35 percent tax means the firm wants 35 percent fewer saws.

ECONOMIST: That's the tempting answer, but the fall is bigger, almost half. To see why, ask how fast a saw's extra output rises when the firm owns fewer saws. In this model, it rises slowly. Suppose Harbor Boatworks had only half as many saws. The last saw's extra output would rise from 10 cents to only about 16 cents. That's a rise of about 59 percent, not double.

HOST: And the tax asks for a rise of about 54 percent, a bit less than 59. So the firm must cut its saws by a bit less than half?

ECONOMIST: Exactly. Here is the exact rule. The tax divides the user cost by 0.65, the share the firm keeps. Then the number of saws the firm wants is multiplied by 0.65 times the square root of 0.65.

HOST: And the square root of 0.65 is?

ECONOMIST: About 0.81. Now multiply: 0.65 times 0.81 is about 0.52. So the firm wants 0.52 times its old number of saws, about half. That's a fall of about 48 percent.

HOST: Does it sell half its saws all at once?

ECONOMIST: No. Investment is the flow that takes the firm from the saws it has to the saws it wants. With too many saws, it simply stops buying, and wear shrinks its capital.

HOST: So investment can swing much more than capital?

ECONOMIST: Yes, and this is why investment is so unstable. A firm that already has the saws it wants buys about 8 cents of new saws a year for each dollar of saws, just to replace wear. Now suppose it wants just 8 percent fewer saws. Then it can stop buying for a whole year. A small change in the capital it wants cuts this year's investment to zero.

HOST: Last leaf of this part: the investment rate. That's the share of output a country spends on new capital each year, right?

ECONOMIST: Yes. Take all of Islandia's firms together. Say their capital grows 2 percent a year. Add the 8 percent that wears out. So for each dollar of capital, firms need 10 cents of new capital a year.

HOST: And how do we turn that into a share of output?

ECONOMIST: As in the production model of Chapter 4, owners of capital get one third of output. Each dollar of capital earns its user cost, 10 cents. So output is three times that: 30 cents for each dollar of capital. The investment rate is the 10 cents of new capital divided by the 30 cents of output. That's one third, or 33.3 percent. Our round numbers make it higher than in the U.S. data.

HOST: And with the 35 percent tax?

ECONOMIST: Firms still need 10 cents of new capital for each dollar of capital. But now each dollar earns 15.4 cents, so it goes with about 46 cents of output. 10 divided by 46 is about 21.7 percent, down from 33.3. The Solow model of Chapter 5 simply assumed the investment rate. Here, taxes and interest rates explain it.

HOST: So in part two we saw that a machine must earn the bank's interest plus its wear, minus any price gain. A tax makes that target higher, and the firm wants far fewer machines.

ECONOMIST: And each year's investment is small compared with the capital firms own. So when rates, taxes or expected prices change the capital firms want a little, investment changes a lot. Next branch: stocks and houses.

## Part 3: Stocks, houses and policy

HOST: Part three of the map: Stocks, houses and policy. First leaf: a stock's price. Islanders can buy shares of Harbor Boatworks. What is a share worth?

ECONOMIST: Same comparison: savings in the bank, or a share held for a year. The share pays a dividend, a part of the firm's profits paid to each owner. Then it sells at a new price. With no risk, both choices must pay the same.

HOST: Numbers, please.

ECONOMIST: For shares, we use new round numbers: the bank pays 5 percent. Say the share pays 3 dollars a year, forever, with no growth. Then the dividend alone must match the bank's 5 percent. So the price is 3 dollars divided by 5 percent: 60 dollars.

HOST: And if the dividend grows?

ECONOMIST: Let it grow 2 percent a year. The price then grows just as fast, a yearly price gain for the owner. So the dividend only needs to pay the other 3 percent: 3 dollars divided by 3 percent is 100 dollars. In general, the price is the dividend divided by the gap between interest and growth. Since that gap is small, small changes in it move the price a lot.

HOST: Next leaf: the price-earnings ratio. That's the share price divided by the firm's earnings, its profit per share?

ECONOMIST: Yes. The economist Robert Shiller divides the price by average earnings over the past ten years. For the U.S. stock market, his ratio was 40.7 in October 2026. Its average since 1881 is 17.8.

HOST: And what does our model say the ratio should be?

ECONOMIST: Divide the share price by earnings, and you get a simple rule. The ratio is the share of earnings paid out as dividends, divided by the gap. Say firms pay out half, and the gap is 3 percent as before. One half divided by 0.03 is about 16.7, close to the long-run average.

HOST: So today's 40.7 is more than twice the average. My guess: prices are twice too high. It's a bubble.

ECONOMIST: Maybe, but the model says we can't be sure. Turn the rule around. A ratio of 40 means the gap is one half divided by 40, only one and a quarter percentage points. With the bank at 5 percent, investors must expect dividends to grow about 3.75 percent a year, forever.

HOST: That sounds very optimistic.

ECONOMIST: It may be. But a small change in beliefs about growth or interest rates can justify a big change in prices. So a bubble is hard to tell apart from real optimism. The same debate ran in 1999, when the ratio hit its record, 44.2.

HOST: Next leaf: Tobin's q. What is q?

ECONOMIST: The economist James Tobin's q compares two values of a firm. The first is what the stock market says the whole firm is worth. The second is what it would cost to buy all its capital new. Tobin's q is the first divided by the second.

HOST: And when q is above 1, the firm should invest?

ECONOMIST: Yes. Then Harbor Boatworks could sell new shares, buy saws with the money, and be worth more than the saws cost. The market values a firm by what its machines will earn. So a q above 1 means a saw earns more than its user cost. Below 1, the firm is worth less than its machines, and it should shrink.

HOST: Next leaf: house price. Islanders buy seaside cottages with a mortgage, a loan to buy a house, and rent them out. Same idea?

ECONOMIST: Same idea. A house is worth its rent divided by its user cost. The buyer pays part of the price with her own money, the down payment, and borrows the rest.

HOST: So what is the user cost of a house?

ECONOMIST: First, the interest on the loan. Islandia's tax rules let owners subtract that interest from their taxable income, so it costs a bit less. Second, the interest she gives up on her own money, which could have stayed in the bank. Third, wear. Then take away the price rise she expects.

HOST: And the numbers for a cottage?

ECONOMIST: New numbers again. The bank and the loan charge 4 percent, wear is 2 percent, and buyers expect prices to rise 2 percent a year. That expected rise cancels the wear. What is left is the interest, made a bit cheaper by the tax rule: about 3 percent of the price. The cottage rents for 6,000 dollars a year, so it's worth about 197,000 dollars.

HOST: So which moves house prices more, easier credit or expectations? I'd bet on credit. If buyers only need their own money for 10 percent of the price instead of 20, prices should jump.

ECONOMIST: That's the tempting answer, but credit matters much less. Her own money and borrowed money cost about the same interest. Only the tax rule makes borrowing a little cheaper. So the price rises by only about 4 percent. Now suppose buyers expect prices to grow 1 point faster each year.

HOST: And what happens then?

ECONOMIST: The expected rise now more than cancels the wear, so the user cost falls from about 3 to about 2 percent. The price is the rent divided by the user cost. Divide by 2 instead of 3, and the price becomes about one and a half times as large. That's a rise of about 49 percent. And if rising prices make people expect more rises, the price keeps pushing itself up. That's how a housing bubble grows.

HOST: Last leaf: policy. The map lists corporate taxes, bubbles and housing. We've seen why bubbles are hard to spot. So start with taxes.

ECONOMIST: In 2017, the United States cut its federal corporate tax rate from 35 to 21 percent. In our model, that raises the capital firms want by about a third. One study found that firms with bigger tax cuts did invest more. But the cut cost a lot of tax revenue, so it is still debated.

HOST: And housing?

ECONOMIST: Rules that require bigger down payments barely move prices in the model. But they limit how much buyers borrow. And heavy borrowing can turn a fall in house prices into a banking crisis, as we saw in Chapter 10.

HOST: So in part three we saw that shares and houses are priced like machines: what they pay, divided by a user cost. And because that cost is small, expectations move prices a lot.

ECONOMIST: Exactly. Let's sum up.

## Wrap-up

HOST: Let's go back to our three goals. First, how does investment swing, and why?

ECONOMIST: It falls hard in recessions, and housing and inventories swing most. It swings because each year's investment is small compared with the capital firms own. And the capital they want depends on rates, taxes and expected prices.

HOST: Second, the user cost: interest plus wear, minus any expected price gain. A 35 percent tax raises it from 10 to 15.4 percent, and the firm wants about half the saws.

ECONOMIST: Right, because a saw's extra output rises only slowly as the firm owns fewer saws. Third, shares and houses are worth what they pay, divided by a small gap. So a small change in expected growth moves their prices a lot.

HOST: So what's the big message?

ECONOMIST: Every investment, in a machine, a share or a house, must earn what the bank pays. That one comparison sets the user cost of capital, how much firms invest, and what stocks and houses are worth. And because it rests on expectations of the future, investment swings sharply in booms and recessions.

HOST: Here's a question to think about. Islandia announces a tariff, a tax on imported goods. It will raise the price of power saws by 3 percent over the coming year, then stop. What happens to Harbor Boatworks' investment this year and next year, and why?

ECONOMIST: Pause the episode and think about it for a moment.

HOST: Okay, we're back. What's the answer?

ECONOMIST: This year, investment jumps. A saw bought now can be sold for 3 percent more later. That price gain lowers the user cost from 10 to 7 percent, so the firm wants about 1.7 times as many saws, and buys them now. Next year, the price gain is gone, and the user cost goes back up. Now the firm owns more saws than it wants. So it stops buying and lets wear shrink its capital. The tariff moves investment forward in time: a boom, then a bust.
