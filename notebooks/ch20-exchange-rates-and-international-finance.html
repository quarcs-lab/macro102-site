Learning goals

Chapter 19 studied trade between countries. This chapter adds the price at which their currencies trade, the exchange rate, and asks what moves it in the long run and in the short run. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • tell the nominal exchange rate (the price of a currency) from the real exchange rate (the price of goods), and explain both with the law of one price and sticky inflation;
  • test the law of one price on data: the yen and the dollar since 1971, and the Big Mac index;
  • add exchange rates to the short-run model: a flatter IS curve, and a rise in foreign interest rates as an aggregate demand shock in the AS/AD framework;
  • explain the policy trilemma and read the history of exchange-rate regimes, currency crises and the euro crisis in the data;
  • in Python: build price levels from inflation rates with cumprod, plot two vertical scales in one figure, switch views with Plotly buttons, rebase an index, and reshape tables with melt for grouped bar charts.

The model in code

Exchange rates in the long run. The nominal exchange rate \(E\) is the number of units of foreign currency that one dollar buys, for example 0.92 euros. The law of one price says that, in the long run, a good sells for the same price everywhere once prices are written in the same currency. With \(P\) the U.S. price and \(P^w\) the foreign (“world”) price,

\[EP = P^w. \qquad (20.1)\]

Apply it to the whole basket of goods of each economy. The quantity theory of money pins down the two price levels in the long run, so the law of one price pins down the exchange rate:

\[\bar{E} = \frac{\bar{P}^w}{\bar{P}}. \qquad (20.3)\]

The real exchange rate \(EP/P^w\) is the price of U.S. goods in units of foreign goods. If the law of one price holds, it equals 1 in the long run, \(\overline{RER} = \bar{E}P/\bar{P}^w = 1\) (20.4). In the short run, \(E\) jumps with financial news while prices are sticky, so the real exchange rate moves with \(E\).

Exchange rates in the short-run model. When the U.S. real interest rate \(R_t\) rises, investors want dollars, the dollar appreciates, U.S. goods become expensive and net exports fall. When the foreign real interest rate \(\bar{R}^w\) rises, the opposite happens:

\[\frac{NX_t}{\bar{Y}_t} = \bar{a}_{nx} - \bar{b}_{nx}\,(R_t - \bar{R}^w). \qquad (20.6)\]

Adding and subtracting the marginal product of capital \(\bar{r}\) shows that net exports react to \(R_t - \bar{r}\) just like investment does. The IS curve keeps its old form, with two new pieces:

\[\tilde{Y}_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R_t - \bar{r}), \qquad \bar{b} \equiv \bar{b}_i + \bar{b}_{nx}, \qquad \bar{a} \equiv \bar{a}_c + \bar{a}_i + \bar{a}_g + \bar{a}_{nx} - 1 + \bar{b}_{nx}(\bar{R}^w - \bar{r}). \qquad (20.8)\]

So a foreign interest rate above \(\bar{r}\) works as a positive aggregate demand shock \(\bar{b}_{nx}(\bar{R}^w - \bar{r})\). Combined with the policy rule of Chapter 13, the IS curve gives the AD curve \(\tilde{Y}_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{m}(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})\), and the Phillips curve gives the AS curve \(\pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + \bar{\nu}\tilde{Y}_t\).

One baseline for every diagram. The textbook draws its diagrams without numbers. Both model diagrams of this notebook start from one illustrative set of parameters:

  • marginal product of capital \(\bar{r} = 5\) percent, as in Chapter 11’s example, and no demand shock at home, \(\bar{a}_c + \bar{a}_i + \bar{a}_g + \bar{a}_{nx} = 1\);
  • interest sensitivities \(\bar{b}_i = 1\) and \(\bar{b}_{nx} = 1\), so the total \(\bar{b} = 2\) is the value of Chapters 11 and 13, split half and half between investment and trade;
  • inflation target \(\bar{\pi} = 2\) percent, policy response \(\bar{m} = 1/2\) and Phillips-curve slope \(\bar{\nu} = 1\), as in Chapter 13;
  • the foreign shock of Figure 5: the foreign real interest rate is 2 points above \(\bar{r}\), at 7 percent, in periods 1 to 9 and back at \(\bar{r}\) afterwards.

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. Writing b_nx=B_NX gives an argument a default value, used whenever we do not pass one. Names in capital letters, such as R_BAR, are constants that we set once and never change.

R_BAR, B_I, B_NX = 5.0, 1.0, 1.0            # marginal product of capital (%), interest sensitivities
PI_BAR, M_BAR, NU_BAR = 2.0, 0.5, 1.0       # inflation target (%), policy response, Phillips-curve slope
RW_GAP, A_LAST, T_MAX = 2.0, 9, 20          # foreign rate minus r_bar in periods 1-9; periods simulated


def long_run_exchange_rate(P_w, P):
    """Equation 20.3: the long-run nominal exchange rate E = P_w / P (foreign currency per dollar)."""
    return P_w / P


def real_exchange_rate(E, P, P_w):
    """The real exchange rate E P / P_w: foreign goods needed to buy one U.S. good (equation 20.4)."""
    return E * P / P_w


def net_exports(R, R_w=R_BAR, a_nx=0.0, b_nx=B_NX):
    """Equation 20.6: net exports in percent of potential output, NX/Y = a_nx - b_nx (R - R_w)."""
    return a_nx - b_nx * (R - R_w)


def foreign_shock(R_w, b_nx=B_NX, r_bar=R_BAR):
    """The part of a_bar that comes from abroad in equation 20.8: b_nx (R_w - r_bar)."""
    return b_nx * (R_w - r_bar)
def is_curve(R, a=0.0, b_i=B_I, b_nx=B_NX, r_bar=R_BAR):
    """Equation 20.8, the open-economy IS curve: Y~ = a - (b_i + b_nx) (R - r_bar), in percent."""
    return a - (b_i + b_nx) * (R - r_bar)


def ad_output(pi, a=0.0, m=M_BAR, b=B_I + B_NX):
    """The AD curve of Chapter 13 with the open-economy b: Y~ = a - b m (pi - pi_bar)."""
    return a - b * m * (pi - PI_BAR)


def as_inflation(Y, pi_e, nu=NU_BAR):
    """The AS curve of Chapter 13: pi = pi_e + nu Y~, with expected inflation pi_e = last period's inflation."""
    return pi_e + nu * Y


def solve_asad(pi_e, a=0.0, m=M_BAR, b=B_I + B_NX, nu=NU_BAR):
    """The point where the AD and AS curves cross: short-run output and inflation in one period."""
    Y = (a - b * m * (pi_e - PI_BAR)) / (1 + b * m * nu)
    return Y, as_inflation(Y, pi_e, nu)

One loop for the dynamics

Figure 5 follows the economy period by period. Each period, firms expect last period’s inflation, and the economy jumps to the point where that period’s AD and AS curves cross. simulate repeats this step; shock_list builds the list of demand shocks, one value per period.

Python note. values.append(x) adds x at the end of the list values, and for t in range(1, 21) repeats the indented lines for t = 1, 2, …, 20. dict(period=0, Y=0.0) is a dictionary, a set of named values, and rows[-1]["pi"] is the value named "pi" in the last element of the list. pd.DataFrame(rows) turns a list of dictionaries into a table, one row per dictionary.

def shock_list(size, last=A_LAST):
    """A demand shock of the given size in periods 1, ..., last and zero afterwards, for T_MAX periods."""
    values = []
    for t in range(1, T_MAX + 1):
        if t <= last:
            values.append(size)
        else:
            values.append(0.0)
    return values


def simulate(a_list, m=M_BAR, b=B_I + B_NX, nu=NU_BAR):
    """Solve the AS/AD model period by period; period 0 is the steady state (Y~ = 0, pi = pi_bar)."""
    rows = [dict(period=0, a=0.0, pi_e=PI_BAR, Y=0.0, pi=PI_BAR)]
    for t in range(1, len(a_list) + 1):
        pi_e = rows[-1]["pi"]                   # adaptive expectations: last period's inflation
        Y, pi = solve_asad(pi_e, a_list[t - 1], m, b, nu)
        rows.append(dict(period=t, a=a_list[t - 1], pi_e=pi_e, Y=Y, pi=pi))
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

Drawing tools

Both model diagrams use the same few drawing steps, so we write them once:

  • add_curve draws a curve and writes its name at its last point inside the axes, as the textbook does;
  • add_point marks a point with a dot, a letter and dashed guides to the axes;
  • add_arrow draws an arrow between two points.

Python note. (x >= 0) & (y <= 8) is true where both conditions hold, x[inside] keeps the values where inside is true, and [-1] takes the last of them. add_annotation with axref="x" and ayref="y" draws an arrow from (ax, ay) to (x, y), both in axis units. Labels such as "AS<sub>8</sub>" use HTML to write AS₈.

def add_curve(fig, x, y, label, color, x_range, y_range, dash="solid"):
    """Draw one curve and write its label at its last point inside the axes (no label if label is "")."""
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x, y=y, mode="lines", name=label, showlegend=False,
                             line=dict(color=color, width=3, dash=dash)))
    inside = (x >= x_range[0]) & (x <= x_range[1]) & (y >= y_range[0]) & (y <= y_range[1])
    if inside.any() and label != "":
        fig.add_annotation(x=x[inside][-1], y=y[inside][-1], text=label, showarrow=False,
                           xanchor="left", yanchor="bottom", font=dict(color=color, size=14))


def add_point(fig, x, y, label, x0, y0, guides=True):
    """Mark the point (x, y) with a dot and a label; with guides, dashed lines to the axes at x0 and y0."""
    if guides:
        fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[x0, x, x], y=[y, y, y0], mode="lines", showlegend=False, hoverinfo="skip",
                                 line=dict(color="black", width=1, dash="dash")))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[x], y=[y], mode="markers+text", text=[label], textposition="top right",
                             name=label, showlegend=False, textfont=dict(size=16), marker=dict(color="black", size=10)))


def add_arrow(fig, x0, y0, x1, y1, color="black"):
    """Draw an arrow from (x0, y0) to (x1, y1) in axis units; nothing if the two points almost coincide."""
    if abs(x1 - x0) + abs(y1 - y0) > 0.15:
        fig.add_annotation(x=x1, y=y1, ax=x0, ay=y0, xref="x", yref="y", axref="x", ayref="y", text="",
                           showarrow=True, arrowhead=3, arrowwidth=2, arrowcolor=color)

Figure 4: Increasing interest rates and the IS curve

Goal. See why the IS curve is flatter in an open economy: a higher interest rate lowers net exports as well as investment.

The central bank raises the real interest rate from \(\bar{r}\) to \(R'\). The economy moves along the IS curve from \(A\) to \(B\). Two forces lower output:

\[\uparrow R \;\Rightarrow\; \downarrow \frac{I}{\bar{Y}} \quad\text{and}\quad \uparrow R \Rightarrow \uparrow E \Rightarrow \uparrow \frac{EP}{P^w} \Rightarrow \downarrow \frac{NX}{\bar{Y}}.\]

From equation (20.8), \(\tilde{Y}' = -(\bar{b}_i + \bar{b}_{nx})(R' - \bar{r})\). The dashed gray line is the IS curve without the trade channel (\(\bar{b}_{nx} = 0\)), the closed economy of Chapter 11.

Figure 5: An increase in foreign interest rates

Goal. Follow the international transmission of monetary policy: the European Central Bank raises its interest rate, and the U.S. economy booms, then slumps.

When the foreign real interest rate rises to \(\bar{R}^w = 7\) percent, 2 points above \(\bar{r}\), investors move from dollars to euros, the dollar depreciates, U.S. goods become cheap and U.S. net exports rise. In equation (20.8) this is a positive demand shock, \(\bar{a} = \bar{b}_{nx}(\bar{R}^w - \bar{r}) = 2\), so the AD curve shifts out to AD₁. Then the familiar dynamics of Chapter 13 take over:

  • period 1: the economy jumps from \(A\) to \(B\), with output and inflation above normal;
  • periods 2–9: inflation rises, the Fed raises U.S. interest rates, and the economy climbs AD₁ toward \(C\) (period 8 in the textbook), with output back at potential but inflation above target;
  • period 10: the foreign rate falls back to \(\bar{r}\), the shock ends, AD shifts back, and the economy drops to a recession at \(D\); it then slides down AD back to \(A\).

Figure 7: The policy trilemma in open economies

Goal. See why an open economy can have at most two of three good things: a stable exchange rate, its own monetary policy, and free flows of international finance.

The textbook draws the policy trilemma as a triangle: the three goals are its corners, and each side is a regime that achieves the two goals at its ends and gives up the one opposite. Here the triangle is live. A small model from the chapter computes what happens when the foreign central bank (say, the Fed) raises its interest rate by \(\Delta i^w\), while your economy needs its own interest rate to change by \(\Delta i^{need}\):

  • with free financial flows, the exchange rate moves with the interest gap (Section 20.3: \(\uparrow i \Rightarrow \uparrow E\)), \(\%\Delta E = \eta\,(\Delta i - \Delta i^w)\), with an illustrative \(\eta = 5\) percent per point;
  • floating exchange rate: the central bank sets \(\Delta i = \Delta i^{need}\), and the exchange rate moves;
  • currency board (or monetary union): to keep \(E\) fixed, the interest rate must follow abroad, \(\Delta i = \Delta i^w\);
  • capital controls: the central bank sets \(\Delta i = \Delta i^{need}\) and keeps \(E\) fixed, because it rations the trade in its currency, so money cannot flow freely.
ETA = 5.0                                       # percent change of E per point of interest gap (illustrative)
LOST = {"floating exchange rate": "stable exchange rate", "currency board": "monetary autonomy",
        "capital controls": "free financial flows"}     # the goal each regime gives up: the opposite corner


def trilemma_outcome(regime, di_world, di_need):
    """Figure 20.7 as a model: the change in the domestic interest rate and the percent change in E."""
    if regime == "currency board":
        di = di_world                           # the peg forces the domestic rate to follow the foreign rate
    else:
        di = di_need                            # floating or capital controls: the central bank chooses
    if regime == "floating exchange rate":
        dE = ETA * (di - di_world)              # free capital flows move the exchange rate
    else:
        dE = 0.0                                # fixed by the peg, or by rationing the currency
    return di, dE + 0.0, LOST[regime]

Key numbers: the interest bill on a debt of 100 percent of GDP

A government with debt \(D\) must pay interest \(R \cdot D\) every year, so the interest bill in percent of GDP is the debt-GDP ratio times the interest rate. If investors fear a default and ask for a higher rate, the bill grows, which makes a default more likely: the crisis can be self-fulfilling.

def interest_bill(debt_ratio, R):
    """Yearly interest payments in percent of GDP: debt-GDP ratio (percent) times the interest rate (percent)."""
    return debt_ratio * R / 100

Summary

  • The nominal exchange rate is the price of a currency; the real exchange rate \(EP/P^w\) is the price of goods. In the long run the law of one price sets the real exchange rate to 1 and ties the nominal rate to relative price levels, so more inflation means a weaker currency.
  • The law of one price holds only loosely. Japan’s real exchange rate rose 2.5-fold until 1995, and Big Macs cost more in rich countries: nontraded goods get dearer as wages rise (the Balassa-Samuelson effect).
  • In the short run, prices are sticky and the exchange rate moves with interest rates. Net exports then react to the interest rate like investment, which makes the IS curve flatter, and a rise in foreign interest rates is a positive demand shock at home that ends in a recession when it is reversed.
  • The policy trilemma: an open economy can have at most two of a stable exchange rate, monetary autonomy and free capital flows. Gold, Bretton Woods, currency boards, capital controls and the euro are different choices of two, and the crises in Mexico, Asia, Argentina and the euro area show what happens when a choice breaks down.
