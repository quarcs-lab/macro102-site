18. The Government and the Macroeconomy

Learning goals

Governments spend between a third and more than half of GDP in the rich countries, and most of them have borrowed a lot. This chapter looks at the data on spending, taxes, deficits and debt, and at the accounting rules that tie them together. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • read the U.S. federal budget: where the money goes, where it comes from, and how large the deficit is, in percent of GDP and in dollars per person (Table 18.1);
  • derive the flow budget constraint from “uses equal sources”, and use it to compute next year’s debt and the primary and total deficits;
  • derive the intertemporal budget constraint by substitution, for two and for more periods, and read its comparative statics: how much a tax cut today raises the taxes of the future;
  • derive the law of motion of the debt-GDP ratio with the growth rules, solve for the primary surplus that holds it constant and for its steady state, and state the sustainability conditions (\(i\) below or above \(g\));
  • explain why the debt-GDP ratio falls when GDP grows faster than the interest rate, and split the changes in the U.S. ratio into deficits and growth;
  • derive the saving-investment identity and use it to measure crowding out;
  • compare government spending and debt across countries, and describe the fiscal problem of the twenty-first century: the rise of health spending;
  • in Python: write small accounting functions, reshape tables with melt and merge, draw a chart with two vertical axes, animate a bar chart, and compare two vintages of the same data.

Model overview

A government spends on goods, services and transfers, and it pays interest on what it has borrowed. Taxes pay for most of it, and borrowing covers whatever taxes do not. This chapter does not build a model of behavior that we solve for an equilibrium. Instead it rests on a handful of accounting relations that must hold in every year and in every country, whatever people and politicians decide. They connect each year’s deficit to the change in the debt, they force the budget to balance over time in present value, and they show why the debt relative to GDP depends on the interest rate and on growth.

We build these relations in five blocks, each going from definitions to algebra to predictions. First the flow budget constraint, which holds year by year. Second, the intertemporal budget constraint, which we get by chaining the flow constraints together. Third, the debt-GDP ratio: how it moves over time, its long-run level, and the conditions under which a debt is sustainable. Fourth, the saving-investment identity, which says when a deficit crowds out private investment. Fifth, the growth arithmetic behind the rise of health spending, the fiscal problem of this century. At the end, ### The model in code turns the relations into Python functions and checks every key result with numbers. The data sections reuse these functions.

Notation: capital letters such as \(G_t\), \(T_t\) and \(B_t\) are amounts in dollars in year \(t\). Lowercase letters such as \(b_t\) and \(d_t\) are the same amounts divided by GDP. The nominal interest rate \(i\) and the growth rate of nominal GDP \(g\) are taken as constant, and in the code we write them as decimals, so 5 percent is 0.05.

The flow budget constraint

Definitions

Think of the government as a household with a bank account that can go negative. In each year \(t\) it has three uses of funds:

  • purchases of goods and services \(G_t\) (soldiers, roads, schools);
  • transfers \(Tr_t\), money paid to people without buying anything in return (Social Security, unemployment insurance, welfare);
  • interest on the debt it owes at the start of the year, \(iB_t\). The stock of government debt \(B_t\) is the value of the bonds sold in the past and not yet repaid.

It also has three sources of funds:

  • taxes \(T_t\);
  • new borrowing \(\Delta B_{t+1} \equiv B_{t+1} - B_t\), the value of the new bonds sold during the year;
  • new money printed by the central bank, \(\Delta M_{t+1}\).

Money cannot appear from nowhere, so the two sides must match every single year:

\[\underbrace{G_t + Tr_t + iB_t}_{\text{uses}} = \underbrace{T_t + \Delta B_{t+1} + \Delta M_{t+1}}_{\text{sources}}. \tag{1}\]

Two simplifications follow the textbook. We set \(\Delta M_{t+1} = 0\): in the United States the Treasury does not pay its bills by printing money (the fiscal side of inflation is the topic of Chapter 8). And we fold transfers into \(G_t\), so from now on \(G_t\) means all non-interest spending. Neither change affects the logic.

The budget balance is taxes minus spending. It is a surplus when positive, a deficit when negative, and the budget is balanced when it is zero. Two versions matter, depending on whether interest is counted:

Measure Formula Includes interest?
primary deficit \(G_t - T_t\) no
total deficit \(G_t + iB_t - T_t\) yes

Newspapers and Table 18.1 report the total deficit. The primary deficit measures what current policy adds to the debt, leaving aside the cost of past borrowing.

Solving the model

Step 1: drop money and transfers. With \(\Delta M_{t+1} = 0\) and \(Tr_t\) inside \(G_t\), equation (1) becomes

\[G_t + iB_t = T_t + \Delta B_{t+1}.\]

Step 2: borrowing covers the total deficit. Move \(T_t\) to the left:

\[\Delta B_{t+1} = G_t + iB_t - T_t.\]

Every dollar of total deficit is a dollar of new bonds sold.

Step 3: next year’s debt. Write \(\Delta B_{t+1} = B_{t+1} - B_t\) and add \(B_t\) to both sides:

\[B_{t+1} = B_t + iB_t + G_t - T_t.\]

Group the two terms in \(B_t\), using \(B_t + iB_t = (1+i)B_t\):

\[B_{t+1} = (1+i)B_t + \underbrace{G_t - T_t}_{\text{primary deficit}}. \tag{2}\]

Intuition. The debt rolls forward like an unpaid credit-card balance: last year’s balance grows with interest, and this year’s excess of spending over taxes is added on top.

What the model predicts

Equation (2) answers the question “how fast does the debt grow?” for any policy. Divide it by \(B_t\):

\[\frac{\Delta B_{t+1}}{B_t} = i + \frac{G_t - T_t}{B_t}.\]

Policy Primary deficit Total deficit Growth rate of the debt
primary budget balanced, \(T_t = G_t\) \(0\) \(iB_t\) \(i\)
total budget balanced, \(T_t = G_t + iB_t\) \(-iB_t\) (a surplus) \(0\) \(0\)
primary deficit \(G_t - T_t > 0\) positive larger than \(iB_t\) above \(i\)
  • A one-dollar rise in the primary deficit raises next year’s debt by one dollar. A one-point rise in \(i\) raises it by \(0.01\,B_t\) dollars.
  • With a balanced primary budget, taxes cover current spending but not the interest, so the government borrows to pay the interest and the debt grows at the rate \(i\).
  • To hold the dollar debt constant, the government must run a primary surplus as large as its interest bill.

The intertemporal budget constraint

Assumptions

  • The economy lasts two periods, 1 and 2. Period 1 can stand for “today” and period 2 for “the whole future”.
  • The government starts period 1 owing \(B_1\), which may be positive (the United States starts with a large debt).
  • Equation (2) holds in each period, with a constant interest rate \(i\).
  • Nobody will lend to the government after period 2, because there is no later period in which to be repaid. So the debt left at the start of period 3 must be zero: \(B_3 = 0\).

Solving the model

Step 1: the two flow constraints. Equation (2) for \(t = 1\) and for \(t = 2\) reads

\[B_2 = (1+i)B_1 + G_1 - T_1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 0 = B_3 = (1+i)B_2 + G_2 - T_2.\]

Step 2: solve the second one for \(B_2\). Move \(G_2 - T_2\) to the left and divide by \(1+i\):

\[B_2 = \frac{T_2 - G_2}{1+i}.\]

The debt carried into period 2 must equal the period-2 surplus, valued in period-1 dollars.

Step 3: substitute into the first one. Replace \(B_2\) in the period-1 constraint:

\[\frac{T_2 - G_2}{1+i} = (1+i)B_1 + G_1 - T_1.\]

Step 4: sort spending and taxes. Split the fraction, \(\frac{T_2 - G_2}{1+i} = \frac{T_2}{1+i} - \frac{G_2}{1+i}\). Then move every spending term and the debt to the left and every tax term to the right:

\[\underbrace{G_1 + \frac{G_2}{1+i}}_{\text{pdv of spending}} + \underbrace{(1+i)B_1}_{\text{initial debt}} = \underbrace{T_1 + \frac{T_2}{1+i}}_{\text{pdv of taxes}}. \tag{3}\]

Dividing an amount of period 2 by \(1+i\) gives its present discounted value (pdv): the sum that, lent today at the rate \(i\), would grow into that amount by period 2 (Appendix C.12). The initial debt carries the factor \(1+i\) because the period-1 interest on it is also due.

Step 5: the same result in budget balances. Move \(G_1\) and \(G_2/(1+i)\) to the right of equation (3) and combine the terms of each period:

\[\underbrace{(T_1 - G_1)}_{\text{period-1 balance}} + \underbrace{\frac{T_2 - G_2}{1+i}}_{\text{period-2 balance}} = \underbrace{(1+i)B_1}_{\text{initial debt}}. \tag{4}\]

Intuition. Equation (3) is “uses equal sources” again, but now each side is measured in present value over the whole life of the economy instead of within one year. Equation (4) says the same thing from the angle of the balances: the government may run a deficit in one period, but then it must run a surplus of equal present value in another. If it starts in debt, the surpluses must be larger still, large enough to pay the debt off. The budget has to balance in present value, not every period.

Step 6: more periods. Nothing in the argument depends on there being two periods. With three periods and \(B_4 = 0\), solve the period-3 constraint for \(B_3 = (T_3 - G_3)/(1+i)\), put it into the period-2 constraint and solve that for \(B_2\):

\[B_2 = \frac{T_2 - G_2}{1+i} + \frac{T_3 - G_3}{(1+i)^2}.\]

Putting this into the period-1 constraint and sorting terms as in step 4 gives

\[G_1 + \frac{G_2}{1+i} + \frac{G_3}{(1+i)^2} + (1+i)B_1 = T_1 + \frac{T_2}{1+i} + \frac{T_3}{(1+i)^2}.\]

Each extra period adds one more term, discounted once more. With \(N\) periods, an amount of period \(t\) is divided by \((1+i)^{t-1}\).

What the model predicts

Solve equation (3) for the period-2 taxes:

\[T_2 = (1+i)\big[(1+i)B_1 + G_1 - T_1\big] + G_2.\]

Holding the other terms fixed, this formula gives the comparative statics:

A one-dollar rise in … changes \(T_2\) by Why
period-1 taxes \(T_1\) (a tax cut is a fall) \(-(1+i)\) a dollar not taxed today is borrowed and repaid with interest
period-1 spending \(G_1\) \(+(1+i)\) the same: it is financed by borrowing
period-2 spending \(G_2\) \(+1\) paid in the same period
the initial debt \(B_1\) \(+(1+i)^2\) it accumulates interest over two periods
  • Borrowing today means higher taxes tomorrow. With \(i = 5\%\), a tax cut of 100 dollars today, with spending unchanged, requires 105 dollars of extra taxes in period 2.
  • The timing of taxes can change, their present value cannot. Once spending and the initial debt are given, equation (3) fixes the pdv of taxes. A tax cut today only moves taxes into the future. If households look ahead and save the tax cut to pay the later taxes, their consumption does not change: this is Ricardian equivalence, from the permanent-income theory of consumption (Chapter 16).
  • Generational accounting. When period 2 is far away, the people who pay the later taxes are not the ones who enjoyed the earlier spending. A growing debt shifts tax burdens toward future generations. That can be fair, for example when a war fought today also protects them, but it is a transfer between generations all the same.

The debt-GDP ratio and sustainability

Definitions

How much a government can borrow depends on how much it can later tax, and the best measure of the tax base is GDP. So we scale every amount by nominal GDP \(Y_t\):

  • the debt-GDP ratio \(b_t \equiv B_{t+1}/Y_t\): the debt at the end of year \(t\) (which is the debt at the start of year \(t+1\)) relative to that year’s GDP. Statistical tables, and the data in Figs. 2 and 4, report the ratio this way;
  • the primary deficit ratio \(d_t \equiv (G_t - T_t)/Y_t\). A negative \(d_t\) is a primary surplus;
  • the growth rate of nominal GDP, \(g\), so that \(Y_t = (1+g)Y_{t-1}\).

The interest rate \(i\) and the growth rate \(g\) are both nominal: both include inflation.

Solving the model

Step 1: divide the flow constraint by GDP. Divide every term of equation (2) by \(Y_t\):

\[\frac{B_{t+1}}{Y_t} = (1+i)\frac{B_t}{Y_t} + \frac{G_t - T_t}{Y_t}.\]

The left side is \(b_t\) and the last term is \(d_t\).

Step 2: rewrite the middle term. \(B_t\) is the debt at the end of year \(t-1\), but it is divided by this year’s GDP. Multiply and divide by \(Y_{t-1}\):

\[\frac{B_t}{Y_t} = \frac{B_t}{Y_{t-1}}\cdot\frac{Y_{t-1}}{Y_t} = b_{t-1}\cdot\frac{1}{1+g}.\]

Step 3: the law of motion of the ratio. Put step 2 into step 1:

\[b_t = \frac{1+i}{1+g}\,b_{t-1} + d_t. \tag{5}\]

This is exact. It is a difference equation: it gives this year’s ratio from last year’s ratio (Appendix C.10).

Step 4: the change in the ratio. Subtract \(b_{t-1}\) from both sides of (5) and use \(\frac{1+i}{1+g} - 1 = \frac{(1+i) - (1+g)}{1+g} = \frac{i-g}{1+g}\):

\[b_t - b_{t-1} = \frac{i-g}{1+g}\,b_{t-1} + d_t \;\approx\; (i-g)\,b_{t-1} + d_t. \tag{6}\]

The approximation drops the division by \(1+g\), which is close to 1 when \(g\) is a few percent (Appendix C.6).

Intuition. Two forces move the ratio. The primary deficit adds new debt. And the old debt is pushed up by interest at the rate \(i\) while GDP, the denominator, grows at \(g\): when \(g > i\) the economy outgrows its debt.

The same result follows from the growth rule for a ratio, \(g_b = g_B - g_Y\) (Appendix C.5). With a balanced primary budget the debt grows at \(i\), so the ratio grows at about \(i - g\). With a balanced total budget the debt is constant, so the ratio shrinks at about the rate \(g\).

Step 5: the primary balance that holds the ratio constant. Set \(b_t = b_{t-1} = b\) in equation (6), using its exact form, and solve for the primary surplus \(-d\):

\[-d = \frac{i-g}{1+g}\,b. \tag{7}\]

When \(i > g\), a country must run a primary surplus just to keep its ratio where it is, and the surplus needed is proportional to the debt it already has. When \(i < g\), it can run a primary deficit forever and still keep the ratio constant.

Step 6: the long-run ratio and the path toward it. Suppose the primary deficit ratio stays at the same value \(d\) every year, and write \(a \equiv (1+i)/(1+g)\). A steady state \(b^*\) is a ratio that reproduces itself, \(b^* = a\,b^* + d\). Collect the \(b^*\) terms, \(b^*(1-a) = d\), and use \(1 - a = \frac{g-i}{1+g}\):

\[b^* = \frac{1+g}{g-i}\,d.\]

Now subtract \(b^* = a b^* + d\) from equation (5). The \(d\) cancels, leaving \(b_t - b^* = a\,(b_{t-1} - b^*)\): each year the gap to \(b^*\) is multiplied by \(a\). After \(t\) years it has been multiplied by \(a^t\):

\[b_t = b^* + \left(\frac{1+i}{1+g}\right)^{t}\,(b_0 - b^*). \tag{8}\]

What the model predicts

Sustainability conditions. Everything depends on whether \(a = (1+i)/(1+g)\) is below or above one, that is, on whether \(i\) is below or above \(g\):

Case What \(a^t\) does What happens to the debt ratio
\(i < g\) shrinks to 0 converges to \(b^*\) from any start. A constant primary deficit gives a finite ratio, even though the dollar debt grows forever.
\(i = g\) stays at 1 \(b_t = b_{t-1} + d_t\): the ratio is the starting ratio plus the sum of all later primary deficits. It is stable only if primary deficits and surpluses cancel.
\(i > g\) grows without limit \(b^*\) is a tipping point, not a resting point. Above it the ratio explodes, below it the ratio falls. Only primary surpluses of at least equation (7) keep the ratio from rising.

With the debt at 99.5 percent of GDP (the U.S. ratio of FY2025), equation (7) asks for no primary surplus at all when \(i = g = 4\%\), the starting values of the projection widget in Fig. 6. With \(i = 5\%\) and \(g = 4\%\) it asks for a primary surplus of \(0.01/1.04 \times 99.5 \approx 0.96\) percent of GDP every year. With \(i = 3\%\) and \(g = 4\%\), a constant primary deficit of 2 percent of GDP would lead to \(b^* = 1.04/0.01 \times 2 = 208\) percent of GDP, reached only very slowly: the gap shrinks by about 1 percent a year, since \(a = 1.03/1.04 \approx 0.990\).

Comparative statics of equation (6). Holding last year’s ratio fixed:

A one-point rise in … changes this year’s \(b_t - b_{t-1}\) by about With \(b_{t-1} = 99.5\%\)
the primary deficit \(d_t\) (% of GDP) \(+1\) point \(+1\) point
the interest rate \(i\) \(+b_{t-1}/100\) points \(+1\) point
nominal GDP growth \(g\) \(-b_{t-1}/100\) points \(-1\) point

At a debt ratio near 100 percent, one point of interest weighs as much as one point of GDP in primary deficit. The higher the debt, the more the ratio reacts to the interest rate and to growth.

Growth is a powerful remedy. The ratio can fall a long way without any repayment, as long as GDP grows faster than the interest rate. The fall of the U.S. ratio after World War II worked mostly like this (Fig. 2).

Inflation, expected and unexpected. Write \(1+i = (1+r)(1+\pi)\) and \(1+g = (1+g_{\text{real}})(1+\pi)\), with \(r\) the real interest rate, \(g_{\text{real}}\) real GDP growth and \(\pi\) inflation (the Fisher equation, Chapter 8). The inflation factor cancels in \(a\):

\[\frac{1+i}{1+g} = \frac{1+r}{1+g_{\text{real}}}.\]

Inflation that lenders expect is already built into \(i\), so it does not reduce the ratio. Inflation that comes as a surprise, after the bonds have been sold at a fixed \(i\), lowers the real rate the government pays and erodes the debt. That is why very high debts raise fears of inflation.

Confidence and default. If lenders start to doubt repayment, they ask for a higher \(i\) on new bonds. A higher \(i\) raises \(a\), which raises the surplus required by equation (7) and can tip a stable ratio into an explosive one. The fear can fulfill itself, ending in high inflation or a default. No single ratio triggers a crisis: it depends on growth prospects, the ability to collect taxes and the credibility of the government.

Saving, investment and crowding out

Definitions

The national income identity of Chapter 2 splits GDP by use: \(Y = C + I + G + EX - IM\). Here \(G\) is government purchases only; transfers are left out to keep the algebra short. With taxes \(T\) we define three kinds of saving:

  • private saving \(Y - T - C\): what households and firms do not consume out of their disposable income, \(Y - T\);
  • government saving \(T - G\): the budget balance;
  • foreign saving \(IM - EX\): when a country buys more from abroad than it sells, foreigners lend it the difference.

Solving the model

Step 1: solve the identity for investment. Subtract \(C\), \(G\) and \(EX - IM\) from both sides of \(Y = C + I + G + EX - IM\):

\[I = Y - C - G + (IM - EX).\]

Step 2: add and subtract taxes. Adding \(-T + T = 0\) changes nothing, but it lets us group the terms by who saves:

\[I = (Y - T - C) + (T - G) + (IM - EX),\]

or, with saving on the left,

\[\underbrace{(Y-T-C)}_{\text{private saving}} + \underbrace{(T-G)}_{\text{government saving}} + \underbrace{(IM-EX)}_{\text{foreign saving}} = I. \tag{9}\]

Intuition. Every machine and building that firms buy must be paid for by someone’s saving: households and firms, the government, or foreigners. A deficit is negative government saving, a claim on the saving of the other two.

What the model predicts

Let the deficit rise by \(\Delta\) dollars, so government saving falls by \(\Delta\). Suppose private saving responds by \(\theta\Delta\) and foreign saving by \(\phi\Delta\). Taking changes of equation (9):

\[\Delta I = \theta\Delta - \Delta + \phi\Delta = -(1 - \theta - \phi)\,\Delta. \tag{10}\]

Response of other saving \(\theta\) \(\phi\) Change in investment
none (full crowding out) 0 0 \(-\Delta\)
Ricardian equivalence: households save the whole tax cut 1 0 \(0\)
private saving offsets half 0.5 0 \(-0.5\Delta\)
half from households, the rest from foreigners 0.5 0.5 \(0\)

The evidence suggests that private saving rises by at most about half of a deficit, so without foreign saving at least half of a deficit would come out of investment. Foreign saving can fill the rest, as Fig. 5 suggests it did in the United States. The foreign side is the topic of Chapter 19.

The arithmetic of rising health spending

Definitions

Long-run budget projections are driven by entitlement programs, above all public health spending. Break health spending into two parts and GDP into two parts:

  • health spending \(H = N_r \times c\): the number of people who receive benefits, \(N_r\), times the cost per recipient, \(c\);
  • GDP \(Y = N \times y\): the population \(N\) times GDP per person \(y\);
  • the health share \(h \equiv H/Y\).

Solving the model

Step 1: the share as a product of two ratios. Divide and regroup:

\[h = \frac{N_r\,c}{N\,y} = \frac{N_r}{N}\times\frac{c}{y}.\]

The share is the fraction of the population that receives benefits, times the cost per recipient relative to income per person.

Step 2: take growth rates. The growth rate of a product is the sum of the growth rates, and that of a ratio is the difference (Appendix C.5):

\[g_h = g_{N_r/N} + (g_c - g_y). \tag{11}\]

What the model predicts

  • Aging raises the share through \(g_{N_r/N}\): fewer workers per retiree means more recipients per person.
  • Costs that outpace income raise it through \(g_c - g_y\). If the cost per recipient grows just 1 point a year faster than income per person, the share grows by about 1 percent a year and doubles in about 70 years (the rule of 70). Add an aging effect of another 1 percent a year and it doubles in about 35 years.
  • The budget link. The primary deficit ratio is non-interest spending minus revenue, both in percent of GDP. If revenue stays near a constant share of GDP while \(h\) keeps rising, \(d_t\) rises year after year and equation (5) pushes the debt ratio up without limit. Taxes must rise or spending must fall.
  • Why the share may keep rising. As people get richer, an extra unit of consumption adds less and less to their well-being (diminishing marginal utility), while an extra year of life to enjoy that consumption does not lose its value in the same way. Standard models therefore predict that health spending should grow faster than income. The problem is then less about waste and more about how to finance spending that people want.

The model in code

We now turn the key results into Python functions. Each function’s description names the equation it computes. The cells after them check the algebra above with numbers.

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. The text in triple quotes is a one-line description. We write the interest rate as a decimal number, so 5 percent is 0.05.

def next_debt(B, G, T, i):
    """Equation (2): next period's debt, (1 + i) B + G - T."""
    return (1 + i) * B + G - T


def primary_deficit(G, T):
    """Primary deficit: spending minus taxes, leaving out the interest payments."""
    return G - T


def total_deficit(G, T, i, B):
    """Total deficit: spending plus the interest on the debt, minus taxes."""
    return G + i * B - T


def pdv(first, second, i):
    """Present discounted value of an amount in period 1 and one in period 2 (equation 3)."""
    return first + second / (1 + i)

Python note. path = [] starts an empty list, and path.append(b) adds one value at its end. For a pandas Series with years as row labels, deficit.loc[1951:1974] keeps the years 1951 to 1974 (both included), and .sum() adds them up.

def next_debt_ratio(b, i, g, d):
    """Equation (5): next year's debt ratio, (1 + i) / (1 + g) b + d."""
    return (1 + i) / (1 + g) * b + d


def debt_ratio_path(b, i, g, deficits):
    """The debt ratio after each year of a list of primary deficits (all in percent of GDP)."""
    path = []
    for d in deficits:
        b = next_debt_ratio(b, i, g, d)
        path.append(b)
    return path


def debt_change_parts(debt, deficit, start, end):
    """Split the change in the debt ratio from start to end into primary deficits and the rest (equation 6)."""
    change = debt[end] - debt[start]
    from_deficits = deficit.loc[start + 1:end].sum()
    return change, from_deficits, change - from_deficits

The part of a change that is not explained by primary deficits, change - from_deficits, is the effect of interest minus growth, the term \(\frac{i-g}{1+g}\,b\) of equation (6), summed over the years. We use it on the U.S. data in Fig. 2. Three more functions give the long-run results of the debt ratio and the saving identity.

def stabilizing_surplus(b, i, g):
    """Equation (7): the primary surplus (% of GDP) that keeps the debt ratio b constant."""
    return (i - g) / (1 + g) * b


def steady_state_ratio(d, i, g):
    """The steady-state debt ratio b* = (1 + g) / (g - i) d for a constant primary deficit d."""
    return (1 + g) / (g - i) * d


def private_and_foreign_saving(investment, government_saving):
    """Equation (9) solved for the saving of households, firms and foreigners: I - (T - G)."""
    return investment - government_saving

Tools for the data

Four small tools help with the figures. period_mean averages a series over some years, and add_period_means draws those averages on a chart. per_person turns a share of GDP into dollars per person. actual_and_projection and one_legend_entry prepare the long-run projections of Figs. 6 and 7, where actual data (solid lines) are followed by projections (dashed lines).

Python note. zip(columns, colors) hands over one (column, color) pair at a time to the loop. fig.add_vrect shades a band between two x values, and fig.add_shape(type="line", ...) draws a straight line between two points.

def period_mean(series, start, end):
    """Average of a Series with years as row labels, from start to end (both included)."""
    return series.loc[start:end].mean()


def add_period_means(fig, data, columns, colors, start, end):
    """Shade the years start-end and draw each column's average over them (Figs. 1 and 5)."""
    fig.add_vrect(x0=start - 0.5, x1=end + 0.5, fillcolor=GRAY, opacity=0.15, line_width=0)
    for column, color in zip(columns, colors):
        mean = period_mean(data[column], start, end)
        fig.add_shape(type="line", x0=start - 0.5, x1=end + 0.5, y0=mean, y1=mean,
                      line=dict(color=color, width=3, dash="dot"))
    return fig


def per_person(percent_of_gdp, gdp_billions, population_millions):
    """Dollars per person of a budget line given in percent of GDP (Table 18.1)."""
    return percent_of_gdp / 100 * gdp_billions * 1e9 / (population_millions * 1e6)

Python note. melt turns a wide table (one column per series) into a long one (one row per year and series), which is the shape Plotly Express likes. .map({0: ..., 1: ...}) replaces each value by its label, .copy() makes an independent copy, and pd.concat([a, b]) stacks two tables. trace.name.rsplit(", ", 1) cuts a text such as "Revenues, actual" at its last comma, into its two parts.

def actual_and_projection(data, names):
    """Long table for Figs. 6 and 7: one row per year and series, actual data and CBO projection.

    FY2025 is repeated in the projection, so that the dashed line starts where the solid line ends."""
    long = data.rename(columns=names).melt(id_vars=["year", "projection"], value_vars=list(names.values()),
                                           var_name="series", value_name="percent")
    long["segment"] = long.projection.map({0: "actual", 1: "CBO projection"})
    joint = long[long.year == 2025].copy()
    joint["segment"] = "CBO projection"
    return pd.concat([long, joint]).sort_values(["series", "segment", "year"])


def one_legend_entry(fig):
    """Plotly Express names each trace 'series, segment': keep one legend entry per series."""
    for trace in fig.data:
        series, segment = trace.name.rsplit(", ", 1)
        trace.update(name=series, legendgroup=series, showlegend=(segment == "actual"))
    return fig

def dropdown_figure(make_figure, options, start):
    """Precompute make_figure(option) for every option and join the results with a dropdown menu.

    figures = [make_figure(o) for o in options]

    for i, f in enumerate(figures):

            owner.append(i)
    buttons = []
    for i, f in enumerate(figures):
        layout = {"title": f.layout.title.to_plotly_json(), "yaxis.range": f.layout.yaxis.range,

Python note. px.colors.qualitative.Dark24 is a list of 24 colors, and + joins two lists. enumerate numbers the items of a list as the loop runs (0, 1, 2, …), and i % len(PALETTE) is the remainder of the division, which starts again at 0 when the colors run out. colors.update(fixed) overwrites some entries of the dictionary. In latest_text, .groupby("country").tail(1) keeps the last row of each country, .iloc[[0, -1]] keeps the first and the last row of a table, and for i, (_, row) in enumerate(...) numbers the rows that .iterrows() hands over one at a time.

PALETTE = px.colors.qualitative.Dark24 + px.colors.qualitative.Light24     # 48 distinct colors


def country_colors(names, fixed):
    """One fixed color per country, so that a country keeps its color when the selection changes."""
    colors = {}
    for i, name in enumerate(sorted(names)):
        colors[name] = PALETTE[i % len(PALETTE)]
    colors.update(fixed)                        # the textbook's countries keep the colors of the book
    return colors


def latest_text(data, column, time):
    """The latest value of each chosen country, highest first; only the maximum and minimum for 3 or more."""
    if len(data) == 0:
        return "choose at least one country"
    last = data.sort_values(time).groupby("country").tail(1).sort_values(column, ascending=False)
    words = ["", ""]
    if len(last) > 2:
        last, words = last.iloc[[0, -1]], ["max ", "min "]
    parts = []
    for i, (_, row) in enumerate(last.iterrows()):
        parts.append(f"{words[i]}{row.country} {row[column]:.1f}")
    return "latest: " + ", ".join(parts)

The axes have two fixed sizes each: up to 150 percent of GDP, or up to 300 percent when a country above 150 percent is chosen; from 1900, or from 1800 when a chosen country’s data start earlier. list(range(50, top + 1, 50)) is the list of tick values 50, 100, … up to top.

def legend_menu(fig, data, shown):
    """Website version of a multi-country chart: the countries in `shown` are drawn, every other country is
    one click away in the legend, and buttons show a group (the textbook's countries, the G7, a region, all).
    Traces that are not countries, such as averages, always stay visible."""
    countries = set(data.country)
    groups = {"Textbook": list(shown), "G7": G7}
    for region in sorted(data.region.unique()):
        groups[region] = list(data[data.region == region].country.unique())
    groups["All"] = list(countries)
    for trace in fig.data:
        if trace.name in countries and trace.name not in shown:
            trace.visible = "legendonly"
    buttons = []
    for label, members in groups.items():
        visible = []
        for trace in fig.data:
            visible.append(True if trace.name not in countries or trace.name in members else "legendonly")
        buttons.append(dict(label=label, method="restyle", args=[{"visible": visible}]))
    fig.update_layout(updatemenus=[dict(type="buttons", direction="right", buttons=buttons, x=0, y=1.02,

                      margin=dict(t=120))

Concluding remarks

  • The U.S. federal government spends about 23 percent of GDP and collects about 17 percent. The deficit, about 6 percent of GDP in recent years, is financed by selling bonds, and the debt held by the public is back near 100 percent of GDP, the level of 1946.
  • The flow budget constraint, equations (1) and (2), links each year’s deficit to the change in the debt. The intertemporal budget constraint, equations (3) and (4), says that the budget must balance in present value: borrowing today means surpluses, and higher taxes, later.
  • What matters is the debt relative to GDP. Its law of motion, equations (5) and (6), shows that when GDP grows faster than the interest rate, the ratio falls even without surpluses: that is how the United States paid down its World War II debt. When the interest rate is above growth, only primary surpluses as large as equation (7) keep the ratio from rising, and the closed form, equation (8), shows the ratio exploding otherwise.
  • The saving-investment identity, equation (9), says that a deficit must be financed by private or foreign saving, or it crowds out investment (equation 10).
  • U.S. government spending is low compared with other rich countries, and its debt ratio is typical. Large deficits have not lowered the U.S. investment rate, because private and foreign saving rose.
  • The fiscal problem of the twenty-first century is the rise of health spending, in the U.S. and in every rich country: by equation (11), aging and costs that grow faster than income both raise its share of GDP. Chapter 19 turns to the international economy, including the foreign saving that has financed much of the U.S. deficit.

Problem 1 (Basic): Who lends to the German government? In 2025 Germany reformed its constitutional debt brake, so that it can borrow more for defense and infrastructure. The IMF’s 2025 Article IV press release on Germany projects the general government balance to fall from −2.4 percent of GDP in 2025 to −3.4 percent in 2026, and the current account surplus to shrink from 4.5 to 4.0 percent of GDP. Foreign saving is minus the current account balance: a country with a surplus lends to the rest of the world.

  1. In our data (gov_finance.csv, from the OECD national accounts via the OECD Data Explorer, the file behind the country menus of Figs. 1 and 5), Germany’s private investment was 17.6 percent of GDP in 2024 and its budget balance −2.6 percent. The same IMF release puts the 2024 current account surplus at 5.8 percent of GDP. Use equation (9) to compute German private saving in 2024.

  2. Suppose the 2026 projections come true and private saving stays at the same share of GDP. By how much does private investment change from 2025 to 2026? Which values of \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) in equation (10) describe this case?

  3. What would private saving have to do for investment not to fall at all?

Hint

In equation (9), government saving is the budget balance \(T - G\) and foreign saving is \(IM - EX\), which is minus the current account. For (b), take the change of every term of equation (9) from 2025 to 2026: the deficit grows by one point of GDP, and a surplus that shrinks by half a point means half a point more foreign saving.

Your answer:

Solution

(a) Solve equation (9) for private saving:

\[Y - T - C = I - (T - G) - (IM - EX) = 17.6 - (-2.6) - (-5.8) = 26.0 \text{ percent of GDP}.\]

German households and firms saved 26.0 percent of GDP. Only 17.6 points financed private investment at home: 2.6 points went to the government’s deficit and 5.8 points were lent abroad. (The budget balance in our file is net lending, which already subtracts the government’s own investment. That is why it pairs with private investment in the identity.)

(b) Take changes of equation (9). Private saving does not change, government saving falls by 1.0 point (\(-3.4 - (-2.4)\)) and foreign saving rises by 0.5 point (the surplus falls from 4.5 to 4.0):

\[\Delta I = 0 + (-1.0) + 0.5 = -0.5 \text{ point of GDP}.\]

This is equation (10) with \(\Delta = 1\), \(\theta = 0\) and \(\phi = 0.5\): \(\Delta I = -(1 - 0 - 0.5) \times 1 = -0.5\). Half of the extra deficit is financed by lending less abroad, and the other half crowds out private investment.

(c) Investment does not fall when \(\theta + \phi = 1\), so with \(\phi = 0.5\) private saving must rise by \(\theta\Delta = 0.5\) point of GDP. That happens if households save part of their higher incomes, or save for the higher taxes they expect later (Ricardian equivalence).

Interpretation. Because Germany starts with a large current account surplus, much of its new borrowing can come from saving that now goes abroad, so crowding out can be small. Equation (9) is an identity, though: it says how the deficit must be financed, not why. The projected fall in the surplus could also come from higher investment rather than from the deficit.

Write both rates as decimals: \(i = 0.1575\) and \(g = -0.090\). A falling GDP makes \(i - g\) larger than \(i\). For (c), follow a rise in \(i\) through equation (7), and ask what a larger required surplus does to the lenders’ belief that they will be repaid.

Your answer:

Solution

(a) Equation (7) with \(i = 0.1575\), \(g = -0.090\) and \(b = 147.8\):

\[-d = \frac{i-g}{1+g}\,b = \frac{0.1575 - (-0.090)}{1 - 0.090} \times 147.8 = \frac{0.2475}{0.910} \times 147.8 = 0.272 \times 147.8 = 40.2 \text{ percent of GDP}.\]

With revenue of 44.6 percent of GDP, a primary surplus of 40.2 percent would have left only 4.4 percent of GDP for all spending other than interest: no schools, no pensions, no police. No government can do that.

(b) With \(i = 0\):

\[-d = \frac{0 - (-0.090)}{1 - 0.090} \times 147.8 = \frac{0.090}{0.910} \times 147.8 = 14.6 \text{ percent of GDP}.\]

Even free borrowing would have required a surplus of 14.6 percent of GDP, because a shrinking GDP raises the ratio by itself: the denominator falls. In equation (7), a fall in \(g\) weighs as much as a rise in \(i\). Growth is the “magic elixir” of Fig. 2 in reverse.

(c) When lenders start to doubt repayment, they ask for a higher \(i\). By equation (7), a higher \(i\) raises the surplus needed to stop the ratio from rising. Raising that surplus means tax increases and spending cuts, which lower GDP in the short run, so \(g\) falls as well. The required surplus grows again, repayment looks even less likely, and lenders ask for a still higher rate. The fear fulfills itself: at a yield of 15.75 percent and a shrinking GDP, no feasible primary surplus could stabilize the ratio. The way out is to lower \(i\) or the debt itself, by a restructuring (a partial default, as in 2012) or by borrowing from official lenders instead of the market. The ratio fell from 175.1 to 164.3 percent in 2012 even though GDP kept falling, because the restructuring cut the face value of the debt.

One detail: only new bonds pay the market yield, while old bonds keep the rate at which they were sold. The average rate on the whole debt was therefore much lower than 15.75 percent, and (a) is an upper bound. That is also why the ratio rose by 27.2 points in 2011, primary deficit included, and not by the 40.2 points that interest minus growth alone would have added at the market yield.

Hint

For (a), look at the sign of the right side of equation (4) when \(B_1 < 0\). Your answer:

Solution

(a) With \(B_1 < 0\), equation (4) reads

\[(T_1 - G_1) + \frac{T_2 - G_2}{1+i} = (1+i)B_1 < 0.\]

The present value of the budget balances is negative: the government may run primary deficits, as long as their present value equals its assets plus the interest on them, \((1+i)\lvert B_1\rvert\). With many periods it can do so forever: a deficit equal to the return on the fund leaves the fund intact.

(b) The first-year deficit is the real return on the fund, \(0.03 \times 413.8 = 12.4\) percent of GDP. The fund’s real value does not change, but real GDP grows, so the ratio shrinks by the growth rule for a ratio, \(g_b = 0 - 1.5\%\):

\[\frac{413.8}{1.015^{30}} = 264.7 \text{ percent of GDP after 30 years},\]

and the deficit the rule then allows is \(0.03 \times 264.7 = 7.9\) percent of GDP.

(c) Equation (7) in real terms, with \(b = -413.8\):

\[-d = \frac{r - g}{1+g}\,b = \frac{0.03 - 0.015}{1.015} \times (-413.8) = -6.1, \qquad\text{so } d = 6.1 \text{ percent of GDP}.\]

A permanent primary deficit of 6.1 percent of GDP keeps the assets at 413.8 percent of GDP. Check with the steady state: \(b^* = \frac{1+g}{g-r}\,d = \frac{1.015}{-0.015} \times 6.115 = -413.8\).

(d) Measured against the same year’s GDP, the 3 percent rule’s first deficit is \(12.4/1.015 = 12.2\) percent, exactly twice the 6.1 percent of (c), since \(r/(r-g) = 0.03/0.015 = 2\). Its deficit shrinks with the ratio, at the rate of real growth, so it falls to the level of (c) when GDP has doubled. By the rule of 70 that takes about \(70/1.5 \approx 47\) years (\(\ln 2 / \ln 1.015 = 46.6\) exactly).

  • The 3 percent rule gives every generation the same real amount (in kroner of today’s value). The generations living in the next half century get more than under the rule of (c), and those after them less, as a share of their (larger) incomes.
  • The constant-ratio rule gives every generation the same share of GDP. It spends less today and lets the fund grow with the economy.

Future generations will be richer, which is an argument for spending more now (the 3 percent rule). Expected returns are uncertain, which is an argument for spending less (if the real return turns out lower than 3 percent, the 3 percent rule eats into the fund). The arithmetic of equations (4) and (7) shows the trade-off; choosing between the two rules is a value judgment. In reality the fund has kept growing, from −154.9 percent of GDP in 2011 to −413.8 percent in 2024 in our data, because oil revenue still flows in and asset prices rose.
