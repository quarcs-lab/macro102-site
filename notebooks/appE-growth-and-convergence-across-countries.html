Learning goals

Do poor countries grow faster than rich ones and catch up? The Solow model says they should, at least when they share the same steady state. This appendix tests that prediction on 84 countries from 1960 to 2019. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • derive the speed of convergence \(\beta=(1-\alpha)(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\) and the half-life \(\ln 2/\beta\) from the Solow model, and turn the model into a regression of growth on initial income;
  • tell β-convergence (poor countries grow faster) from σ-convergence (incomes become more equal), and explain why the first does not guarantee the second;
  • estimate β-convergence by OLS with robust standard errors, convert the slope into a speed and a half-life, and estimate the speed directly by nonlinear least squares (NLS);
  • check whether the answer depends on the period, with a structural break, rolling windows and a heatmap of every start and end year;
  • measure σ-convergence with the variance of log income and its confidence interval;
  • do all of this in Python with pandas, statsmodels, SciPy and interactive Plotly charts.

E.2 Why convergence?

In the Solow model of Chapter 5, an economy that starts below its steady state grows fast, and its growth slows down as it gets closer. This is the principle of transition dynamics: the further below its steady state an economy is, the faster it grows. Two economies with the same saving rate, population growth, depreciation and technology have the same steady state, so the poorer one should grow faster and catch up. Chapter 5 found this pattern among OECD countries, but not in a large sample of countries around the world.

This appendix turns that picture into numbers. It asks four questions:

  1. Do poor countries grow faster? Regress growth on initial income: a negative slope is β-convergence.
  2. How fast do they catch up? Convert the slope into a speed of convergence β, the share of the gap to the steady state that closes each year, and into a half-life, the number of years it takes to close half of the gap.
  3. Does the answer depend on the period? Split the sample in 2000, roll the start year forward, and look at every pair of start and end years.
  4. Are incomes becoming more equal? Track the cross-country variance of log income: a falling variance is σ-convergence.

E.3 From the Solow model to a convergence regression

This section derives, step by step, the equation that the rest of the appendix estimates. We use the Solow model with population growth and technological progress, in continuous time. Bars mark parameters, as in the chapters.

E.3.1 Assumptions

  • Production is Cobb–Douglas with labor-augmenting technology: \(Y=K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha}\), with capital share \(0<\alpha<1\). Chapters 4 and 5 use \(\alpha=1/3\).
  • Workers grow at rate \(\bar{n}\) and technology at rate \(\bar{g}\): \(\dot{L}/L=\bar{n}\) and \(\dot{A}/A=\bar{g}\). A dot over a variable is its change per unit of time (Appendix C.11).
  • A constant share \(\bar{s}\) of output is invested and capital depreciates at rate \(\bar{d}\): \(\dot{K}=\bar{s}Y-\bar{d}K\).

Because output per worker grows forever when technology grows, we measure everything per effective worker, \(AL\): \(\hat{k}\equiv K/(AL)\) and \(\hat{y}\equiv Y/(AL)\). Dividing the production function by \(AL\) gives \(\hat{y}=\hat{k}^{\alpha}\).

E.3.2 Solving the model

Step 1: the law of motion. The growth rate of a ratio is the growth rate of the numerator minus that of the denominator (Appendix C.5):

\[\frac{\dot{\hat{k}}}{\hat{k}}=\frac{\dot{K}}{K}-\bar{n}-\bar{g}.\]

The growth rate of capital is \(\dot{K}/K=\bar{s}Y/K-\bar{d}\), and \(Y/K=\hat{y}/\hat{k}=\hat{k}^{\alpha-1}\). So

\[\frac{\dot{\hat{k}}}{\hat{k}}=\bar{s}\,\hat{k}^{\alpha-1}-(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}). \tag{1}\]

Investment per effective worker pushes \(\hat{k}\) up. Depreciation, new workers and better technology all spread the capital stock more thinly and push it down. When the two forces are equal, \(\hat{k}\) stops changing. That is the steady state:

\[\hat{k}^*=\left(\frac{\bar{s}}{\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)},\qquad \hat{y}^*=(\hat{k}^*)^{\alpha}.\]

Step 2: logs. Write \(x\equiv\ln\hat{k}\). The growth rate of \(\hat{k}\) is the time derivative of its log, \(\dot{x}=\dot{\hat{k}}/\hat{k}\), and \(\hat{k}^{\alpha-1}=e^{-(1-\alpha)x}\). Equation (1) becomes

\[\dot{x}=f(x)\equiv\bar{s}\,e^{-(1-\alpha)x}-(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}).\]

Step 3: a straight-line approximation near the steady state. Close to \(x^*=\ln\hat{k}^*\), replace \(f\) by its tangent: \(f(x)\approx f(x^*)+f'(x^*)(x-x^*)\). At the steady state \(f(x^*)=0\), which also tells us that \(\bar{s}\,e^{-(1-\alpha)x^*}=\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}\). The derivative is

\[f'(x^*)=-(1-\alpha)\,\bar{s}\,e^{-(1-\alpha)x^*}=-(1-\alpha)(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}).\]

So \(\dot{x}\approx-\beta\,(x-x^*)\), with the speed of convergence

\[\beta\equiv(1-\alpha)(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}). \tag{2}\]

A note on notation. We follow Barro and Sala-i-Martin: β is the speed and, below, λ is the regression slope. Appendix F, which follows Mankiw, Romer and Weil (1992), writes the speed as λ, because there β is the share of human capital.

Step 4: from capital to output. Since \(\ln\hat{y}=\alpha x\), the log gap of output is \(\alpha\) times the log gap of capital, and it shrinks at the same speed: \(\frac{d}{dt}\left(\ln\hat{y}-\ln\hat{y}^*\right)=-\beta\left(\ln\hat{y}-\ln\hat{y}^*\right)\).

Step 5: solve the differential equation. A variable whose change is \(-\beta\) times its level decays exponentially (Appendix C.11):

\[\ln\hat{y}(t)-\ln\hat{y}^*=e^{-\beta t}\left[\ln\hat{y}(0)-\ln\hat{y}^*\right]. \tag{3}\]

Each year, a share β of the remaining log gap closes. Half of the gap is gone when \(e^{-\beta t}=1/2\), so the half-life is

\[t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln 2}{\beta}. \tag{4}\]

This is the continuous-time cousin of the half-life of a difference equation in Appendix C.10.

Step 6: back to output per worker. The data measure output per person, \(y=Y/L=A\hat{y}\), so \(\ln y(t)=\ln A(0)+\bar{g}t+\ln\hat{y}(t)\). Growth over \(T\) years is

\[\ln y(T)-\ln y(0)=\bar{g}T+\ln\hat{y}(T)-\ln\hat{y}(0)=\bar{g}T+\left(1-e^{-\beta T}\right)\left[\ln\hat{y}^*-\ln\hat{y}(0)\right],\]

where the second step uses equation (3) at \(t=T\). Now replace \(\ln\hat{y}(0)\) by \(\ln y(0)-\ln A(0)\) and divide by \(T\):

\[\frac{1}{T}\ln\frac{y(T)}{y(0)}=a-\frac{1-e^{-\beta T}}{T}\,\ln y(0),\qquad a\equiv\bar{g}+\frac{1-e^{-\beta T}}{T}\left[\ln\hat{y}^*+\ln A(0)\right]. \tag{5}\]

This is the Barro–Sala-i-Martin equation. Average annual growth falls with log initial income, and the slope depends only on the speed β and the length of the period \(T\).

E.3.3 What the model predicts

A regression. For a cross-section of countries \(i\), equation (5) becomes a linear regression of growth on log initial income,

\[g_i\equiv\frac{1}{T}\ln\frac{y_{i,T}}{y_{i,0}}=a+\lambda\,\ln y_{i,0}+\varepsilon_i,\qquad \lambda=-\frac{1-e^{-\beta T}}{T}. \tag{6}\]

A positive speed β gives a negative slope λ. Solving the second equation for β turns an estimated slope into a speed:

\[\beta=-\frac{\ln(1+\lambda T)}{T},\qquad\text{which needs } 1+\lambda T>0. \tag{7}\]

For short periods, \(\lambda\approx-\beta\). Over long periods λ is smaller in absolute value than β, because most of the gap has already closed. With β = 0.02 and \(T=59\), λ = −0.0117.

Absolute or conditional? The intercept \(a\) holds the steady state \(\hat{y}^*\), the technology level \(A(0)\) and its growth rate \(\bar{g}\). A single regression line fits all countries only if these are the same everywhere. Then the model predicts absolute (unconditional) convergence, and this is what we test. If countries have different steady states, each converges to its own. That is conditional convergence, which needs controls for the steady state in the regression.

How fast? With \(\alpha=1/3\), \(\bar{n}=0.01\), \(\bar{g}=0.02\) and \(\bar{d}=0.05\), equation (2) gives β = (2/3)(0.08) ≈ 0.053: the gap closes by about 5.3 percent a year, with a half-life of about 13 years. Studies of conditional convergence usually find a speed near 2 percent a year, a half-life of about 35 years. The model matches this only with a much larger capital share, near \(\alpha=0.75\). One reading is that “capital” should include human capital, which raises the share of output paid to accumulated factors.

E.3.4 The model in code

speed_of_convergence computes equation (2). solow_gap_path simulates the exact, nonlinear law of motion, equation (1), with small time steps, so we can see how good the straight-line approximation is.

Python note. A for loop with a small step dt approximates a differential equation: each step adds the change per unit of time times dt. This is Euler’s method. np.zeros(n) makes an array of n zeros that the loop fills in.

def speed_of_convergence(alpha, n, g, d):
    """Equation (2): beta = (1 - alpha)(n + g + d)."""
    return (1 - alpha) * (n + g + d)


def solow_gap_path(alpha=1/3, n=0.01, g=0.02, d=0.05, s=0.2, start=0.75, years=150, dt=0.05):
    """Simulate equation (1) from y-hat = start * y-hat*; return the years and the log gap ln(y-hat*/y-hat)."""
    k_star = (s / (n + g + d)) ** (1 / (1 - alpha))
    k = k_star * start ** (1 / alpha)            # so that y-hat(0) = start * y-hat*
    steps = int(years / dt)
    t = np.zeros(steps + 1)
    gap = np.zeros(steps + 1)
    for i in range(steps + 1):
        t[i] = i * dt
        gap[i] = alpha * (math.log(k_star) - math.log(k))
        k = k + dt * (s * k ** alpha - (n + g + d) * k)
    return t, gap

Check the algebra with numbers. Start an economy at three-quarters of its steady-state output per effective worker. The approximation predicts a half-life of \(\ln 2/\beta\). The simulation tells us when the gap really halves.

E.5 β-convergence: do poor countries grow faster?

We now estimate equation (6) for 1960–2019. Each country gives one point: log GDP per person in 1960 on the horizontal axis and its average annual growth from 1960 to 2019 on the vertical axis.

cross_section builds this table for any start and end year, and beta_ols runs the regression. We use robust standard errors (HC1), which stay valid when the spread of the errors differs across countries.

Python note. sm.add_constant(x) adds a column of ones, so the regression has an intercept \(a\). sm.OLS(y, X).fit(cov_type="HC1", use_t=True) runs ordinary least squares with robust standard errors and \(t\)-based p-values. def to_speed(lam, T):
    """Equation (7): the speed beta implied by the slope lam over T years."""
    if 1 + lam * T <= 0:
        return np.nan
    return -math.log(1 + lam * T) / T


def half_life(beta):
    """Equation (4): years to close half of the gap (only for convergence, beta > 0)."""
    return math.log(2) / beta if beta > 0 else np.nan
def beta_ols(start, end):

E.8 Estimating the speed directly: nonlinear least squares

Equation (6) is linear in λ but nonlinear in β: β sits inside an exponential. OLS can only estimate λ, which is why we converted. Nonlinear least squares (NLS) estimates β directly. It searches for the values of \(a\) and β that make the sum of squared errors as small as possible:

\[\min_{a,\beta}\ \sum_i\left[g_i-a+\frac{1-e^{-\beta T}}{T}\ln y_{i,0}\right]^2.\]

There is no formula for the answer, so the computer finds it step by step, starting from a first guess. SciPy’s optimize.curve_fit does this.

Robust standard errors for NLS. Near the solution, NLS behaves like OLS on the derivatives of the model with respect to its parameters. We collect them in a matrix \(J\) (one row per country, one column per parameter) and apply the same robust “sandwich” formula that statsmodels uses for HC1: \((J'J)^{-1}\,J'\,\text{diag}(e_i^2)\,J\,(J'J)^{-1}\) times \(N/(N-2)\), where \(e_i\) are the residuals.

Python note. A function can be defined inside another function. Here bsm (for Barro–Sala-i-Martin) needs T, which it takes from the outer function. optimize.curve_fit(f, x, y, p0=...) returns the best parameters and a covariance matrix, starting from the guess p0. In NumPy, @ multiplies matrices, .T transposes, and np.linalg.inv inverts.

def beta_nls(start, end):
    """Estimate the speed beta directly by NLS (the slope formula of equation (6)), with robust errors."""
    table = cross_section(start, end)
    x, growth = table["log_y0"].values, table["growth"].values
    T = end - start

    def bsm(x, a, beta):
        """Average growth predicted by equation (6)."""
        return a - (1 - np.exp(-beta * T)) / T * x

    (a, beta), _ = optimize.curve_fit(bsm, x, growth, p0=[0.0, 0.0])
    resid = growth - bsm(x, a, beta)
    J = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), -x * np.exp(-beta * T)])   # d(bsm)/da and d(bsm)/d(beta)
    bread = np.linalg.inv(J.T @ J)
    meat = (J * resid[:, None] ** 2).T @ J
    N = len(x)
    se = math.sqrt((bread @ meat @ bread)[1, 1] * N / (N - 2))
    t_crit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, N - 2)
    p = 2 * stats.t.sf(abs(beta / se), N - 2)
    return {"beta": beta, "se": se, "p": p, "beta_lo": beta - t_crit * se, "beta_hi": beta + t_crit * se}

E.10 σ-convergence: are incomes becoming more equal?

β-convergence asks whether poor countries grow faster. σ-convergence asks a different question: is the spread of incomes across countries shrinking? We measure the spread with the sample variance of log GDP per person across the \(N\) countries in year \(t\):

\[\sigma_t^2=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\ln y_{i,t}-\overline{\ln y_t}\right)^2. \tag{8}\]

Logs make the measure independent of units and of the overall level of income: if every country became twice as rich, the variance would not change.

How precise is it? If log incomes are roughly normal, \((N-1)\sigma_t^2\) divided by the true variance follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(N-1\) degrees of freedom. This gives a 95% confidence interval:

\[\left[\frac{(N-1)\,\sigma_t^2}{\chi^2_{0.975,\,N-1}},\ \frac{(N-1)\,\sigma_t^2}{\chi^2_{0.025,\,N-1}}\right]. \tag{9}\]

def variance_ci(values):
    """Equations (8) and (9): sample variance of log income and its 95% chi-squared interval."""
    logs = np.log(values)
    N = len(logs)
    v = logs.var()                               # pandas divides by N - 1
    low = (N - 1) * v / stats.chi2.ppf(0.975, N - 1)
    high = (N - 1) * v / stats.chi2.ppf(0.025, N - 1)
    return v, low, high

E.11 Why β-convergence is not enough

Since 2000, poor countries have grown faster, so why has the variance fallen so little? Because β-convergence is necessary for σ-convergence, but not sufficient. Write equation (6) as a statement about levels: \(\ln y_{i,T}=\ln y_{i,0}+T g_i=Ta+(1+\lambda T)\ln y_{i,0}+T\varepsilon_i\). OLS residuals are uncorrelated with the regressor, so the variance across countries splits into two parts:

\[\sigma_T^2=(1+\lambda T)^2\,\sigma_0^2+T^2\,\text{Var}(\varepsilon). \tag{10}\]

The first term shrinks the spread when λ < 0: catch-up pulls countries together. The second term spreads them apart: new country-specific shocks (wars, crises, booms, good or bad policies) keep pushing countries away from the line. The variance falls only if catch-up is strong enough to beat the new shocks.

Check equation (10) with the data, for 2000–2019.

E.14 Discussion and summary

What we found.

  • No absolute convergence over 1960–2019. The slope of growth on initial income is about zero. Poor countries as a group did not catch up.
  • Two eras. From 1960 to 2000 incomes diverged: richer countries grew faster. From 2000 on, poorer countries grew faster, a shift that rolling windows and heatmaps confirm from every angle.
  • Slow convergence. Even in the recent era, the speed is about 0.4 percent a year, a half-life of almost two centuries. That is far below the 2 percent of conditional-convergence studies, and the more than 5 percent of the Solow model with α = 1/3.
  • OLS and NLS agree. The two routes to β give the same estimate, because equation (7) is an exact transformation.
  • σ-divergence, then a pause. The variance of log income nearly doubled up to 2006 and has fallen only about 8 percent since. β-convergence is necessary for σ-convergence, but new shocks can offset it, as equation (10) shows.

What this means for the Solow model. The model does not predict that all countries converge. It predicts that each country converges to its own steady state, set by its investment rate, population growth and technology (Chapter 5 and Chapter 6). Absolute convergence is a test of the extra assumption that steady states are the same everywhere. The data reject that assumption for 1960–2000.

Limitations and next steps.

  • Conditional convergence. Adding controls for the steady state (investment, schooling, population growth, institutions) usually turns the slope negative; the Your-turn task in E.10 is a first step.
  • Sample selection. The balanced panel drops 40 countries whose data start after 1960, many of them poor. Results for an unbalanced or larger sample can differ.
  • Convergence clubs. Groups of countries may converge to different steady states: rich countries to one, poor countries to another. Club-convergence methods look for these groups in the data.
  • Galton’s fallacy. A negative slope can arise from regression toward the mean, even when the distribution is not narrowing. This is why σ-convergence must be checked separately (Quah 1993).

E.14.1 Common errors and how to fix them

Problem Why it happens Fix
The slope is positive, but you expected convergence A positive λ means divergence in equation (6) Convergence means λ < 0 and β > 0
to_speed returns nan \(1+\lambda T\le 0\): the slope is too negative for its period length Check the period length; this happens only with extreme slopes
The half-life is nan There is no half-life under divergence (β ≤ 0) Report the speed and say “divergence”
The β confidence interval is upside down The bounds of λ map to the opposite bounds of β Take beta_lo from lam_hi, as beta_ols does
Different p-values from another program Robust standard errors come in several versions (HC0 to HC3) Use cov_type="HC1" and use_t=True, the common default
curve_fit warns or fails A poor starting guess or a flat sum of squares Start from p0=[0.0, 0.0] or from the OLS-route β
Growth computed in percent and in decimals mixed λ and β depend on the units of growth Keep growth in decimals (0.02 = 2 percent)
KeyError: 2020 The data end in 2019 Use years from 1960 to 2019

E.15 Concluding remarks

  • The Solow model predicts that an economy closes a share \(\beta=(1-\alpha)(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\) of its log gap to the steady state each year, equation (2), with a half-life of \(\ln 2/\beta\), equation (4). With α = 1/3 this is fast: a half-life of about 13 years.
  • The Barro–Sala-i-Martin equation (5) turns the prediction into a regression of growth on log initial income, and equation (7) converts the slope into a speed. NLS estimates the speed directly and gives the same answer.
  • In 84 countries, there was no absolute convergence over 1960–2019: divergence until 2000, and slow convergence, about 0.4 percent a year, since then.
  • The variance of log income, equation (8), nearly doubled until 2006 and has barely fallen since. Equation (10) explains why β-convergence does not guarantee σ-convergence.
  • Countries do not share one steady state. Growth policy is about raising a country’s own steady state (its investment, education, technology and institutions), which is the subject of Chapters 4 to 6.
