At today’s pace, closing half of the income gaps between countries would take about two centuries

190 years The half-life of the income gaps across 84 countries, at the speed of convergence measured for 2000–2019

Open with the number. It comes from the appendix notebook (Section G.6): since 2000, poorer countries have grown faster than richer ones, but only fast enough to close about 0.36 percent of the log gap each year. At that speed, half of the gap closes in about 190 years.

Ask the room: the Solow model of Chapter 5 says poor countries should catch up. Do you think they have? How fast? Collect a few guesses: most students expect either “yes, quickly” (China, Korea) or “no, never” (Sub-Saharan Africa). The data say: not at all for most of the period, and very slowly since 2000.

How the deck fits the book: Appendix G is the empirical companion of Chapter 5’s transition dynamics. It uses the Penn World Table and two simple tools, a regression and a variance, to test the model’s catch-up prediction.

Three questions for today

  • I. The Solow prediction: why should poor countries grow faster, and how fast should they catch up?
  • II. β-convergence in 84 countries: did poorer countries actually grow faster from 1960 to 2019?
  • III. σ-convergence and lessons: have incomes across countries become more equal, and what does that mean for the model?

The three acts mirror the mind map on the title slide.

Act I builds the prediction from the Solow model: a speed of convergence and a half-life, then a regression that tests them. Act II runs that regression on 84 countries from 1960 to 2019, and asks whether the answer depends on the period. Act III looks at a second definition of convergence, the spread of incomes, and explains why the two definitions can disagree.

Two words to define now. β-convergence: poorer countries grow faster (a negative slope of growth on initial income). σ-convergence: the dispersion of incomes across countries shrinks over time.

The Solow prediction

Act I

The further below its steady state, the faster an economy grows

  • In the Solow model, capital is very productive when it is scarce: each new machine adds a lot of output.
  • So an economy far below its steady state invests much more than it loses to depreciation, and grows fast.
  • As it approaches the steady state, diminishing returns slow growth down: the principle of transition dynamics of Chapter 5.
  • Two economies with the same steady state: the poorer one grows faster and catches up.

Catch-up is a prediction about economies that share a steady state, not about all countries.

Recall Chapter 5: the growth rate of capital per person is larger the further the capital stock is below its steady-state value. In Chapter 5’s notation, g_k = d̄[(k*/k)^(2/3) − 1]: when k is far below k*, the bracket is large.

Use a picture: a ball rolling down a slope toward a valley floor (the mind map’s sketch). It moves fast on the steep part and slows near the floor.

The takeaway card is the key qualifier of the whole deck: the model predicts catch-up among economies with the same saving rate, population growth, depreciation and technology. Keep it in mind for Act III.

The model pins down how fast the gap closes

\[\frac{d\ln\widehat{k}}{dt} = \bar{s}\,\widehat{k}^{\,\alpha-1} - (\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\]

\[\beta = (1-\alpha)(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}) \qquad\qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\beta}\]

Each year, a share β of the remaining log gap to the steady state closes.

The full derivation is in Section G.2 of the appendix. Walk through it in three steps.

  1. The law of motion for capital per effective worker, k̂ = K/(AL), with Cobb–Douglas production Y = K^α (AL)^(1−α), population growth n̄, technology growth ḡ, saving s̄ and depreciation d̄.
  2. Take logs and draw the tangent at the steady state (a first-order approximation). The slope of the tangent is −(1−α)(n̄+ḡ+d̄). That number is the speed of convergence β.
  3. The log gap then decays exponentially: ln ŷ(t) − ln ŷ* = e^(−βt) [ln ŷ(0) − ln ŷ*]. Half of the gap is gone when e^(−βt) = 1/2, so the half-life is ln 2 / β.

Notation: β is the Barro–Sala-i-Martin speed. Appendix H, which follows Mankiw, Romer and Weil, calls the speed λ, because β there is human capital’s share.

Point at: the exact path lies a little below the approximation. Away from the steady state, convergence is faster than the linear formula says.

The model’s answer: half of the gap closes in about 13 years

  • With \(\alpha = 1/3\): \(\beta = \tfrac23(0.08) \approx\) 5.3% a year, half-life about 13 years.
  • The exact economy is even faster: starting at three-quarters of its steady state, it halves its gap in about 10.5 years.
  • A capital share of 0.75 gives the 2% a year found in many studies, a half-life of 35 years.

In the Solow model with standard numbers, convergence is fast: a matter of decades, not centuries.

Answer to the prediction: 13 years.

The numbers are from the notebook’s check in Section G.2: β = 0.0533 (5.3 percent a year), half-life from the formula 13.0 years, half-life in the simulation 10.5 years.

Why might α be larger than 1/3? One reading (Mankiw, Romer and Weil 1992): “capital” should include human capital, so the share of output paid to all accumulated factors is closer to 2/3 or more. Appendix H takes up that model.

Keep the 13 years in mind: the data in Act II will give a half-life more than ten times longer.

Diminishing returns and dilution set the speed; the saving rate does not

A rise in … speed \(\beta\) why
capital share \(\alpha\) ↓ returns diminish more slowly
population growth \(\bar{n}\) ↑ capital is diluted faster
technology growth \(\bar{g}\) ↑ capital is diluted faster
depreciation \(\bar{d}\) ↑ capital wears out faster
saving rate \(\bar{s}\) — moves the steady state only

A faster speed means a shorter half-life, \(\ln 2/\beta\).

The saving rate decides where an economy converges to, not how fast it gets there.

All rows follow from β = (1 − α)(n̄ + ḡ + d̄). The saving rate is not in the formula: it changes the steady state k̂* but not the slope of the law of motion at the steady state, once we evaluate it there.

Ask: a country that doubles its saving rate. Does it catch up faster? No: it moves to a higher steady state, and it grows faster for a while because it is now further below its new steady state, but the share of the gap that closes each year is the same.

One regression turns the prediction into a test

\[g_i \equiv \frac{1}{T}\ln\frac{y_{i,T}}{y_{i,0}} = a + \lambda \ln y_{i,0} + \varepsilon_i\]

\[\lambda = -\frac{1-e^{-\beta T}}{T} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \beta = -\frac{\ln(1+\lambda T)}{T}\]

A negative slope λ is β-convergence; the formula turns the slope into a speed per year.

This is the Barro–Sala-i-Martin equation (Section G.2, equations 5 to 7 of the appendix). Average annual growth of country i over T years is regressed on the log of its initial income.

The slope λ depends on the length of the period: over long periods most of the gap has closed, so λ is smaller in absolute value than β. Example from the appendix: with β = 0.02 and T = 59 years, λ = −0.0117. That is why we convert slopes into speeds before comparing periods of different lengths.

The conversion needs 1 + λT > 0. Under divergence (λ > 0) the speed is negative, and there is no half-life.

One line for all countries assumes that all countries share one steady state

If the regression has the same intercept \(a\) for every country, poor countries should catch up with rich ones: absolute convergence.

The intercept holds the steady state, the technology level and its growth rate. If countries differ in these, each converges to its own steady state: conditional convergence.

This slide sets up the interpretation of everything in Acts II and III.

Absolute (unconditional) convergence: one regression line for all countries, i.e. one steady state. That is what we test in this appendix, following the tutorial.

Conditional convergence: add controls for each country’s steady state (investment rate, schooling, population growth, institutions). The Solow model predicts conditional convergence, not absolute convergence. The appendix’s Your-turn task in Section G.10 adds population growth as a first control.

β-convergence in 84 countries

Act II

The test uses one balanced panel: 84 countries observed every year from 1960 to 2019

Rule Why

no oil producers their income depends on oil, not on capital accumulation

A balanced panel keeps the sample fixed, at the cost of 40 countries whose data start later.

The sample follows the tutorial the appendix is based on (Mendez, Beta and Sigma Convergence Across Countries: A Stata Tutorial). It has 84 countries × 60 years = 5,040 observations.

The 40 countries dropped for lack of 1960 data are mostly in Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union, and in Africa. Many are poor, so this is a sample-selection caveat: results for a larger, unbalanced sample can differ.

Why logs? Income per person is very skewed (mean about 11,951 dollars, median about 5,197 in this panel). The log of income has a nearly symmetric distribution (skewness 0.12).

How fast is the recent catch-up?

Since 2000 poor countries have grown faster. How long would it take, at that pace, to close half of the income gap?

Remind the room of the Solow answer from Act I: about 13 years with α = 1/3, 35 years at the 2 percent benchmark.

The measured speeds are far below the 2 percent benchmark

Data: Penn World Table 10.0 (Feenstra, Inklaar and Timmer 2015). The 2 percent benchmark is the speed of conditional convergence in Barro and Sala-i-Martin (1992).

How to read it: one bar per period; the bar is the speed β in percent a year, converted from the OLS slope with β = −ln(1 + λT)/T. Blue: significant convergence; orange: significant divergence; gray: no significant relation. The dashed line is the 2 percent benchmark.

Point at: the only blue bar, 2000–2019, at 0.36; the orange bar for 1960–2000 at −0.40.

Since 2000: 0.36% a year, a half-life of about 190 years

  • 2000–2019: \(\beta =\) 0.36% a year, half-life about 190 years.
  • Estimating β directly by nonlinear least squares gives the same speed; its 95% interval runs from about 0.05% to 0.68% a year.
  • The Solow model with \(\alpha = 1/3\) predicts more than 5% a year: absolute convergence is more than ten times slower.

Countries do not converge to one common steady state.

Numbers from Sections G.6 and G.7: OLS-route speed 0.00365 (shown as 0.36 percent), half-life 190 years; NLS speed 0.00365, SE 0.00157, p = 0.023, 95% interval [0.00052, 0.00678].

OLS and NLS agree to about nine decimal places, because the formula linking λ and β is an exact one-to-one transformation. The standard errors differ a little: NLS measures the uncertainty of β directly, the OLS route measures it for λ.

Why this matters for policy: at 0.36 percent a year, today’s income gaps would persist for generations. Catch-up is not automatic.

β-convergence is necessary for σ-convergence, but not sufficient

\[\sigma_T^2 = (1+\lambda T)^2\,\sigma_0^2 + T^2\,\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon)\]

2000–2019 variance
in 2000 1.89
catch-up alone, \((1+\lambda T)^2\sigma_0^2\) 1.65
plus new shocks, \(T^2\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon)\) + 0.11
in 2019 1.76

Catch-up pulls countries together; new country-specific shocks push them apart.

Derivation (Section G.10): write the regression in levels, ln y_T = Ta + (1 + λT) ln y_0 + Tε. OLS residuals are uncorrelated with the regressor, so the variance splits exactly into the two terms.

The first term shrinks the spread when λ < 0. The second term (wars, crises, booms, good or bad policies) spreads countries apart. The variance falls only if catch-up beats the new shocks.

Numbers from the notebook’s check: variance in 2000 1.8937; catch-up term 1.6487; shock term 0.1148; sum 1.7635, equal to the actual 2019 variance 1.7635. The identity holds exactly.

Faster growth of the poor does not by itself make the world more equal

Since poor countries have grown faster since 2000, income differences across countries must be shrinking fast.

Catch-up since 2000 cut the variance by only about 0.24 over 19 years, and new shocks added back about 0.11: the spread in 2019 was still 91% above 1960.

This is the β versus σ distinction in one sentence. The numbers are from the decomposition on the previous slide (1.89 → 1.65 from catch-up, then +0.11 from shocks → 1.76) and from Section G.9 (+91 percent since 1960).

No absolute convergence does not refute the Solow model

The data show no catch-up over 1960–2019, so the Solow model is wrong.

The model predicts that each country converges to its own steady state. Absolute convergence tests the extra assumption that all countries share one steady state, and that is what the data reject.

Countries differ in saving rates, population growth, schooling, technology and institutions, which the intercept of the regression absorbs. With controls for these (conditional convergence), studies such as Barro (1991) and Mankiw, Romer and Weil (1992) find negative slopes and speeds near 2 percent a year.

Appendix H estimates the augmented Solow model of Mankiw, Romer and Weil on this question.

Can transfers to poor regions speed up convergence?

The EU spends a large share of its budget on cohesion policy, transfers meant to help its poorest regions catch up. Does convergence analysis say they work?

What the analysis says

  • Convergence is about growth rates, so a policy must raise the growth of poor regions for years, not their income once.
  • In the Solow logic, transfers that raise investment move a region’s steady state up.

What it leaves out

  • Whether the money is spent well: effects differ across regions.
  • The counterfactual: regions that receive funds differ from those that do not.

Evidence, presented as contested. Becker, Egger and von Ehrlich (2010) use the EU’s eligibility threshold (75 percent of average EU GDP per head) as a regression discontinuity and find that the poorest regions’ growth rises with Objective 1 transfers. The same authors (2013) find that the effects depend on the regions’ absorptive capacity: human capital and the quality of government. Other studies find smaller or no effects.

Link to the appendix: the same tools (β- and σ-convergence) are standard for measuring the success of regional policy, for example across EU regions.

Catch-up requires raising each country’s own steady state

A finance ministry in a poor country asks: will we catch up with the rich world by ourselves, or does growth policy matter?

What the model predicts

  • Without policy change, a country converges to its own steady state, not to the rich world’s.
  • Higher investment, more schooling, lower population growth and better technology raise the steady state.

What it leaves out

  • Which policies raise investment and productivity: the model takes them as given.
  • Institutions and ideas, which Chapter 6 and the growth literature emphasize.

Conditional-convergence studies (Barro 1991; Mankiw, Romer and Weil 1992) find that, controlling for investment, schooling and population growth, poorer countries do grow faster. That means policies that raise those determinants raise the steady state, and catch-up follows.

Caution: these are cross-country correlations, not causal estimates of any single policy. Which policies work remains debated.

A skeptic: “A negative slope may be only regression to the mean”

A skeptic says: “Countries that were unusually poor in the start year will look like fast growers just by bouncing back. A negative slope proves nothing.”

Reply: right, and that is Galton’s fallacy (Quah 1993). It is why we also check σ: since 2000, the slope turned negative and the spread stopped rising, but it fell only about 8% from its peak.

Galton’s fallacy: in any noisy series, extreme observations tend to be followed by less extreme ones. A negative β can then appear even when the distribution is not narrowing. Quah (1993) made this point about convergence regressions.

Two further caveats to mention: the balanced panel drops 40 countries whose data start after 1960, many of them poor; and the breakpoint in 2000 was chosen after looking at the data (the rolling windows and heatmap help here). Convergence clubs, groups converging to different steady states, are another way to read the data.

What to remember

  • The Solow prediction: an economy closes a share \(\beta = (1-\alpha)(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\) of its gap each year; with \(\alpha = 1/3\), a half-life of about 13 years.
  • No absolute convergence over 1960–2019: divergence until 2000, then slow convergence, about 0.36% a year.
  • The spread of incomes nearly doubled and has barely fallen since its 2006 peak.
  • β is necessary but not sufficient for σ: new shocks offset catch-up.
  • Countries converge to their own steady states: growth policy is about raising them.
