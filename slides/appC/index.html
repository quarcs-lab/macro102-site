Three ways to solve one equation: compute, use letters, or search

When does income growing at 2% a year double? Solve \((1+0.02)^t = 2\).

years = np.arange(0, 51)                                    # 1. NumPy: compute and look
income = 1.02 ** years
print("Income is at least twice its start value in year", years[income >= 2][0])

g, t = sp.symbols("g t")                                    # 2. SymPy: solve with letters
doubling_time = sp.solve(sp.Eq((1 + g) ** t, 2), t)[0]      # log(2)/log(g + 1)
print(f"Exact doubling time at 2 percent: {float(doubling_time.subs(g, 0.02)):.2f} years")

t_double = optimize.brentq(lambda t: 1.02 ** t - 2, 1, 100) # 3. SciPy: a root finder
print(f"brentq finds {t_double:.2f} years")
Income is at least twice its start value in year 36
Exact doubling time at 2 percent: 35.00 years
brentq finds 35.00 years

NumPy computes many numbers, SymPy does the algebra with letters, SciPy searches when there is no formula.

This is the appendix’s opening example (“Three ways to do math in Python”), condensed into one cell; the outputs are the notebook’s.

  1. NumPy computes income for every whole year from 0 to 50 and keeps the first year in which it is at least 2. It says year 36 because it only looks at whole years: after 35 years income is 1.9999, just short.
  2. SymPy treats g and t as letters and solves (1+g)^t = 2 for t. The answer is a formula, ln 2 / ln(1+g), which holds for every growth rate. Substituting g = 0.02 gives 35.00.
  3. SciPy’s brentq is a root finder: it needs a function whose sign differs at the two ends of an interval (here 1 and 100 years) and narrows down the zero in between.

When to use which: NumPy when you want a table or a chart; SymPy when you want to see why a result holds or check a chapter’s algebra; SciPy when there is no formula or it is too messy. Many slides below use one of the three.

The two marks students mix up most: a bar means “given from outside the model” (a parameter or an exogenous variable), a star means “the value the model settles at” (a steady state or an equilibrium). So s̄ is something we choose when we run the model; k* is something the model tells us.

Ask: in Y = A K^(1/3) L^(2/3), which letters are variables and which would carry a bar in a model? (Ā is a parameter in Chapter 4; K and L are inputs.)

An equation is a sentence: output falls when the interest rate is high

\[\widetilde{Y}_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R_t - \bar{r})\]

  • \(\widetilde{Y}_t\): short-run output, the outcome (percent above or below potential).
  • \(R_t - \bar{r}\): how far the real interest rate is above its long-run value \(\bar{r}\).
  • \(\bar{b}\): how strongly spending reacts to that gap; \(\bar{a}\): a demand shock.

Read it aloud: output is the demand shock, minus \(\bar{b}\) times the interest-rate gap.

Ch. 11 (the IS curve), Ch. 12–13 (the short-run model)

A function links an input to an output. The IS curve of Chapter 11, equation (11.14), says how short-run output depends on the real interest rate.

Read it as a sentence: “Short-run output equals the demand shock ā, minus b̄ times the amount by which the real interest rate exceeds its long-run value r̄.” When R equals r̄ and there is no shock, output is at potential.

Units: Ỹ and R are both in percent. b̄ is “points of output per point of interest rate”.

Ask: what sign does b̄ have, and why? (Positive: a higher interest rate makes borrowing for houses, machines and cars more expensive, so spending falls.)

Worked example: which interest rate closes the output gap?

Step 1. Set output to potential, \(\widetilde{Y}_t = 0\): \[0 = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R - \bar{r})\]

Step 2. Move the interest-rate term to the left: \[\bar{b}\,(R - \bar{r}) = \bar{a}\]

Step 3. Divide by \(\bar{b}\) and add \(\bar{r}\): \[R = \bar{r} + \frac{\bar{a}}{\bar{b}}\]

Step 4. Numbers: \(\bar{a} = 1\), \(\bar{b} = 0.5\), \(\bar{r} = 2\): \[R = 2 + \frac{1}{0.5} = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{4\%}}\]

Do this on the board, one step per click. The appendix solves it with SymPy (sp.solve(IS.subs(Y_tilde, 0), R)) and gets the same formula, r̄ + ā/b̄, before putting in the numbers. Its printed output: “Real interest rate that closes the gap: 4.0 percent.”

The lesson: work with letters first. The formula shows which parameters matter: a bigger demand shock needs a bigger rate rise; a more rate-sensitive economy (bigger b̄) needs a smaller one.

The numbers are the appendix’s own illustrative ones: a positive demand shock of 1 percent, b̄ = 0.5, r̄ = 2 percent.

Reading the IS curve off a graph

On the IS curve, where is output at potential, and is output above or below potential when the real rate is 6%?

With \(\bar{a} = 1\), \(\bar{b} = 0.5\) and \(\bar{r} = 2\): predict output at \(R = 2\), at \(R = 4\) and at \(R = 6\).

Collect guesses. The answers: at R = 2, Ỹ = 1 − 0.5 × 0 = 1 (the demand shock alone); at R = 4, Ỹ = 1 − 0.5 × 2 = 0; at R = 6, Ỹ = 1 − 0.5 × 4 = −1.

A common slip: putting R on the horizontal axis. The book draws the IS curve with output horizontal and the interest rate vertical, so a higher rate is a point higher up and to the left.

A higher rate moves the economy down and to the left of the IS curve

The answer: \(\widetilde{Y} = 1\) at \(R = 2\), \(0\) at \(R = 4\), and \(-1\) at \(R = 6\). Each point of \(R\) costs \(\bar{b} = 0.5\) points of output.

Spending reacts twice as strongly to the interest rate: \(\bar{b} = 1\). Which real interest rate now closes the gap?

\(R = \bar{r} + \bar{a}/\bar{b} = 2 + 1/1 = 3\%\). A more rate-sensitive economy needs a smaller rate rise.

Give students a minute. The answer uses the formula from the worked example; no new algebra.

Extension question: what if the shock is negative, ā = −1? Then R = 2 − 2 = 0 with b̄ = 0.5: the central bank cuts the real rate to zero. Chapter 14 asks what happens when the rate it needs is below zero.

A percent change is not a change in percentage points

\[\text{percent change} = 100 \times \frac{x_1 - x_0}{x_0}\]

Unemployment rises from 4% to 5%:

\[\text{change in points: } 5 - 4 = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{1}\textbf{ percentage point}}\]

\[\text{percent change: } 100 \times \frac{5 - 4}{4} = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{25}\textbf{ percent}}\]

Ch. 2 (GDP growth), Ch. 7 (unemployment), Ch. 8 (inflation)

The appendix’s first example (Section C.2). Its output: “Change: 1.0 percentage point” and “Change: 25.0 percent”.

The rule: when a variable is already a rate in percent (unemployment, inflation, interest rates, debt as a share of GDP), say “percentage points” for the difference. “Percent” is the relative change.

Both numbers are correct. They answer different questions. Newspapers mix them up all the time.

“Unemployment rose 1 percent” is the wrong way to say it

Unemployment went from 4% to 5%, so it rose by 1 percent.

It rose by 1 percentage point. In percent, it rose by 25 percent: a quarter more people are out of work, relative to the labor force.

This is the most common slip in macro writing. A central bank that raises its policy rate from 2 to 3 percent raised it by 1 percentage point (or 100 basis points), which is a 50 percent increase in the rate.

Ask: a mortgage rate falls from 7 to 6 percent. How many points? How many percent? (1 point; about 14 percent.)

Points or percent? Practice both

Inflation rises from 2% to 3%. By how many percentage points, and by how many percent? The debt ratio rises from 100% to 110% of GDP: same two questions.

Inflation: \(3 - 2 = 1\) point, and \(100 \times 1/2 = 50\) percent. Debt: \(110 - 100 = 10\) points, and \(100 \times 10/100 = 10\) percent.

When the base is 100, points and percent coincide; when the base is small, they are far apart.

Let students work in pairs for one minute.

The second case is a trick: with a base of 100 the two numbers agree. That is why the confusion often goes unnoticed for debt ratios near 100 percent, and is large for small rates such as inflation or interest rates.

A ratio such as debt to GDP removes the size of the economy: 30 trillion dollars means little on its own, while 100 percent of GDP can be compared over time and across countries (Chapter 18).

An index sets a base year to 100; rebasing just divides

\[\text{rebased index}_t = 100 \times \frac{x_t}{x_{\text{base}}}\]

What would 100 dollars of 1913 buy in 2025? The CPI was 9.60 in 1913 and 321.94 in 2025 (base 1982–84 = 100).

\[\text{price ratio} = \frac{321.94}{9.60} = 33.5 \qquad 100 \times 33.5 = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{\$3{,}354}} \text{ of 2025}\]

\[\text{1913 with 2025} = 100: \quad 100 \times \frac{9.60}{321.94} = 3.0\]

A price index measures the price level relative to a base period. The U.S. CPI has the base 1982–84 = 100. To rebase it to 2025 = 100, divide every value by the 2025 value and multiply by 100.

Annualizing: a monthly change compounds over 12 months

\[\text{annual rate} = (1+m)^{12} - 1\]

Prices rise 0.3% in one month, \(m = 0.003\):

\[1.003^{12} = 1.0366 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{3.66\%}} \text{ a year, a bit more than } 12 \times 0.3 = 3.6\]

A quarterly change uses the 4th power: 0.5% a quarter is \(1.005^4 - 1 = 2.02\%\) a year.

Ch. 8 and 12 (monthly inflation), Ch. 9 (quarterly GDP growth)

Monthly and quarterly changes are usually reported at an annual rate: the change that would result if the same change went on for a whole year. Because changes compound, the annual rate is a bit more than 12 times the monthly change.

The appendix’s printed output: “0.3% a month is 3.66% at an annual rate (12 x 0.3 = 3.6)” and “0.5% a quarter is 2.02% at an annual rate”.

When the BEA says “GDP grew at a 2 percent annual rate in the second quarter”, it means the quarter’s growth was about 0.5 percent, annualized.

Five exponent rules do most of the work in growth models

Rule Example in the book
\(x^a \cdot x^b = x^{a+b}\) \(K^{1/3}K^{2/3} = K\)
\((x^a)^b = x^{ab}\) \((k^{1/3})^{3/2} = k^{1/2}\)
\(x^{-a} = 1/x^a\) \(K^{-2/3} = 1/K^{2/3}\)
\(x^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{x}\) \(k^{1/3}\) is the cube root of \(k\)
\((xy)^a = x^a y^a\) \((AK)^{1/3} = A^{1/3}K^{1/3}\)

An exponent counts how many times a number multiplies itself: \(x^3 = x \cdot x \cdot x\).

Ch. 4 (production function), Ch. 5 (Solow), Ch. 6 (Romer)

The appendix’s exponent table (Section C.3). Each example comes from a production function in the book.

Quick check of rule 1: K^(1/3) K^(2/3) = K^(1/3 + 2/3) = K^1 = K. That is the algebra behind constant returns to scale in Chapter 4: doubling K and L doubles output because the exponents add up to one.

Rule 4 is the one students forget: a fractional exponent is a root. 8^(1/3) = 2 because 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.

Exponential growth: the same percent each year, more dollars each year

Start from \(y_0\) and grow at the constant rate \(\bar{g}\): \[y_1 = y_0\,(1+\bar{g})\]

\[y_2 = y_1\,(1+\bar{g}) = y_0\,(1+\bar{g})^2\]

\[y_t = y_0\,(1+\bar{g})^t\]

Income grows by the same percent each year, so it grows by more and more dollars.

Ch. 3 (growth facts), Ch. 5 (Solow), Ch. 6 (Romer)

Build the formula one year at a time on the board: each year multiplies the previous one by (1 + ḡ). After t years we have multiplied t times. This is equation (3.7) of the book.

Numbers to make it concrete: start at 100 with 10 percent growth. Year 1: 110 (+10). Year 2: 121 (+11). Year 3: 133.1 (+12.1). The percent is constant; the dollar increase grows.

This is why a constant growth rate produces a curve that bends upward on a normal (linear) chart.

Worked example: a century of growth at 1%, 2% and 3%

Raise \((1+\bar{g})\) to the power 100:

\[1.01^{100} = 2.7 \qquad 1.02^{100} = 7.2 \qquad 1.03^{100} = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{19.2}}\]

In continuous time growth is written \(y_t = y_0 e^{gt}\), with \(e \approx 2.718\). For small rates the two are close:

\[(1.02)^{100} = 7.24 \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad e^{0.02 \times 100} = 7.39\]

Tripling the growth rate multiplies the century’s gain by seven.

The appendix’s printed outputs: “1% a year for 100 years multiplies income by 2.7”, “2% … 7.2”, “3% … 19.2”, and “(1.02)^100 = 7.24 versus e^(0.02 x 100) = 7.39”.

Why the continuous version is a bit higher: e^(gt) compounds continuously, every instant, not once a year. Section C.6 explains why the two are close for small g (ln(1+g) ≈ g), and Section C.11 uses continuous time.

The Rule of 70: doubling time ≈ 70 divided by the growth rate in percent

Step 1. Income doubles when \((1+g)^t = 2\). Take logs of both sides: \[t\,\ln(1+g) = \ln 2\]

Step 2. Solve for \(t\), then use \(\ln(1+g) \approx g\) for a small \(g\): \[t = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln(1+g)} \approx \frac{0.693}{g} = \frac{69.3}{100\,g}\]

Step 3. Write \(G = 100\,g\), the growth rate in percent, and round 69.3 to 70: \[t \approx \frac{70}{G} \qquad G = 2: \quad t \approx \frac{70}{2} = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{35}} \text{ years (exact: 35.0)}\]

On a ratio scale (log scale), equal distances on the vertical axis mean equal ratios. Plotting y on a ratio scale is the same as plotting ln y on a normal scale.

The mean of yearly growth rates overstates growth

Prices fall 50% one year and rise 50% the next. The average change is zero, so they are back where they started.

Three growth rules: products add, ratios subtract, powers multiply

\[\text{product: } z = x \cdot y \;\Rightarrow\; g_z \approx g_x + g_y\]

\[\text{ratio: } z = x/y \;\Rightarrow\; g_z \approx g_x - g_y\]

\[\text{power: } z = x^a \;\Rightarrow\; g_z \approx a \cdot g_x\]

These three rules turn every growth question in the book into addition and multiplication.

Ch. 3 (rules), Ch. 4 and 6 (growth accounting), Ch. 8 (quantity theory)

The three rules of Chapter 3. They follow from the log rules: the growth rate is approximately the change in the log, and logs turn products into sums, ratios into differences and powers into multiples.

Examples to say aloud: - GDP = GDP per person × population: GDP growth ≈ per-person growth + population growth. - GDP per person = GDP / population: per-person growth ≈ GDP growth − population growth. - k^(1/3): its growth is one third of the growth of k.

Why “approximately”: the product rule drops a tiny cross term

Step 1. Over one year, each factor grows by its own rate: \[1 + g_z = (1+g_x)(1+g_y)\]

Step 2. Multiply out: \[1 + g_z = 1 + g_x + g_y + g_x g_y\]

Step 3. With 2% and 3%, the cross term is \(0.02 \times 0.03 = 0.0006\), so drop it: \[g_z = 0.0506 \text{ exactly} \qquad \approx \; g_x + g_y = 0.05\]

In continuous time the rules are exact (Act V).

The appendix’s SymPy check: sp.expand((1 + g_x)(1 + g_y) − 1) prints “g_xg_y + g_x + g_y”, and with 2 and 3 percent it gives 0.0506.

The cross term is the product of two small numbers, so it is tiny. It stops being tiny when the rates are large (high inflation): the next live figure shows when.

Worked example: the growth of \(Y = A K^{1/3} L^{2/3}\)

Step 1. Product rule: add the growth rates of the three factors: \[g_Y = g_A + g_{K^{1/3}} + g_{L^{2/3}}\]

Step 2. Power rule: each growth rate times its exponent: \[g_Y = g_A + \tfrac{1}{3}\,g_K + \tfrac{2}{3}\,g_L\]

Step 3. Numbers: \(g_A = 1\), \(g_K = 3\), \(g_L = 1\) (percent): \[g_Y = 1 + 1 + 0.67 = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{2.67\%}} \qquad g_{Y/L} = g_Y - g_L = 1.67\%\]

A derivative is a slope: the change in \(f\) per unit change in \(x\)

\[f'(x) = \frac{df}{dx} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\]

Function Derivative Example in the book
\(x^n\) \(n\,x^{n-1}\) \(\frac{d}{dk}k^{1/3} = \tfrac{1}{3}k^{-2/3}\)
\(\ln x\) \(1/x\) \(\frac{d}{dc}\ln c = 1/c\), marginal utility (Ch. 16)
\(e^{ax}\) \(a\,e^{ax}\) continuous growth
\(c \cdot f(x)\) \(c \cdot f'(x)\) constants stay
\(f(g(x))\) \(f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\) the chain rule

Economists say marginal: the marginal product of capital is the extra output from one more unit of capital.

The derivative f’(x), also written df/dx, is the slope of a function at a point: how much f changes per unit change in x, for a very small change. The limit says: take the slope between x and x + h, and let h shrink.

The rules table is the appendix’s (Section C.7). The power rule does most of the work in this book, because production and utility functions are powers.

Chain rule example: d/dx ln(1 + x) = 1/(1 + x) × 1. We used it for the Taylor approximation in Act III.

Worked example: the slope of \(y = k^{1/3}\) at \(k = 1\) and \(k = 8\)

Step 1. Power rule: bring the exponent down, reduce it by one: \[\frac{dy}{dk} = \tfrac{1}{3}\,k^{1/3 - 1} = \tfrac{1}{3}\,k^{-2/3}\]

Step 2. At \(k = 1\): \(1^{-2/3} = 1\): \[\tfrac{1}{3} \times 1 = 0.333\]

Step 3. At \(k = 8\): \(8^{2/3} = (8^{1/3})^2 = 4\), so \(8^{-2/3} = 1/4\): \[\tfrac{1}{3} \times \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{12} = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{0.083}}\]

Ch. 4 (marginal products), Ch. 5 (Solow: output per person \(y = k^{1/3}\))

This is the marginal product of capital in the Solow model’s per-person production function y = k^(1/3) (with Ā = 1, equation 5.6). The appendix’s exact slope at k = 8: 0.08333.

The slope falls from 0.333 to 0.083 as capital rises from 1 to 8: each extra unit of capital adds less output. That is diminishing returns, the engine of the Solow model.

8^(2/3) = 4 was in the Act II exercise.

Partial derivatives give the marginal products of \(K\) and \(L\)

\[Y = \bar{A}\,K^{1/3}L^{2/3}\]

\[MPK = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial K} = \tfrac{1}{3}\bar{A}K^{-2/3}L^{2/3} = \tfrac{1}{3}\,\frac{Y}{K} \qquad MPL = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial L} = \tfrac{2}{3}\,\frac{Y}{L}\]

Differentiate again in \(K\): the slope of the slope. \[\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial K^2} = -\tfrac{2}{9}\,\bar{A}\,K^{-5/3}L^{2/3} < 0\]

A negative second derivative means diminishing returns: each extra machine adds less.

Ch. 4 (equations 4.3, 4.4), Ch. 5

A function of several inputs has a partial derivative for each input: change one, hold the others fixed. Written ∂Y/∂K.

For MPK, treat Ā and L^(2/3) as constants and apply the power rule to K^(1/3). Then rewrite: (1/3) Ā K^(−2/3) L^(2/3) = (1/3) × Ā K^(1/3) L^(2/3) / K = (1/3) Y/K. The marginal product is a share of the average product.

SymPy confirms (appendix outputs): “MPK − (1/3) Y/K = 0”, “MPL − (2/3) Y/L = 0”, and “Second derivative in K: −2AbarL**(2/3)/(9*K**(5/3)) (negative: diminishing returns)“.

In SymPy, write the exponent as sp.Rational(1, 3), not 1/3: the decimal 0.3333 would stop SymPy from simplifying exactly.

Each exponent of a Cobb-Douglas function is an elasticity

\[\text{elasticity of } Y \text{ with respect to } K = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial K}\cdot\frac{K}{Y} = \frac{\%\,\Delta Y}{\%\,\Delta K}\]

Step 1. Put in \(MPK = \tfrac{1}{3}\,Y/K\):

\[\tfrac{1}{3}\,\frac{Y}{K}\cdot\frac{K}{Y} = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{3}}} \qquad \text{and likewise for labor: } \tfrac{2}{3}\,\frac{Y}{L}\cdot\frac{L}{Y} = \tfrac{2}{3}\]

A 1% rise in capital raises output by 1/3 of a percent, whatever the levels.

Ch. 4 (production), Ch. 6 (growth accounting), Ch. 16 (consumption)

An elasticity is a ratio of percent changes: it does not depend on units. That is why economists like them: “a 1 percent rise in capital raises output by 0.33 percent” is true in dollars, euros or machines.

SymPy’s output in the appendix: “Elasticity of Y with respect to K: 1/3” and “… L: 2/3”.

The power rule of growth rates (Act III) is the same fact in time: g_Y = (1/3) g_K when only K grows.

Constant returns: double both inputs, and output doubles

Step 1. Scale both inputs by \(\lambda\): \[F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \bar{A}(\lambda K)^{1/3}(\lambda L)^{2/3} = \lambda^{1/3 + 2/3}\,\bar{A}K^{1/3}L^{2/3} = \lambda\,Y\]

Step 2. Pay each input its marginal product and add up (Euler’s theorem): \[K \cdot MPK + L \cdot MPL = \tfrac{1}{3}Y + \tfrac{2}{3}Y = Y\]

Step 3. With \(r = MPK\) and \(w = MPL\): capital’s share \(rK/Y = 1/3\), labor’s share \(wL/Y = 2/3\).

Payments to capital and labor exhaust output exactly: nothing is left over as profit.

Ch. 4 (equations 4.5, 4.6)

Constant returns to scale: F(λK, λL) = λF(K, L). The exponents add up to one, so λ^(1/3) λ^(2/3) = λ.

The appendix’s SymPy outputs: “F(λK, λL) / F(K, L) = lambda” and “K·MPK + L·MPL − Y = 0”.

This is why factor shares add up to one in Chapter 4: when firms pay r = MPK and w = MPL, capital gets 1/3 of output and labor 2/3, and together they exhaust output. Chapter 2 compares these shares with the data.

The exponent 1/3 is not the marginal product of capital

In \(Y = \bar{A}K^{1/3}L^{2/3}\), the marginal product of capital is 1/3.

1/3 is the elasticity (and capital’s share). The marginal product is \(MPK = \tfrac{1}{3}\,Y/K\): it falls as capital rises, from 0.333 at \(k = 1\) to 0.083 at \(k = 8\).

The confusion is natural because both numbers involve 1/3. One is a ratio of percent changes (unitless, constant); the other is a ratio of units (output per machine) that changes with K.

Quick check: if MPK were the constant 1/3, there would be no diminishing returns and no Solow steady state.

At the top of a hill, the slope is zero

\[\text{first-order condition: } f'(x^*) = 0 \qquad\qquad \text{second-order condition: } f''(x^*) < 0\]

  • Left of the peak the slope is positive; right of it, negative; at the peak, zero.
  • The second-order condition checks it is a maximum: the slope falls as you pass through \(x^*\).
  • Recipe: differentiate, set to zero, solve for \(x^*\), check the sign of \(f''\).

Ch. 4 (firms hire), Ch. 16 (households consume), Ch. 17 (firms invest)

When a function has a maximum at x, its slope there is zero. That is the first-order condition (FOC). To be sure it is a maximum and not a minimum, the slope must fall as you pass through x: the second derivative is negative.

Every optimization in the book follows this recipe: the firm’s choice of workers (Chapter 4), of capital (Chapter 17) and the household’s choice of consumption (Chapter 16).

Worked example: a firm hires until the marginal product equals the wage

\(\bar{A} = 1\), \(\bar{K} = 8\), so \(\bar{K}^{1/3} = 2\). Profit is \(\Pi(L) = 2L^{2/3} - wL\).

Step 1. First-order condition, \(MPL = w\): \[\Pi^{\,\prime}(L) = \tfrac{4}{3}\,L^{-1/3} - w = 0\]

Step 2. Solve for \(L\): \(L^{1/3} = \dfrac{4}{3w}\), so \[L^* = \frac{64}{27\,w^3}\]

Step 3. At \(w = 2/3\): \(27 \times (2/3)^3 = 8\), so \[L^* = 64/8 = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{8}} \text{ workers}\]

The firm’s problem of the appendix (compare equation 4.2 of the book). Profit is revenue minus the wage bill, with the price of output normalized to 1.

Step 1: 2 × (2/3) L^(2/3 − 1) = (4/3) L^(−1/3). This is the MPL; the FOC says hire until MPL = w.

Step 2: invert: L^(1/3) = 4/(3w); cube both sides: L = 64/(27 w^3). SymPy prints 64/(27 w^3), the same formula.

Step 3: with w = 2/3, L* = 8. The labor demand curve slopes down: a higher wage means fewer workers.

A household chooses consumption today and tomorrow

\[\max_{c_{\text{today}},\,c_{\text{future}}} \; U = \ln c_{\text{today}} + \beta \ln c_{\text{future}}\]

\[\text{subject to} \quad c_{\text{today}} + \frac{c_{\text{future}}}{1+R} = \bar{X}\]

  • \(\beta\): patience; a smaller \(\beta\) means the future counts for less.
  • \(\bar{X}\): lifetime wealth, in today’s dollars.
  • Future consumption costs \(1/(1+R)\) today: saving one dollar returns \(1+R\).

Ch. 16 (equation 16.5, the two-period model)

The household of Chapter 16 lives two periods and values consumption with log utility. Its budget constraint says the present value of consumption equals lifetime wealth.

Two ways to solve such a problem: substitution (when the constraint is easy to solve) and the Lagrangian (which always works). The next two slides do both and get the same answer.

Method 1: substitute the budget constraint into utility

Step 1. Solve the constraint for future consumption: \[c_{\text{future}} = (1+R)\,(\bar{X} - c_{\text{today}})\]

Step 2. Utility now has one choice variable: \[U = \ln c_{\text{today}} + \beta \ln\!\left[(1+R)(\bar{X} - c_{\text{today}})\right]\]

Step 3. First-order condition (chain rule on the second term): \[\frac{1}{c_{\text{today}}} - \frac{\beta}{\bar{X} - c_{\text{today}}} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; c_{\text{today}} = \frac{\bar{X}}{1+\beta}\]

With \(\beta = 1\): consume half of lifetime wealth today, \(\bar{X}/2\) (equation 16.9).

Step 3 in detail: d/dc of β ln[(1 + R)(X̄ − c)] is β × 1/[(1 + R)(X̄ − c)] × (−(1 + R)) = −β/(X̄ − c). Setting the FOC to zero: X̄ − c = β c, so c = X̄/(1 + β).

SymPy (appendix output): sp.solve(sp.diff(U, c_today), c_today)[0] gives X/(β + 1).

Note R drops out of c_today: with log utility, a higher interest rate has an income effect and a substitution effect that cancel exactly for today’s consumption.

Method 2: the Lagrangian gives the Euler equation

Step 1. Add the constraint, times a multiplier \(\mu\): \[\mathbf{L} = \ln c_{\text{today}} + \beta \ln c_{\text{future}} + \mu\left[\bar{X} - c_{\text{today}} - \frac{c_{\text{future}}}{1+R}\right]\]

Step 2. Set each derivative to zero: \[\frac{1}{c_{\text{today}}} = \mu \qquad\qquad \frac{\beta}{c_{\text{future}}} = \frac{\mu}{1+R}\]

Step 3. Divide the two conditions to remove \(\mu\): \[\frac{c_{\text{future}}}{c_{\text{today}}} = \beta\,(1+R)\]

Patience (\(\beta\)) and a high interest rate (\(R\)) tilt consumption toward the future.

Ch. 16 (equation 16.8, the Euler equation)

The third condition, ∂ℒ/∂μ = 0, just gives back the budget constraint.

The appendix’s SymPy output: c_today = X/(β + 1), c_future = Xβ(R + 1)/(β + 1), and “c_future / c_today = beta*(R + 1) (the Euler equation, 16.8)“.

μ is the marginal utility of one more dollar of wealth: the “shadow price” of the constraint.

A zero derivative can also be a minimum

Wherever the derivative is zero, the function is at its maximum.

A zero slope also marks a minimum or a flat point. Check the second derivative: for the firm, \(\Pi^{\,\prime\prime}(8) = -1/36 < 0\), a maximum. And compare with the corners when the choice is bounded.

Example: f(x) = x² has f’(0) = 0 at its minimum; f(x) = x³ has f’(0) = 0 at a flat point that is neither.

In economics the objective functions (profit with diminishing returns, log utility) are usually concave, so the FOC does give the maximum. But check: a problem with increasing returns can have its FOC at a minimum of profit.

The short-run model is three linear equations in three unknowns

\[\begin{aligned} \text{IS:}\quad & \widetilde{Y}_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R_t - \bar{r}) \\ \text{MP rule:}\quad & R_t - \bar{r} = \bar{m}\,(\pi_t - \bar{\pi}) \\ \text{AS:}\quad & \pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + \bar{\nu}\,\widetilde{Y}_t + \bar{o} \end{aligned}\]

Move the unknowns \(\widetilde{Y}_t, R_t, \pi_t\) to the left and everything else to the right: the matrix form \(Mz = c\).

\[\begin{pmatrix}1 & \bar{b} & 0\\ 0 & 1 & -\bar{m}\\ -\bar{\nu} & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{Y}_t\\ R_t\\ \pi_t\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\bar{a}+\bar{b}\bar{r}\\ \bar{r}-\bar{m}\bar{\pi}\\ \pi_{t-1}+\bar{o}\end{pmatrix}\]

Ch. 11 (IS), Ch. 12 (Phillips curve), Ch. 13 (the policy rule and AS/AD)

The short-run model of Chapters 11–13: the IS curve (11.14), the monetary policy rule (13.1) and the aggregate supply (Phillips) curve (13.2).

Building the matrix, row by row: - IS: Ỹ + b̄R = ā + b̄r̄ (move −b̄R to the left, b̄r̄ to the right). - MP: R − m̄π = r̄ − m̄π̄. - AS: −ν̄Ỹ + π = π_{t−1} + ō.

Each row of M holds the coefficients on (Ỹ, R, π); c holds everything that is known this period.

Worked example: the Solow steady state by hand

Capital stops changing when investment equals depreciation, with \(\bar{s} = 0.4\), \(\bar{d} = 0.1\), \(\bar{A} = 1\): \[0.4\,k^{1/3} = 0.1\,k\]

Step 1. Divide both sides by \(0.1\,k^{1/3}\) (for \(k > 0\)): \[4 = k^{2/3}\]

Step 2. Raise both sides to the power \(3/2\): \[k^* = 4^{3/2} = (\sqrt{4})^3 = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{8}} \qquad \text{in general } k^* = \left(\frac{\bar{s}\bar{A}}{\bar{d}}\right)^{3/2}\]

Ch. 5 (equation 5.8)

A nonlinear equation, but one we can solve by hand with the exponent rules of Act II: k / k^(1/3) = k^(2/3), and (k(2/3))(3/2) = k.

The appendix’s outputs: “SymPy: [8]” (with k declared positive) and “Formula (5.8): 8.0”.

There is a second root, k = 0: with no capital, there is no output, no investment and no depreciation. We divided it away in Step 1.

The stability argument on this slide is informal; the next slides make it precise with difference equations.

A difference equation says how today’s value depends on yesterday’s

\[x_{t+1} = a\,x_t + b\]

Step 1. The steady state repeats itself: \(x^* = a\,x^* + b\), so \[x^* = \frac{b}{1-a} \qquad (a \neq 1)\]

Step 2. Subtract the two equations: the gap to the steady state is multiplied by \(a\) each period: \[x_{t+1} - x^* = a\,(x_t - x^*) \;\Rightarrow\; x_t = x^* + a^t\,(x_0 - x^*)\]

Ch. 5 (capital), Ch. 7 (unemployment), Ch. 12–13 (inflation), Ch. 18 (public debt)

A first-order linear difference equation. The book’s examples: capital accumulation Δk_{t+1} = s̄y_t − d̄k_t (5.7), the bathtub model of unemployment (Chapter 7), the Phillips curve π_t = π_{t−1} + ν̄Ỹ_t (12.5), and the government budget constraint B_{t+1} = (1 + i)B_t + G_t − T_t (18.2).

Step 2: x_{t+1} − x* = a x_t + b − (a x* + b) = a (x_t − x*). Apply it t times: the gap after t periods is a^t times the initial gap.

SymPy’s rsolve gives the same solution (appendix output): steady state −b/(a − 1), and x(t) = a^t (a x_0 + b − x_0)/(a − 1) − b/(a − 1), which simplifies to the formula on the slide.

Stability depends on \(a\): the gap shrinks only if \(\lvert a \rvert < 1\)

Value of \(a\) What the gap \(a^t(x_0 - x^*)\) does Path
\(0 < a < 1\) shrinks, keeping its sign smooth approach
\(a = 0\) vanishes after one period jumps to \(x^*\)
\(-1 < a < 0\) shrinks, switching sign each period zigzag approach
\(\lvert a \rvert > 1\) grows without limit explodes

\[\text{half-life} = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln \lvert a \rvert} \qquad a = 0.8: \;\; \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln 0.8} = 3.1 \text{ periods}\]

The closer \(\lvert a \rvert\) is to 1, the slower the approach.

The half-life is the number of periods it takes to close half of the gap: solve |a|^t = 0.5 for t (logs again).

a = 0.8: ln 0.5 = −0.693 and ln 0.8 = −0.223, so 3.1 periods. a = 0.5: exactly 1 period.

A negative a makes the path overshoot: x jumps above x*, then below, and so on, while the gap still shrinks.

A loop simulates any difference equation

def simulate_linear(a, b, x0, periods):
    path = [x0]
    for period in range(periods):
        path.append(a * path[-1] + b)                  # x_{t+1} = a x_t + b
    return np.array(path)

Example: the Solow model closes half its gap in about a decade

Capital follows \(k_{t+1} = k_t + \bar{s}k_t^{1/3} - \bar{d}k_t\): nonlinear. Near \(k^*\) it behaves like a linear equation whose \(a\) is the slope at \(k^*\): \[a = \frac{\partial k_{t+1}}{\partial k_t}\Big|_{k^*} = 1 + \tfrac{1}{3}\bar{s}\,k^{*-2/3} - \bar{d}\]

The debt ratio is a difference equation too

Step 1. The budget constraint: debt grows with interest \(i\) and the primary deficit \(D_t\): \[B_{t+1} = (1+i)\,B_t + D_t\]

Step 2. Divide by next year’s GDP, \(Y_{t+1} = (1+g)\,Y_t\), with \(b_t = B_t/Y_t\) and \(d = D_t/Y_t\): \[b_{t+1} = \frac{1+i}{1+g}\,b_t + \frac{d}{1+g}\]

Step 3. Linear with \(a = (1+i)/(1+g)\): stable if \(g > i\). The steady state solves \(b(1+g) = (1+i)b + d\): \[b^* = \frac{d}{g - i}\]

Ch. 18 (the government budget constraint, equation 18.2)

The primary deficit is spending other than interest minus taxes, D = G − T. g is the growth rate of nominal GDP.

Step 3: when the economy grows faster than the interest rate (g > i), a < 1 and the ratio settles at b* = d/(g − i). When i > g, a > 1 and the ratio keeps rising unless the government runs primary surpluses.

Units: with i, g and d in percent, b* = d/(g − i) is directly in percent of GDP when you write g − i as a decimal: 2.5 / 0.005 = 500 percent.

A balanced primary budget does not freeze the debt ratio

If taxes cover all spending except interest (\(d = 0\)), the debt-to-GDP ratio stays where it is.

With \(d = 0\) the ratio is multiplied by \((1+i)/(1+g)\) every year: it falls when \(g > i\) (99.5% → 86% by 2055) and grows without limit when \(i > g\).

The interest on the debt is not in the primary balance. If the interest rate is above the growth rate, interest payments make the debt grow faster than GDP even with a balanced primary budget: a > 1. If growth is faster, the economy “grows out of” its debt.

In continuous time, the growth rules are exact

\[\dot{x} \equiv \frac{dx}{dt} \qquad\qquad g_x = \frac{\dot{x}}{x} = \frac{d \ln x}{dt}\]

The growth rate is the time derivative of the log (chain rule: \(d\ln x/dx = 1/x\)). Then, with no term dropped:

\[\frac{d\ln(xy)}{dt} = \frac{\dot{x}}{x} + \frac{\dot{y}}{y} \qquad \frac{d\ln(x/y)}{dt} = \frac{\dot{x}}{x} - \frac{\dot{y}}{y} \qquad \frac{d\ln x^a}{dt} = a\,\frac{\dot{x}}{x}\]

The cross term \(g_x g_y\) of Act III disappears when time is continuous.

A dollar in the future is worth less than a dollar today

A dollar saved today at the interest rate \(R\) grows to \((1+R)^T\) dollars in \(T\) years. Run it backwards:

\[\text{present discounted value: } \; pdv = \frac{X}{(1+R)^T}\]

Worked example: 100 dollars paid in 10 years, at 4%: \[pdv = \frac{100}{1.04^{10}} = \frac{100}{1.48} = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{67.6}}\]

Discounting is compounding in reverse.

Ch. 7 (equation 7.6, human capital), Ch. 16 (consumption), Ch. 17 (investment), Ch. 18 (debt)

The present discounted value of a payment X that arrives in T years. Ask first: would you rather have 100 dollars today or 100 dollars in ten years? Everybody says today. Why? Because 100 dollars today, saved at 4 percent, become 148 dollars in ten years.

1.04^10 = 1.480, and 100/1.480 = 67.6. This is also the price of a 10-year zero-coupon bond paying 100 when the interest rate is 4 percent (the appendix’s bond output, next slides).

A stream of equal payments is a geometric series with a closed form

Step 1. Write the sum and multiply it by \(a\): \[S = 1 + a + a^2 + \dots + a^n \qquad aS = a + a^2 + \dots + a^{n+1}\]

Step 2. Subtract: everything cancels except the first and the last term: \[S - aS = 1 - a^{n+1} \;\Rightarrow\; S = \frac{1 - a^{n+1}}{1 - a} \quad (a \neq 1)\]

Step 3. With \(a = 1/(1+R) < 1\) and \(n \to \infty\), \(a^{n+1} \to 0\): a perpetuity of 1 from today on is worth \[\frac{1}{1 - \frac{1}{1+R}} = \frac{1+R}{R}\]

Ch. 7 (equations 7.10–7.14)

The pdv of a stream of payments is the sum of the pdv of each payment. With equal payments, each term is the previous one times a = 1/(1 + R): a geometric series.

The appendix’s SymPy outputs: “Finite sum: Piecewise((n + 1, Eq(a, 1)), ((1 − a**(n + 1))/(1 − a), True))” (the special case a = 1, where all terms are 1, gives n + 1) and “Perpetuity: (R + 1)/R (payments of 1 from today on)”.

Example: at R = 5 percent, a perpetuity of 1 a year from today is worth 1.05/0.05 = 21.

Worked example: a working life is worth 2.53 million dollars

Correlation measures how tightly two series move together

\[\text{corr}(x, y) = \frac{\text{cov}(x, y)}{s_x\,s_y} \in [-1, 1] \qquad \text{least-squares slope} = \frac{\text{cov}(x, y)}{s_x^2}\]

The Phillips curve \(\Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\,\widetilde{Y}_t\) predicts that inflation rises when output is above potential. Test it on U.S. data, 1950–2025:

A correlation is not a slope

The correlation is 0.37, so inflation rises 0.37 points for each point of output gap.

The slope (0.34) says how much \(y\) moves with \(x\); the correlation (0.37) says how tightly the points cluster around the line. Measure inflation in tenths of a point and the slope becomes 3.4; the correlation stays 0.37.

The two numbers are close here by coincidence. The slope has units (points of inflation per point of output gap); the correlation has none.

A second classic mistake: correlation is not causation. The positive correlation is consistent with the Phillips curve, but a common cause (an oil shock, a change in expectations) could move both.

Two ways to split GDP into a trend and a cycle

Method 1. A straight line through the logs, by least squares: \[\ln y_t = \alpha + \beta\,t \qquad \text{U.S. GDP per person, 1870–2025: } \beta = \color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{1.95}} \text{ log points a year}\]

Method 2. The Hodrick–Prescott filter: a smooth trend \(\tau_t\) that trades off fit against smoothness: \[\min_{\tau} \sum_t (\ln y_t - \tau_t)^2 + \lambda \sum_t (\Delta\tau_{t+1} - \Delta\tau_t)^2 \qquad \Delta\tau_t \equiv \tau_t - \tau_{t-1}\]

With \(\lambda = 100\) (annual data), the HP gap \(100(\ln y_t - \tau_t)\) has a correlation of 0.80 with the CBO output gap, 1950–2025.

What to take away from the math review

  • Measure change carefully: percent vs. points, index numbers, real vs. nominal, annual rates.
  • Growth compounds: \(y_t = y_0(1+\bar{g})^t\), the Rule of 70, logs and the ratio scale.
  • Growth rules turn products, ratios and powers into sums; approximations work for small rates and fail at extremes.
  • Derivatives give marginal products and elasticities; optima have a zero slope.
  • Equations and difference equations give equilibria and paths; stability needs \(\lvert a \rvert < 1\).
  • Present value turns future payments into one number today; statistics test the models.
