Learning goals

The United States buys more from the rest of the world than it sells to it, and it has done so every year since 1976. Is that a problem? This chapter looks at the facts of trade and at a few small examples that explain why countries trade at all. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • read the U.S. trade data: exports, imports and the trade balance (net exports) as shares of GDP;
  • use the national income identity \(Y = C + I + G + NX\), and its saving–investment form \(NX = S - I\), to explain why a trade deficit is borrowing from the rest of the world, and how deficits add up to a foreign debt;
  • solve the textbook’s trade examples (trade across time, comparative advantage, free migration) and say who gains from each;
  • compare trade balances across countries and periods, and check which year or which window a published number really refers to;
  • in Python: write small accounting functions once and reuse them, reshape tables with melt and pivot, draw grouped bar charts, average over a window with .loc[start:end], and add up flows with cumsum.

The model in code

The figures of this chapter are all data, and they all rest on a few lines of national income accounting. We write these accounting rules once, as small functions, and every figure reuses them. Then we use the same functions to solve the textbook’s three small trade examples (Tables 19.1 to 19.5).

The trade balance. Exports \(EX\) are goods and services that a country sells abroad, and imports \(IM\) are those it buys abroad. The trade balance, or net exports, is the difference, \(NX \equiv EX - IM\). A positive \(NX\) is a trade surplus, a negative one a trade deficit. Figures always show such flows as a share of GDP, \(100 \times X / Y\), so that years and countries of different size can be compared.

The national income identity. Everything an economy produces, \(Y\), is consumed (\(C\)), invested (\(I\)), bought by the government (\(G\)) or sold abroad on net (\(NX\)): \(Y = C + I + G + NX\). Solved for net exports,

\[NX = Y - (C + I + G), \qquad\text{so}\qquad NX < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; C + I + G > Y \quad (19.1)\]

and \(NX > 0 \Rightarrow C + I + G < Y\) (19.2). A country with a trade deficit uses more goods than it produces; the rest comes from abroad, on credit.

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. The text in triple quotes is a short description. The same function works for single numbers and for whole columns of a data table, because pandas does arithmetic on every row at once.

def share_of_gdp(x, gdp):
    """Any variable as a percent of GDP: 100 x / Y."""
    return 100 * x / gdp


def net_exports(exports, imports):
    """The trade balance (net exports): NX = EX - IM."""
    return exports - imports


def net_exports_from_uses(Y, C, I, G):
    """The national income identity Y = C + I + G + NX solved for NX (equations 19.1 and 19.2)."""
    return Y - (C + I + G)

Trade balances from GDP shares

The Penn World Table, the source of Figures 3 and 4, does not report net exports directly. It reports the shares of GDP that go to consumption, investment and government purchases: \(c = C/Y\), \(i = I/Y\) and \(g = G/Y\). Dividing the identity by \(Y\) gives the trade balance as a share of GDP, \(NX/Y = 1 - c - i - g\). In code, this is the same identity with \(Y = 1\):

def trade_balance_share(c, i, g):
    """NX as a percent of GDP from the GDP shares of C, I and G: 100 (1 - c - i - g)."""
    return 100 * net_exports_from_uses(1, c, i, g)


def share_of_us_gdp(share, gdp, gdp_us):
    """Rescale a share of a country's own GDP to a share of U.S. GDP (Figure 19.3, second bar)."""
    return share * gdp / gdp_us

Saving, investment and the trade balance (equation 19.6)

Total saving pays for investment. Saving comes from three sources: households, \(Y - T - C\); the government, \(T - G\); and foreigners, \(IM - EX\), the goods they send us on credit. Domestic saving is \(S \equiv (Y - T - C) + (T - G) = Y - C - G\). Put into the identity, \(Y - C - G = I + NX\), that is

\[\underbrace{NX}_{\text{trade balance}} = \underbrace{S - I}_{\text{net capital outflow}}. \quad (19.6)\]

A country that invests more than it saves (\(S < I\)) must borrow the difference abroad, and it does so by running a trade deficit. Two more functions measure the data over time: an average over a window of years and the sum of past flows, which turns yearly trade balances into a stock of foreign assets or debt.

Python note. For a table whose rows are years, data.loc[start:end] keeps the rows from start to end, both included, and .mean() averages them (one average per column). .cumsum() is the cumulative sum: row by row, the sum of all values so far.

def domestic_saving(I, NX):
    """Domestic saving S = Y - C - G, from equation (19.6): S = I + NX."""
    return I + NX


def net_capital_outflow(S, I):
    """Equation (19.6): the trade balance equals domestic saving minus investment, NX = S - I."""
    return S - I


def period_average(data, start, end):
    """Simple average over the years start to end (rows are years): one number per column."""
    return data.loc[start:end].mean()


def accumulate(start_value, flows):
    """Net foreign assets: start from start_value and add each year's trade balance."""
    return start_value + flows.cumsum()
S = Y - C - G                                   # the economy of the quick check above
print(f"S = {S}, I = {I}: S - I = {net_capital_outflow(S, I)}, the same as NX = {NX}")
print(f"... and back again: S = I + NX = {domestic_saving(I, NX)}")
S = 15, I = 20: S - I = -5, the same as NX = -5
... and back again: S = I + NX = 15

Trade across time (Table 19.1)

Two islands grow wheat, which cannot be stored. Oddtopia has a harvest of 100 bushels in odd years and nothing in even years; Eventopia has the opposite luck. Each island lends half of a good harvest to the other and is repaid the next year, so both eat 50 bushels every year. With no investment and no government, \(NX = Y - C\): the harvest minus what is eaten. The sum of past trade balances is what the other island owes.

Each island runs a deficit in its bad years and a surplus in its good years, and both smooth their consumption perfectly: this is risk sharing. The average trade balance is zero: in the long run, trade must be balanced, because nobody lends without being repaid. A deficit today is a promise of a surplus later.

Comparative advantage: the North and the South (Tables 19.2 to 19.5)

Two countries consume two goods, apples and computers, and spend half of their income on each. Labor is the only input (Table 19.2). Prices are quoted in apples, so an apple costs 1, a computer costs \(p\) apples, and the wage \(w\) is in apples too. With half of income spent on each good, a worker eats \(w/2\) apples (19.3) and buys \(w/(2p)\) computers (19.4).

x_n, z_n, L_n = 160, 16, 100        # North: apples or computers per worker, number of workers (Table 19.2)
x_s, z_s, L_s = 100, 2, 400         # South
print(f"North / South productivity: apples {x_n / x_s:.1f} times, computers {z_n / z_s:.0f} times")
North / South productivity: apples 1.6 times, computers 8 times

The North is better at both goods: it has an absolute advantage in both. But its lead is much larger in computers (8 times) than in apples (1.6 times), so it has a comparative advantage in computers, and the South in apples.

Autarky (no trade). A worker can make \(x\) apples or \(z\) computers, so the wage is \(w = x\) apples, and both sectors pay the same only if a computer costs \(p = x/z\) apples. Half of the workers make each good.

Python note. pd.Series({...}) builds a list of numbers with labels from a dictionary. pd.DataFrame then puts two such lists side by side as the columns “North” and “South”. pd.concat([...], axis=1, keys=[...]) places two tables next to each other under two headings.

def autarky(x, z, L):
    """Table 19.3: a closed economy whose L workers each make x apples or z computers."""
    w = x                        # the wage in apples: what a worker makes in the apple sector
    p = x / z                    # the price of a computer: both sectors must pay the same wage
    return pd.Series({"Wage, w (apples)": w,
                      "Price of a computer, p (apples)": p,
                      "Apples per person": w / 2,              # equation (19.3), an apple costs 1
                      "Computers per person": w / 2 / p,       # equation (19.4)
                      "Workers making apples (%)": 50,
                      "Workers making computers (%)": 50,
                      "Apples produced": L / 2 * x,
                      "Computers produced": L / 2 * z})

Free trade. Computers cost 10 apples in the North and 50 in the South, so it pays to buy computers in the North and sell them in the South (arbitrage). Each country then makes only the good it is relatively good at: the North makes only computers and the South only apples. World spending on computers equals world spending on apples (each is half of world income), so \(p \times \text{computers} = \text{apples}\), and the world price is \(p = \text{apples} / \text{computers}\).

def free_trade(x_n, z_n, L_n, x_s, z_s, L_s):
    """Table 19.4: the North makes only computers, the South only apples, and they trade."""
    computers = L_n * z_n                      # world production of computers
    apples = L_s * x_s                         # world production of apples
    p = apples / computers                     # half of world spending goes to each good
    w_n, w_s = z_n * p, x_s                    # wages in apples
    north = pd.Series({"Wage, w (apples)": w_n, "Price of a computer, p (apples)": p,
                       "Apples per person": w_n / 2, "Computers per person": w_n / 2 / p,
                       "Workers making apples (%)": 0, "Workers making computers (%)": 100,
                       "Apples produced": 0, "Computers produced": computers})
    south = pd.Series({"Wage, w (apples)": w_s, "Price of a computer, p (apples)": p,
                       "Apples per person": w_s / 2, "Computers per person": w_s / 2 / p,
                       "Workers making apples (%)": 100, "Workers making computers (%)": 0,
                       "Apples produced": apples, "Computers produced": 0})
    return pd.DataFrame({"North": north, "South": south})

Goal. Follow how decades of trade deficits turned the United States from a lender into a debtor.

The net international investment position (NIIP) is what U.S. residents own abroad (foreign shares, bonds, factories) minus what foreigners own in the United States, at the end of each year. When it is negative, it is the net foreign debt. Each year’s trade deficit is new borrowing, so the position should fall by about the size of the deficit. Summary

  • Trade has become much more important: U.S. exports and imports have risen from about 4 percent of GDP each in 1950 to 11 and 14 percent. Since 1976 imports have exceeded exports every year, and the trade deficit reached 5.7 percent of GDP in 2006.
  • The national income identity shows what a deficit means: with \(NX < 0\), a country uses more than it produces (\(C + I + G > Y\)), and it invests more than it saves (\(NX = S - I\)). The deficit is borrowing from the rest of the world, and the sum of past deficits is the net foreign debt, about 70 percent of U.S. GDP today.
  • Trade can make everyone better off: across time it shares risks (Oddtopia and Eventopia), and across goods it lets countries specialize by comparative advantage (the North and the South). Free migration follows absolute advantage and can raise world output even more.
  • The data hold surprises: fast-growing economies tend to run surpluses, not deficits, and the large U.S. budget deficits of the 1980s and 2000s came with large trade deficits (the twin deficits). They also teach care: the textbook’s “2019” bars are 2017 data, and revisions changed the recent foreign debt by up to 16 points of GDP.
