Institutions and income

Act I

Capital and schooling are proximate causes; institutions may be a fundamental one

Proximate causes

  • Capital per worker
  • Schooling
  • TFP: how well a country uses both
  • Measured by the production model (Chapter 4, Appendix D)

Fundamental causes

  • Geography: climate, disease, soils
  • Culture: values and beliefs
  • Institutions: the rules of the game
  • Why some countries invest, school and produce more

Explaining TFP needs a cause behind the causes.

Proximate causes only push the question back: why do some countries invest so little, school their children so little, and use what they have so badly? The fundamental causes are the answers.

Douglass North (1990) called institutions “the rules of the game in a society”. Good economic institutions protect property, enforce contracts and give a broad part of the population the chance to invest. Bad ones let a small elite take what others produce.

Ask: which of the three fundamental causes can a government change? (Institutions, slowly; geography, not at all.)

Two natural comparisons point to institutions

North and South Korea

  • Same history, culture and geography until 1945
  • Different rules since then
  • The night-time satellite photograph of Chapter 4

Botswana

  • Kept secure property and stable rules after independence
  • Grew faster than almost any country for three decades
  • Many of its neighbours stagnated

Examples are vivid, but they are not proof.

These two cases are the motivating examples of Section G.1. They suggest that institutions matter, but two cases could be special for many reasons. We need many countries and a way to tell cause from correlation.

Ask: what else differs between Botswana and its neighbours? (Diamonds, a small population, a stable ruling party.) This is exactly the omitted-variables problem of Act II.

A tight correlation is not a causal effect

Rich countries have secure property, so secure property makes countries rich.

Rich countries can also afford honest courts, and a third factor, such as a healthy climate, can give a country both good institutions and high income. The correlation alone cannot tell which way the arrow points.

This is the problem that the whole appendix solves. The next act makes the three reasons precise: omitted variables, reverse causality and measurement error.

The model and the instrument

Act II

An archipelago where some islands protect their builders

Picture Islandia in an archipelago of island countries with the same boats and tools. On some islands, a boat a builder makes stays hers; on others, a chief can seize it.

  • Secure islands build more boats: institutions raise investment
  • Secure islands also try new ways to fish: institutions raise productivity
  • Each island also has its own soil, harbours, climate and luck

This is the Model overview of the appendix (Section G.3), in the book’s Islandia style. The next slides turn the story into the production model of Chapter 4 and the Solow model of Chapter 5.

The “own soil, harbours, climate and luck” line is the error term: everything else that moves income. Keep it in mind; it is where the trouble starts.

Institutions raise both productivity and the investment rate

\[y_i = \bar{A}_i\,k_i^{1/3} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y_i^* = \bar{A}_i^{3/2}\left(\frac{\bar{s}_i}{\bar{d}}\right)^{1/2}\]

Production per worker (Chapter 4) and the Solow steady state (Chapter 5)

\[\ln \bar{A}_i = a_0 + \bar{\phi}\,R_i + \varepsilon_i^A \qquad\qquad \ln \bar{s}_i = s_0 + \bar{\psi}\,R_i + \varepsilon_i^s\]

\(R_i\): how secure property is, 0 to 10. The \(\varepsilon\) terms: everything else that makes an island productive or thrifty.

Assumption 1 is the Cobb–Douglas production function with a capital share of one third. Assumption 2 is Solow: capital settles where investment s y just covers depreciation d k, which gives the steady state on the right. With α = 1/3, the exponent α/(1−α) is 1/2 and 1/(1−α) is 3/2.

Assumption 3 is the new part: institutions enter both TFP and the investment rate, linearly in logs. φ̄ and ψ̄ are the two effects.

The model gives a straight line between log income and institutions

\[\ln y_i^* = \tfrac{3}{2}\ln \bar{A}_i + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln \bar{s}_i - \tfrac{1}{2}\ln \bar{d}\]

\[\ln y_i = \mu + \beta R_i + u_i, \qquad \beta = \tfrac{3}{2}\bar{\phi} + \tfrac{1}{2}\bar{\psi}\]

\[\underset{\text{constant}}{\mu = \tfrac{3}{2}a_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}s_0 - \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\bar{d}} \qquad \underset{\text{everything else}}{u_i = \tfrac{3}{2}\varepsilon_i^A + \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_i^s}\]

The regression AJR estimate, and what its slope means.

Step 1: take logs of the steady state (the log of a product is the sum of logs; the log of a power is the exponent times the log; Appendix C).

Step 2: substitute the two rules for ln A and ln s and collect terms: those that are the same on every island go into μ, those with R into β R, the rest into u.

Productivity gets the larger weight, 3/2, for the same reason as in Chapter 5: higher TFP raises output directly, and the extra output is partly invested, which raises capital and output again. A higher investment rate works only through capital, with diminishing returns.

In Islandia’s baseline, one point of institutions multiplies income by 2.7

The baseline

  • \(\bar{\phi} = \bar{\psi} = 0.5\)
  • \(\beta = 0.75 + 0.25 =\) 1
  • One more point: \(e^{1} \approx\) 2.7 times as rich
  • Two more points: \(e^{2} \approx\) 7.4 times

Check yourself

  • Institutions raise productivity only: \(\bar{\phi} = 0.5\), \(\bar{\psi} = 0\). What is \(\beta\)?
  • \(\beta = 1.5 \times 0.5 = 0.75\): still large, through TFP alone

A few points of institutions can explain income gaps as large as those we see.

Write equation (1) for two islands and subtract: the constant cancels, and the income gap is β times the institutions gap plus the gap in u. So a 2-point gap is worth e^2 ≈ 7.4 on average, not exactly: the islands also differ in soil and luck.

The check question is from the “Check your understanding” box in Section G.3.1.

The OLS slope finds β only if institutions are unrelated to everything else

\[\widehat{b}_{OLS} = \frac{\text{Cov}(\ln y, R)}{\text{Var}(R)} = \beta + \frac{\text{Cov}(R, u)}{\text{Var}(R)}\]

A regressor that moves with the error term \(u\) is endogenous. Then OLS is biased, by the second term.

Derivation: put ln y = μ + βR + u into the covariance. Cov(μ, R) = 0, Cov(βR, R) = β Var(R), and the rest is Cov(u, R). Divide by Var(R).

Ask: in the Islandia story, what is in u? Soil, harbours, climate, luck. Is it plausible that none of them is related to the rules an island chose?

Three reasons why OLS can miss, in opposite directions

Problem The story Bias of OLS
Omitted variables a healthy climate raises income and lets good rules grow too large
Reverse causality rich islands can pay for honest judges too large
Measurement error we see an expert rating \(\widetilde{R} = R + m\), not the true \(R\) too small

Which bias wins is an empirical question, with a surprising answer.

The first two make Cov(R, u) positive: a lucky draw of u raises income, and through it (or alongside it) institutions.

The third is different: the index of expropriation risk is built from the judgment of experts, with an error m unrelated to everything else. Noise in the regressor flattens the line.

Hold back the answer: in AJR’s data, OLS turns out to be too small, so measurement error must be the strongest of the three. We find this in Act III.

Noise in the measure of institutions flattens the line

\[\widehat{b}_{OLS} = \beta\,\frac{\text{Var}(R)}{\text{Var}(R) + \text{Var}(m)}\]

  • The fraction is below one: attenuation bias
  • If the noise has half the variance of the true institutions: \(1 \times \dfrac{1}{1 + 0.5} =\) 0.67, a third of the effect lost
  • More countries do not help: the bias does not depend on the sample size

Derivation: regress ln y on the index R̃ = R + m. The numerator is Cov(ln y, R̃) = β Var(R), because m is unrelated to everything; the denominator is Var(R̃) = Var(R) + Var(m).

The third bullet is the second check question of Section G.3.2: more data make the OLS slope more precise, but precisely wrong. It settles on β plus the bias.

Two conditions make a piece of history an instrument

Long ago, deadly fevers on some islands killed most newcomers. Sailors set up trading posts with harsh rules there and left; on the healthy islands they settled and wrote rules that protected property. The rules outlived the sailors.

Relevance

The fevers \(Z_i\) changed institutions: \[R_i = \pi_0 + \pi Z_i + v_i, \quad \pi \neq 0\] Testable in the data

Exclusion

The old fevers have no other path to income today: \[\text{Cov}(Z, u) = 0\] Not testable: an argument about history

The first equation is the first stage, equation (5) of the appendix.

Exclusion is the hard part. It says the fevers that killed sailors long ago do not make today’s islanders less productive, except through the rules they left behind. Keep a list on the board of everything that could break it; Act III comes back to it.

The IV estimate is a ratio of two slopes

\[\ln y_i = (\mu + \beta\pi_0) + \beta\pi\,Z_i + (\beta v_i + u_i) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \rho = \beta\pi\]

\[\widehat{b}_{IV} = \frac{\widehat{\rho}}{\widehat{\pi}} = \frac{\text{Cov}(\ln y, Z)}{\text{Cov}(\widetilde{R}, Z)}\]

  • \(\widehat{\pi}\): how much the fevers moved institutions (the first stage)
  • \(\widehat{\rho}\): how much they moved income (the reduced form)
  • If the fevers reach income only through institutions, the ratio is \(\beta\)

Step 1: put the first stage into equation (1). Log income is a straight line in Z too, the reduced form, with slope ρ = βπ.

Step 2: divide. Each slope is a covariance with Z divided by Var(Z), which cancels.

Intuition for the room: it is a natural experiment. History “assigned” fevers to some islands. Compare the incomes of fever and healthy islands, and scale by how much their rules differ.

Why measurement error does not fool IV, and why two stages give the same number

Noise drops out

  • \(\widetilde{R} = R + m\) appears only in \(\text{Cov}(\widetilde{R}, Z)\)
  • The noise \(m\) is unrelated to the old fevers
  • So \(\text{Cov}(\widetilde{R}, Z) = \text{Cov}(R, Z)\): no flattening

Two-stage least squares

  1. Regress \(\widetilde{R}\) on \(Z\); keep the fitted \(\widehat{R}\)
  2. Regress \(\ln y\) on \(\widehat{R}\)
  • The slope is again \(\widehat{\rho}/\widehat{\pi}\)

IV uses only the part of institutions that the instrument created.

The 2SLS step: Cov(ln y, R̂) = π̂ Cov(ln y, Z) and Var(R̂) = π̂² Var(Z), so the second-stage slope is ρ̂/π̂.

This is also why IV can be larger than OLS: IV is not flattened by the noise in the index.

One caution for later: computing the second stage by hand gives the right coefficient but the wrong standard error.

A weak instrument turns a small problem into a large error

\[\widehat{b}_{IV} = \beta + \frac{\text{Cov}(Z, u)}{\text{Cov}(R, Z)}\]

  • If exclusion fails a little, the numerator is small but not zero
  • If the instrument barely moves institutions, the denominator is small too
  • A small number over a small number can be large: hence the first-stage F test in Act III

Put equation (1) into the numerator of the IV ratio. With perfect exclusion the second term is zero. With a slight violation, or plain bad luck in a sample of 64, the denominator decides how much damage it does.

Rule of thumb, used in Act III: a first-stage F statistic above 10 (Staiger and Stock 1997).

IV estimates a local effect

  • If institutions matter more in some countries than others, IV averages over the countries whose institutions the instrument moved
  • Here: former colonies whose rules were set by how deadly they were for settlers
  • A local average treatment effect (LATE; Imbens and Angrist 1994), not a statement about every country

IV trades the many assumptions of OLS for one strong one: exclusion.

This is the last idea of the model. Two reasons why IV and OLS can differ even with a valid instrument: measurement error (IV is not flattened) and locality (IV weights the countries the instrument moved).

Ask: does the effect for former colonies tell us the effect of a reform in, say, Argentina today? Come back to this on the policy slide.

More data do not fix a biased regression

With enough countries, OLS will find the true effect of institutions.

The bias of OLS does not depend on the number of countries. More data make OLS more precise around the wrong number; only a better design, such as a valid instrument, removes the bias.

Point back to the simulation: the OLS histograms were the narrow ones. With 200,000 islands (the notebook’s check), OLS lands on 0.67 with tiny noise, a third below the truth, while IV lands on 1.00.

Is the instrument strong enough?

The tests

  • First-stage \(F\) (robust): 16.85
  • Effective \(F\) (Olea and Pflueger 2013): 16.32
  • First-stage \(R^2\): 0.27

The thresholds

  • Rule of thumb: \(F > 10\) (Staiger and Stock 1997)
  • Stricter: 16.38 (Stock and Yogo 2005)

Strong enough, but not by a wide margin.

The F statistic tests that the instrument’s coefficient in the first stage is zero. It measures the denominator of the weak-instrument equation from Act II.

The Stock–Yogo threshold of 16.38 is for an instrument whose IV tests are at most a little distorted. AJR’s F sits right at it. With controls related to mortality, F falls, as the next chart shows.

The Wu–Hausman test says OLS is not enough

\[\text{Wu–Hausman statistic} = 24.22, \qquad p = 0.000007\]

  • The question: is the gap between OLS (0.52) and IV (0.94) too large to be chance?
  • The answer: yes. Institutions are endogenous, and IV is needed
  • The test assumes the instrument is valid: it compares OLS with IV, not IV with the truth

If institutions were not endogenous, OLS and IV would estimate the same thing, and OLS, being more precise, would be better. The test rejects that.

The third bullet is the most important: a Wu–Hausman rejection says nothing about exclusion.

A strong first stage does not make an instrument valid

The first stage is strong and the Wu–Hausman test rejects OLS, so the data confirm that settler mortality is a good instrument.

The data can test relevance, and compare OLS with IV. They cannot test exclusion with one instrument: whether mortality has no other path to income is an argument about history and health.

With more instruments than endogenous variables, an overidentification test (Hansen J) checks whether the instruments agree. AJR do this in their Table 8 (see Go further in the appendix). But agreeing witnesses can share the same mistake.

Why is IV larger than OLS?

  • Measurement error wins: the expropriation index is a noisy measure of broader, older institutions, so OLS is flattened (the “Measurement error only” scenario of the simulation)
  • A local effect: IV is the effect for colonies whose institutions settler mortality shaped; it can be larger than an average over all countries
  • Omitted variables and reverse causality push OLS up, so they must be weaker than the two above

The surprise of the paper: the naive regression understates the effect of institutions.

This resolves the puzzle from Act II: two biases push OLS up, one pulls it down, and the data say the downward pull is stronger.

Ask: which of the two explanations would you test first, and how? (A better measure of institutions; heterogeneous effects by region.)

Exclusion is the weak point: disease and human capital

Disease

  • The malaria that killed settlers still makes workers sick today
  • AJR control for malaria and life expectancy: the effect falls to about 0.6 to 0.7 but stays large
  • But health is partly an outcome of institutions

Human capital

  • Settlers brought skills and schooling, not only rules (Glaeser and coauthors 2004)
  • Then mortality reaches income through schooling too
  • The IV estimate would mix the two effects

The disease controls are AJR’s Table 7. Controlling for today’s health is a “bad control” problem: if institutions improve health, holding health constant hides part of their effect.

The Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes and Shleifer (2004) argument is that human capital is the more basic cause, and that institutions follow growth. Present it as an open debate.

The mortality data are old, and many numbers are borrowed

  • Albouy (2012): 36 of the 64 countries are assigned mortality rates from other countries, often neighbours
  • Rates from very different groups are combined: soldiers in barracks and on campaign, labourers, bishops
  • Correcting for this, the first stage weakens and the IV estimates become fragile
  • AJR (2012) reply: limiting the outliers, such as capping mortality at 250, leaves the results intact

An instrument is only as good as the data it is built from.

Albouy, “The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development: An Empirical Investigation: Comment”, AER 2012; AJR’s reply in the same issue. Both papers are in the Sources.

A data lesson for every policy student: check where every number in a key variable comes from before building on it.

Can a country raise its income by importing better institutions?

A development agency asks: if AJR are right, should aid be tied to reforms of property rights and courts?

What the estimate says

  • Institutions set by history have large effects on income
  • The effect works through TFP and investment
  • It is a long-run effect, over centuries

What it does not say

  • How to raise an expropriation score
  • How fast income would respond
  • Whether reforms imposed from outside work: they change rules in ways history did not

The IV estimate is a local effect of institutions that were set long ago. It does not identify the effect of a particular reform. Reforms can fail, and formal rules can differ from how power is actually used.

Ask the MPP students: what would a credible evaluation of an institutional reform look like?

Institutions, geography, or both?

A finance ministry must choose between spending on courts and governance or on disease control and infrastructure. Which matters more for income?

“Institutions rule”

  • Rodrik, Subramanian and Trebbi (2004): once institutions are instrumented, geography and trade add little directly
  • Consistent with AJR

“Institutions don’t rule”

  • Sachs (2003): malaria risk and geography lower income directly, beyond institutions
  • Consistent with the disease doubt about exclusion

Present this as a contested question. Both camps use instrumental variables on cross-country data; they differ on what they instrument, which controls they include, and what they believe about exclusion.

The honest summary for a ministry: the evidence that institutions matter a great deal is strong; the evidence that geography and health have no direct effect is not.

Maybe settlers brought themselves, not their institutions

A skeptic says: “Where Europeans settled, they brought their schooling, their skills and their health. Mortality predicts income because it predicts who lived there, not which rules they wrote.”

Reply: A serious challenge, and one instrument cannot settle it. Controlling for the European share of the population in 1900 barely moves the estimate (0.99), but that share is also a link of their chain, so the instrument weakens: \(F\) falls to about 5. The debate on human capital and institutions is still open.

Use this to build ethos: the key identifying assumption is strong, and the evidence for it is indirect.

The 0.99 and F ≈ 5 come from the notebook’s Your turn on Fig. 5 (control for euro1900). Controlling for a link of the chain takes away the variation the instrument works with.

Glaeser and coauthors (2004) make the argument in full.

Institutions are a candidate answer to the TFP puzzle

  • Chapter 4 and Appendix D: most income gaps come from TFP
  • The model of this appendix: institutions raise TFP and investment
  • AJR: the effect is large enough to explain much of the gaps across former colonies
  • Hall and Jones (1999), with the author of our textbook, reach a similar conclusion with a different instrument: social infrastructure

Institutions look like a fundamental cause of why some countries use their capital and workers so much better.

This ties the appendix back to the growth block of the book: accounting (Appendix D), convergence (Appendix E), the augmented Solow model (Appendix F), and now the deep causes.

What to remember from this appendix

  • In the growth models, log income is a straight line in institutions: \(\ln y = \mu + \beta R + u\), with \(\beta = \tfrac{3}{2}\bar{\phi} + \tfrac{1}{2}\bar{\psi}\)
  • OLS can miss: omitted variables and reverse causality push it up, measurement error pulls it down
  • An instrument that moves institutions and has no other path to income gives \(\beta\) as the ratio of two slopes
  • With settler mortality, AJR find 0.94, 80% above OLS, with a first-stage \(F\) of about 17; the result rests on exclusion and old data

One takeaway per act, plus the caveat. If time is short, keep the third bullet: it is the tool students will use again.

Walk the three branches in one minute each: institutions and income (a steep correlation, not yet a cause); the model and the instrument (a line, three biases, relevance and exclusion, a ratio of two slopes); AJR’s answer and its limits (first stage −0.61 with F about 17, IV 0.94, Nigeria ×8.6; exclusion, borrowed data, a local effect).
