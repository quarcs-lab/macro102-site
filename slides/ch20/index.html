No market is bigger than the market for money itself. What sets the price it clears at, why does that price jump when a central bank moves, and what does a country give up when it decides to fix it?

Open with the number. It is the notebook’s check of the textbook’s comparison: more than 5 trillion dollars of currencies are traded on a typical day, and U.S. GDP is about 25 trillion dollars a year, or 68 billion a day. The ratio is 73. The textbook rounds daily GDP up to 70 billion and says “more than 75 times”. Against world GDP (the U.S. is about 15 percent of it) the ratio is still 11.

Why it matters: in the short run the exchange rate is set in this market, by investors who move money between countries in seconds. In the long run it is pinned down by something much slower, the prices of goods. The chapter is about those two horizons and the policy choices between them.

Ask the room: who has changed money abroad recently? Did you get more or fewer euros, yen or pesos for a dollar than a few years ago? Why do you think it changed?

Today: three questions

  • What sets the exchange rate in the long run? Money, prices and the law of one price; why real exchange rates still drift.
  • What moves it in the short run? Interest rates, a flatter IS curve, foreign rates as a demand shock and the policy trilemma.
  • What happens when a country fixes it? Gold, Bretton Woods, currency crises and the euro crisis; then three policy choices.

These are the three acts of the deck and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide.

The headline result: in the long run, money and prices set the exchange rate; in the short run, interest rates move it. An open economy can have only two of a stable exchange rate, its own monetary policy and free capital flows, and the crises show what happens when that choice breaks.

The chapter builds on Chapter 8 (the quantity theory of money), Chapters 11 to 13 (the IS curve, monetary policy and AS/AD) and Chapter 19 (trade and the current account). Nothing in the short-run model has to be relearned: the open economy only adds a channel and a shock.

The law of one price ties the exchange rate to two price levels

\[EP = P^w \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar{E} = \frac{\bar{P}^w}{\bar{P}}\]

\[\bar{E} = \frac{\bar{M}^w}{\bar{M}}\times\frac{\bar{V}^w}{\bar{V}}\times\frac{\bar{Y}}{\bar{Y}^w}\]

The real exchange rate, \(RER \equiv EP/P^w\), the price of U.S. goods in foreign goods, equals 1 in the long run.

In the long run the exchange rate is the relative price of two piles of paper money.

The Model overview, first block. Assumption: arbitrage in goods. If a good is cheaper abroad, traders buy it there and ship it here until the gap closes. So once prices are written in one currency, a traded good costs the same everywhere: E P = P^w. Units: euros per dollar times dollars per good equals euros per good.

Apply it to the whole basket, so P and P^w are price levels, and use the quantity theory of Chapter 8 for each: P = M V / Y. Then the long-run exchange rate is the ratio of the two price levels, and each money supply enters with an elasticity of one. Doubling the number of dollars doubles U.S. prices and halves the value of the dollar (the notebook’s check: E from 0.92 to 0.46).

Intuition: a dollar is worth what it buys. If a basket costs 100 dollars here and 92 euros in Germany, a dollar is worth 0.92 euros.

The real exchange rate is the price of U.S. goods in foreign goods. The nominal rate is the price at which two currencies trade; the real rate is the price at which two countries’ goods trade. The law of one price sets the real rate to one in the long run.

Full derivation, with logs: the book chapter’s Model overview.

Under PPP, the dollar should lose value against the yen at the yearly rate of the inflation gap.

Step 4 of the Model overview. Take logs of E = P^w / P and subtract last year’s version. The change in a log is approximately a growth rate, and the growth rate of a price level is inflation. This is the growth rule for a ratio: g of X/Z is g of X minus g of Z.

The exact yearly version is E_t / E_(t−1) = (1 + π^w) / (1 + π). The notebook simulates it: starting at 250 yen, 245.2 after one year and 153.9 after 25 years, a fall to 0.615 of the start, exactly (1.02/1.04)^25. The real exchange rate stays at 1.000 throughout.

With the quantity theory, inflation is money growth minus output growth, so the same equation says that a country that prints money faster ends up with a weaker currency.

The second equation, for the real exchange rate, is the one we use on the data in a few slides: when the real rate changes, either the nominal rate moved more than the inflation gap or prices moved while the nominal rate stood still.

The number of units in a currency means nothing by itself

A dollar buys 162 yen but less than one euro, so the yen must be a weak currency and the euro a strong one.

The level of \(E\) only reflects units. A new currency worth 1,000 old ones changes \(E\) a thousandfold and nothing real. What matters is how \(E\) changes, and what goods it buys.

A frequent confusion, also in the press. In September 2026 a dollar bought 1,359 Korean won but only 0.82 Swiss francs (the notebook’s numbers). That does not make the won “weak” in any useful sense. Korea could replace 1,000 old won by one new won tomorrow; E would fall a thousandfold, and every price in Korea would be divided by 1,000 too. The real exchange rate, and every real variable, would be untouched: the classical dichotomy of the long run.

What does carry meaning: the change in E over time (the dollar depreciated against the yen from 1971 to 1995), and the real exchange rate EP/P^w, which compares what goods cost.

Ask: if a country drops three zeros from its currency overnight, what happens to its living standards that day? (Nothing.)

Next: one reason the real rate drifts, the Balassa-Samuelson effect.

Fast productivity growth in factories makes a whole country dearer

\[P_TA_T = P_NA_N = W \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P_N = P_T\,\frac{A_T}{A_N}\]

\[RER = \left(\frac{A_T/A_N}{A^w_T/A^w_N}\right)^{1-\theta}\]

With \(\theta = 1/2\) and a productivity edge in traded goods of 3 points a year: \(1.015^{24} \approx 1.43\), a real appreciation of 43% in 24 years.

Only traded goods obey the law of one price. Haircuts follow local wages, so rich countries look expensive.

The Balassa-Samuelson block of the Model overview, which writes the textbook’s verbal explanation as a small model in our own notation.

Assumptions: two sectors, traded (cars, phones) and nontraded (haircuts, restaurant meals), with output per worker A_T and A_N. Workers move between sectors, so both pay the same wage W, and competition makes the wage equal the value of what a worker produces. The law of one price holds for traded goods only. The price level is an average of the two prices with weight θ on traded goods.

Step 1: a haircut costs more where the barber could have earned more in a factory. Step 2: substitute into the price level. Step 3: the traded parts cancel in E P / P^w, and what remains is relative productivity.

Intuition: productivity grows fast in factories, wages rise across the whole economy; factory goods do not get dearer, because they compete with the world, but services do. A country whose traded-goods productivity grows faster than abroad sees its real exchange rate rise. Japan caught up fast until 1995.

The number is the notebook’s check: a gap of 3 points a year, θ = 1/2, 24 years: times 1.43.

A cheap burger does not prove a cheap currency

China’s Big Mac costs 30.9% less than the U.S. one, so the yuan is undervalued by 30.9% and must rise.

Part of the gap is the Balassa-Samuelson effect: in a poorer country, local labor and rent are cheap, so the price level is low even when the currency is fairly priced.

This myth appears in news stories every time the index is released. The Economist itself publishes a GDP-adjusted version of the index for this reason.

The correction uses the scatter: at China’s income, the fitted line predicts a cheaper burger than in the U.S. The right comparison is with countries of similar income, not with the U.S. Even then, the law of one price holds only for traded goods, and a Big Mac is mostly not traded.

The serious version of the claim, that China held its currency down in the 2000s, rests on other evidence (large current account surpluses and reserve accumulation behind capital controls), which Act II’s trilemma helps to read.

The short run: interest rates move the rate

Act II

In the long run goods set the exchange rate; in the short run, money markets do

Long run Short run
Nominal rate \(E\) relative price levels (quantity theory) set in currency markets; moves with the interest rate
Real rate \(EP/P^w\) equal to 1 (law of one price) moves with every surprise in \(E\): prices are sticky

Shipping goods takes months; moving money takes seconds. So in the short run the price of a currency follows the return on holding it.

This is the textbook’s Table 20.2, in our own words, and the hinge of the chapter.

Long run: the left column is Act I. Short run: inflation is sticky (the short-run model of Chapters 9 to 13), so over weeks P and P^w barely move. By equation (5), g_RER ≈ g_E + π − π^w ≈ g_E: any surprise move in the nominal rate is a move in the real rate.

Recall the hook: foreign exchange traded per day is 73 times U.S. daily GDP. Most of it is financial, not trade in goods. What do these investors care about? The return on holding each currency: the interest rate, and what they expect the currency to do.

Uncovered interest parity: two ways to invest a dollar must pay the same

\[1 + i_t = (1+i^w)\,\frac{E_t}{E^e_{t+1}}\]

\[\ln E_t \approx \ln E^e_{t+n} + n\,(i_t - i^w)\]

A U.S. rate 1 point higher for 5 years, with the anchor unchanged: the dollar jumps about 5% today (4.85% exactly).

A higher interest rate makes a currency stronger at once, by more the longer the gap is expected to last.

The UIP block of the Model overview. The textbook argues in words that higher U.S. rates make the dollar stronger; UIP is the standard equation behind that argument.

Assumptions: sticky prices; free financial flows; investors care only about the expected return; the expected future rate is anchored by PPP in the long run.

Step 1: kept at home, a dollar grows to 1 + i. Sent abroad, it becomes E euros, grows to E(1 + i^w), and is changed back at the expected rate E^e: E(1 + iw)/Ee dollars. Money flows into the better route until both pay the same: the notebook checks 1.0600 at home and 1.0600 abroad.

Step 2: solve for today’s rate and take logs; for a gap expected to last n years, the effect is n times the gap. Notebook: a gap lasting 1 year gives a jump of 0.95 percent; 5 years, 4.85 percent (the log approximation says 1 and 5).

The trilemma model of Fig. 7 uses a jump of 5 percent per point: a gap expected to last about 5 years.

With sticky inflation a higher nominal rate is also a higher real rate (Fisher), so the same chain runs from R: R up, E up, the real exchange rate up.

After the jump, the currency is expected to slide back

The Fed raised its rate, so investors expect the dollar to keep rising from here.

UIP says the reverse: \(g^e_E \approx i^w - i_t\). With higher U.S. rates, the dollar jumps now and must then be expected to lose value by the interest gap, so holding dollars pays no more than holding euros.

Step 3 of the UIP block. Rearrange UIP as E^e/E = (1 + i^w)/(1 + i). If U.S. bonds pay more, the dollar must be expected to depreciate from now on, by exactly the gap. The only way to have both a higher rate and an expected depreciation is an immediate jump up, then a slide back toward the anchor.

This is the same logic as overshooting in Dornbusch’s famous model, which the textbook does not cover. Mention it as a pointer for curious students.

Caveat for later (Devil’s advocate): in the data, high-interest currencies often do not depreciate as UIP predicts; that is why “carry trades” can pay for a while. UIP is a benchmark, not a law of nature.

Net exports add a second channel to the IS curve

\[\frac{NX_t}{\bar{Y}_t} = \bar{a}_{nx} - \bar{b}_{nx}\,(R_t - \bar{R}^w)\]

\[\widetilde{Y}\!_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R_t - \bar{r}) \qquad \bar{b} \equiv \bar{b}_i + \bar{b}_{nx}\] \[\bar{a} \equiv \bar{a}_c + \bar{a}_i + \bar{a}_g + \bar{a}_{nx} - 1 + \bar{b}_{nx}(\bar{R}^w - \bar{r})\]

Same form as the closed-economy IS curve: only the meaning of \(\bar{a}\) and \(\bar{b}\) is richer.

The open-economy IS block. Assumption 1: when U.S. goods are expensive (a high real exchange rate), exports are low and imports high. Assumption 2: by UIP, the real exchange rate rises with the U.S. real rate and falls with the foreign one. Together: net exports fall with the gap R − R^w. With b_nx = 0 we are back in Chapter 11.

Step 1, the trick: write R − R^w = (R − r̄) − (R^w − r̄). Now trade depends on the same gap as investment, plus a term that only the rest of the world can move.

Step 2 and 3: divide spending by potential output, substitute the shares, subtract one. The slope parameter b̄ now adds the trade channel to the investment channel; the shock ā now contains a demand shock from abroad, b_nx (R^w − r̄).

Why trade reacts like investment: national saving is used at home or lent abroad, S = I + NX (Chapter 19). A higher U.S. rate makes both uses less attractive at once.

The notebook’s baseline: b_i = 1 and b_nx = 1, so b̄ = 2, the value of Chapters 11 and 13, split half and half. The shares C 60 + I 19 + G 20 + NX 1 − 100 = 0: no shock at home.

Raising interest rates in an open economy

The Fed raises the real rate from \(\bar{r} = 5\%\) to 6%. In a closed economy with \(\bar{b}_i = 1\), output falls by 1%. With trade, \(\bar{b}_{nx} = 1\), how far does output fall? Is the IS curve steeper or flatter?

Give students a minute with the IS curve of the previous slide. The answer is a number: Ỹ’ = −(b_i + b_nx)(R’ − r̄).

A common wrong answer: “trade does not matter for interest rates”. Ask: what does a higher U.S. rate do to the dollar (UIP), and what does a stronger dollar do to exports?

Trade doubles the punch of a rate rise: output falls 2%, not 1%

  • The prediction: output falls by 2%, 1 point through investment and 1 through net exports.
  • The chain: \(\uparrow R \Rightarrow \uparrow E \Rightarrow \uparrow EP/P^w \Rightarrow \downarrow NX/\bar{Y}\). The IS curve, slope \(-1/\bar{b}\), is flatter.
  • The more net exports react, the more powerful monetary policy is: with \(\bar{b}_{nx} = 2\), a 1-point rise costs 3% of output.

In an open economy, monetary policy works through the exchange rate as well as through investment.

Reveal the prediction first: −2 percent, and a flatter curve. Numbers: the notebook’s slope check (−2.0 open, −1.0 closed) and the slider steps (b_nx = 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 give −1.0, −1.5, −2.0, −2.5, −3.0).

The “Your turn” of the book chapter: with b_i = 1, which b_nx makes a 1-point rise lower output by 3 percent? Answer: 2.

For the policy students: this is why central banks of small open economies watch the exchange rate so closely. Much of their policy works through it, and a currency that appreciates “on its own” tightens policy without any decision.

A credible peg ties the interest rate to the anchor country

\[E_t = E^e_{t+1} = E_{fix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad i_t = i^w\]

\[1 + i_t = (1+i^w)\,\frac{E_{fix}}{E^e_{t+1}} \qquad i_t \approx i^w + \text{expected depreciation}\]

With \(i^w = 5\%\) and an expected devaluation of 10%: \(i_t = 1.05/0.90 - 1 \approx\) 16.7% to keep investors holding the currency.

Fix the exchange rate and keep money free to cross borders, and you give up your own interest rate.

The fixed-exchange-rate block. Assumptions: the central bank promises to trade at E_fix and holds foreign reserves to keep the promise; investors believe it; flows are free, so UIP holds.

Step 1: put E_t = E^e into UIP; the ratio is one, so i = i^w. When the Fed raises its rate, the central bank of a pegging country must follow, whatever its own economy needs.

Step 2: in the long run, PPP with a fixed E makes inflation equal to inflation abroad, and money growth must match. That is why a peg can import a trusted central bank, and why it breaks when a government prints money to pay its bills (Chapter 8).

Step 3, the gold standard: each country fixed the price of an ounce of gold, so the exchange rate was the ratio of the two gold prices. Act III computes it.

Step 4, a peg that markets doubt: the notebook’s check, 16.67 percent (the rough rule says 5 + 10 = 15). If the central bank will not raise its rate that far, investors swap the currency for reserves, and when reserves run out the peg breaks: Mexico 1994, Asia 1997, Argentina 2002.

Does a floating exchange rate buy monetary independence?

Small open economies float to keep their own interest rate. But when the Fed tightens, capital flows out of them anyway. How much freedom does a float really give?

What the model predicts

  • A float lets you set \(i\) for your own economy.
  • The exchange rate absorbs the foreign shock.
  • A hard peg must copy the anchor’s rate.

What is debated

  • A global financial cycle may move credit even under floats.
  • Do partial capital controls help, or leak?
  • Currency falls hurt dollar borrowers.

The trilemma says floating restores autonomy. The history supports it: Obstfeld, Shambaugh and Taylor (2005, Review of Economics and Statistics) show, across the gold standard, the interwar years, Bretton Woods and the float, that pegs with open capital markets followed the base country’s interest rate much more closely than floats did.

The challenge: Rey (2013, Jackson Hole symposium), “dilemma not trilemma”, argues that a global financial cycle driven by U.S. monetary policy moves capital flows, credit and asset prices even in countries with floating rates, so true independence may need capital-flow management, whatever the exchange rate regime.

The middle ground: Klein and Shambaugh (2015, American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics) find that floats and wide capital controls give some autonomy, while limited, partial controls give little. The IMF’s “institutional view” (2012) accepts temporary capital-flow measures in some circumstances.

Name it as contested: the size of the global cycle, and how much a float insulates, are active research questions.

Ask the policy students: if you advised a central bank in a small emerging economy with large dollar debts, would you let the currency fall when the Fed tightens?

Should the central bank stand behind government debt?

In July 2012, the ECB’s president promised to do “whatever it takes” to preserve the euro. Spreads fell before any bond was bought. Was that the right promise?

What the model predicts

  • A credible buyer lowers \(p\), so \(i\) and the bill fall.
  • It can stop a self-fulfilling run.
  • A country with its own currency has this backstop.

What is debated

  • Moral hazard: less pressure to fix budgets.
  • Who bears the losses if a state defaults?
  • Is it monetary policy or fiscal aid?

The facts: Mario Draghi’s London speech of 26 July 2012; the ECB then announced Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT), conditional bond purchases, in September 2012. OMT was never used, yet spreads fell (Fig. 9).

The case for: De Grauwe (2012, Australian Economic Review) argues that members of a monetary union issue debt in a currency they do not control, so they are prone to self-fulfilling liquidity crises that stand-alone countries with their own central bank avoid. De Grauwe and Ji (2013, Journal of International Money and Finance) find that a large part of the rise in southern spreads in 2010–2011 was not explained by fundamentals such as debt ratios, consistent with panic, and that spreads fell after the ECB’s announcement.

The case against: moral hazard (governments relax once the backstop exists) and the legal question of whether a central bank may finance states. OMT was challenged in the German Constitutional Court; the EU Court of Justice upheld it in 2015 (Gauweiler).

Name it as contested: how much of the 2010–2012 spreads was panic and how much fundamentals is still debated.

Can a currency union work without exchange rates between its members?

Greece and Germany share one interest rate and one currency. When one booms and the other slumps, what adjusts instead of the exchange rate?

What theory asks of a union

  • Workers who move to where the jobs are.
  • Wages and prices that can fall.
  • Shared budgets that send money to slumping regions.

What the euro had

  • Little labor mobility across borders.
  • Slow wage adjustment (Fig. 13).
  • No large shared budget until 2020.

The optimum currency area: Mundell (1961, American Economic Review) asked when a region should share a currency. His answer: when shocks hit its parts alike, or when factors (above all labor) move freely among them. Later work added wage flexibility and fiscal transfers.

Krugman (2013, NBER Macroeconomics Annual, “Revenge of the Optimum Currency Area”) argues that the euro crisis played out as the theory predicted: large asymmetric shocks (the credit boom and bust of Figs. 9 to 12), little labor mobility, no fiscal union, so adjustment had to come through slow internal devaluation and high unemployment. The U.S. states share a dollar but also a federal budget and a mobile labor force.

Other side: the euro also brought benefits (no currency crises inside it, lower transaction costs, credibility for high-inflation members), and the union has added tools since: the ECB’s backstop, a banking union, and the joint recovery fund agreed in 2020 (NextGenerationEU). The right-hand column’s last bullet refers to that.

“Exchange-rate models can’t even beat a coin toss”

A skeptic says: “Exchange rates swing every day for no visible reason. Famous studies found that no model forecasts them better than a random walk, and high-interest currencies don’t depreciate as interest parity says. Why teach theories that fail?”

Reply: the theories are not day-trading tools. They explain what forecasts cannot: over decades low-inflation currencies gained (Fig. 2), pegs that copy the wrong interest rate break (Fig. 8), and spreads track default fear (Fig. 9). The model says which forces move the rate, not where it will be next month.

Take the objection seriously: it is right on the facts. Meese and Rogoff (1983, Journal of International Economics) found that the standard exchange-rate models of the 1970s forecast no better than a random walk at horizons up to a year, even when given the actual future values of their fundamentals. Fama (1984, Journal of Monetary Economics) found that currencies with high interest rates tend, if anything, to appreciate, the opposite of UIP: the “forward premium puzzle” behind carry trades.

The reply uses the deck. PPP fails in the short run but its direction holds over decades: Act I. Rogoff (1996, Journal of Economic Literature), “The Purchasing Power Parity Puzzle”, documents that deviations from PPP do die out, but only slowly, over years. The trilemma and the peg arithmetic explain the crises of Act III, which are the events policymakers care most about. And the open-economy IS curve gives the right sign of a monetary policy shock.

The honest summary: in the short run exchange rates are hard to forecast because they are asset prices driven by expectations, like stock prices; the models tell us how they respond to policy and why they return toward fundamentals in the long run.

What to remember from this chapter

  • The nominal rate \(E\) is the price of a currency; the real rate \(EP/P^w\) is the price of goods. In the long run \(\bar{E} = \bar{P}^w/\bar{P}\) and \(g_E \approx \pi^w - \pi\).
  • The law of one price holds only loosely: Japan’s real rate rose 2.5-fold to 1995, and rich countries look expensive (Balassa-Samuelson).
  • In the short run UIP moves \(E\) with interest rates. Net exports flatten the IS curve, and foreign rates are a demand shock at home.
  • The trilemma: at most two of a stable rate, monetary autonomy and free flows. Gold, Bretton Woods, pegs and the euro are different choices of two.
  • When a choice breaks, currencies collapse or spreads explode; inside a union, the cure is a slow fall in unit labor costs.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: what sets the price of a currency, and what does a country give up when it fixes it? A good answer: in the long run relative price levels, set by money; in the short run interest rates and expectations. A country that fixes its rate with open capital markets gives up its own monetary policy, and must defend the peg with interest rates and reserves.

For the policy students, three habits: write down the units of an exchange rate before you interpret a change; ask which two corners of the triangle a country has chosen; and when a peg or a union is under stress, ask what will adjust instead of the exchange rate.
