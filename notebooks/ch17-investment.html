Learning goals

Investment links the present to the future: a firm that buys a machine today gives up money now for output later. One tool, the arbitrage equation, explains investment in machines, in stocks and in houses. By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  • use an arbitrage equation to compare two ways of investing the same money;
  • compute the user cost of capital and find the desired capital stock, where the marginal product of capital equals the user cost;
  • show how a corporate income tax raises the user cost and lowers capital and the investment rate, and rebuild Table 17.1 for any year;
  • price a stock as the present value of its dividends, \(\text{dividend}/(R-g)\), and use this formula to read the price-earnings ratio of the stock market;
  • describe the components of U.S. private investment and how they move in recessions;
  • write Python functions with default values, draw Plotly figures with sliders and buttons, reshape tables with rename, melt and merge, and repeat steps with for and while loops.

def slider_figure(make_figure, name, values, start, label=None):
    """Precompute make_figure(name=value) for every value and join the results with one slider.

    The website cannot run Python, so each interactive figure also gets this precomputed version.
    """
    figures = [make_figure(**{name: v}) for v in values]
    first = values.index(start)
    fig = go.Figure(layout=figures[first].layout)
    owner = []                                  # which precomputed figure each trace comes from
    for i, f in enumerate(figures):
        for trace in f.data:
            trace.visible = (i == first)
            fig.add_trace(trace)
            owner.append(i)
    steps = []
    for i, f in enumerate(figures):
        layout = {"title": f.layout.title.to_plotly_json(),
                  "annotations": [a.to_plotly_json() for a in f.layout.annotations],
                  "shapes": [s.to_plotly_json() for s in f.layout.shapes]}
        visible = [o == i for o in owner]
        steps.append(dict(method="update", label=str(values[i]), args=[{"visible": visible}, layout]))
    fig.update_layout(sliders=[dict(active=first, steps=steps, pad=dict(t=60),
                                    currentvalue=dict(prefix=(label or name) + " = "))])
    return fig

The model in code

The arbitrage equation. Gino’s pizza restaurant has some cash. It can put the money in the bank and earn the interest rate \(R\), or it can buy a pizza oven at the price \(p_k\), use it for a year and sell it. If Gino’s maximizes profits, the last dollar must earn the same in both uses, otherwise moving money to the better use would raise profits:

\[\underbrace{R\cdot p_k}_{\text{bank account}} = \underbrace{MPK + \Delta p_k}_{\text{pizza oven}} \qquad (17.2)\]

The oven earns its marginal product \(MPK\), plus a capital gain \(\Delta p_k\) when its price rises (a capital loss when it falls). With the price normalized to 1 and depreciation \(\bar{d}\) written out explicitly, the firm invests until

\[MPK = \underbrace{R + \bar{d} - \frac{\Delta p_k}{p_k}}_{\text{user cost of capital}} \qquad (17.4)\]

The user cost of capital is the full cost of using one more unit of capital for a year: the interest given up, the wear and tear, minus any rise in the price of the capital good. A corporate income tax at rate \(\tau\) takes part of the marginal product, so the marginal product must be higher:

\[MPK = \frac{R + \bar{d} - \Delta p_k/p_k}{1-\tau} \qquad (17.6)\]

Diminishing returns. With the production function of Chapter 4, \(Y = \bar{A}K^{1/3}L^{2/3}\), the marginal product of capital falls as capital rises:

\[MPK = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{Y}{K} = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\bar{A}\left(\frac{L}{K}\right)^{2/3} \qquad (17.7)\]

(The textbook’s text prints \(L^{1/2}\) here, a typo: equation 17.7 needs the exponent \(2/3\).) Setting \(MPK\) equal to the user cost \(uc\) gives the desired capital stock, \(K^{\text{desired}} = L\,(\bar{A}/(3\,uc))^{3/2}\), and the capital-output ratio \(Y/K = 3\cdot uc\) (17.9).

Our baseline. The textbook’s own example has a 2 percent real interest rate, an 8 percent depreciation rate and no capital gain, so the user cost is 10 percent. We add \(\bar{A} = 0.3\) and \(L = 1\), chosen so that the desired capital stock is exactly \(K = 1\) (and output \(Y = 0.3\)). Figures 1 and 2 both start from this baseline.

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. Writing gpk=0.0 gives the argument a default value, used whenever we do not pass one. The text in triple quotes is a short description of what the function does. ** means “to the power of”. Names in capital letters, such as A_BAR, are constants that we set once and reuse.

A_BAR, L_BAR = 0.3, 1.0          # illustrative productivity and labor: the baseline desired capital is K = 1


def user_cost(R, d, gpk=0.0, tau=0.0):
    """User cost of capital: (R + d - gpk) / (1 - tau)   (equations 17.4 and 17.6; gpk is the capital gain)."""
    return (R + d - gpk) / (1 - tau)


def mpk(K, A=A_BAR, L=L_BAR):
    """Marginal product of capital: MPK = (1/3) A (L/K)^(2/3)   (equation 17.7)."""
    return A / 3 * (L / K) ** (2 / 3)


def desired_capital(uc, A=A_BAR, L=L_BAR):
    """Desired capital stock, where MPK = uc: K = L (A / (3 uc))^(3/2)   (from equation 17.7)."""
    return L * (A / (3 * uc)) ** (3 / 2)


def investment_rate(uc, g_K=0.02, d=0.08):
    """Investment rate: I/Y = (g_K + d) / (3 uc)   (equation 17.13)."""
    return (g_K + d) / (3 * uc)

The same arbitrage idea prices a stock. An investor can earn \(R\) in the bank, or buy a stock at the price \(p_s\) and receive its dividend plus the capital gain. The two returns must be equal:

\[R = \frac{\text{dividend}}{p_s} + \frac{\Delta p_s}{p_s} \qquad (17.15)\]

When dividends grow at a constant rate \(g\), the stock price grows at \(g\) too, and solving for the price gives

\[p_s = \frac{\text{dividend}}{R - g} \qquad (17.16)\]

Dividing by the earnings of the firm gives the price-earnings ratio, which depends on the share of earnings paid out as dividends (the payout ratio) and on \(R-g\):

\[\frac{p_s}{\text{earnings}} = \frac{\text{dividend}/\text{earnings}}{R - g} \qquad (17.17)\]

def stock_price(dividend, R, g=0.0):
    """Price of a stock: dividend / (R - g)   (equation 17.16; dividends grow at rate g)."""
    return dividend / (R - g)


def pe_ratio(payout, R, g):
    """Price-earnings ratio: (dividend / earnings) / (R - g)   (equation 17.17)."""
    return payout / (R - g)

Drawing tools

Figures 1 and 2 are the same kind of diagram, so we write its drawing steps once:

  • capital_figure starts a diagram with fixed axes, so that you can see the curves move;
  • add_mpk draws the marginal product curve, and add_level draws a flat user-cost line; both write the name of the line on the plot, as the textbook does, and add_label skips a name that would sit on top of another one;
  • add_point marks a desired capital stock with a dashed guide to the axis, and add_arrow draws an arrow.

Python note. np.linspace(0.05, K_max, 300) creates 300 evenly spaced numbers from 0.05 to K_max, the right end of the x-axis, which fig.layout.xaxis.range[1] reads from the figure. Because mpk works on a whole array at once, one call gives the whole curve. go.Figure() starts an empty chart, and each add_trace adds one line or set of points. MPK <= 0.4 is true where the curve is inside the axes, K[inside] keeps those values, and [0] takes the first of them. A for loop over fig.layout.annotations looks at every label already written, and return inside the loop stops the function early.

K_RANGE, RETURN_RANGE = [0, 3], [0, 0.4]          # fixed axes of Figures 1 and 2


def capital_figure(title):
    """An empty diagram: capital on the x-axis, returns per unit of capital (percent) on the y-axis."""
    fig = go.Figure()
    fig.update_layout(title=title, showlegend=False,
                      xaxis=dict(title="Capital, K", range=K_RANGE, tickvals=[0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3], zeroline=False),
                      yaxis=dict(title="Return per unit of capital", range=RETURN_RANGE,
                                 tickvals=[0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4], tickformat=".0%", zeroline=False))
    return fig


def add_label(fig, x, y, text, color, xanchor="left"):
    """Write a line name at (x, y), unless another name already sits within 2 points of it."""
    for old in fig.layout.annotations:
        if old.showarrow is False and abs(old.x - x) < 0.5 and abs(old.y - y) < 0.02:
            return
    fig.add_annotation(x=x, y=y, text=text, showarrow=False, xanchor=xanchor, yanchor="bottom",
                       font=dict(color=color, size=14))
def add_mpk(fig, A=A_BAR):
    """Draw the MPK curve of equation (17.7) and write its name next to its top-left end."""
    K_max = fig.layout.xaxis.range[1]                     # the right end of the x-axis
    K = np.linspace(0.05, K_max, 300)
    MPK = mpk(K, A)
    inside = MPK <= RETURN_RANGE[1]                       # the part of the curve inside the axes
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=K[inside], y=MPK[inside], mode="lines", name="MPK", line=dict(color=BLUE, width=3)))
    add_label(fig, K[inside][0] + 0.05, MPK[inside][0] - 0.03, "Marginal product of capital, MPK", BLUE)


def add_level(fig, uc, label, color, dash="solid"):
    """Draw a flat user-cost line at the height uc and write its name at its right end."""
    K_max = fig.layout.xaxis.range[1]
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[0, K_max], y=[uc, uc], mode="lines", name=label, line=dict(color=color, width=3, dash=dash)))
    add_label(fig, K_max, uc, label, color, xanchor="right")


def add_point(fig, K, uc, label, anchor="left"):
    """Mark (K, uc) with a dot and a dashed guide down to the x-axis, labelled next to the guide."""
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[K, K], y=[0, uc], mode="lines", hoverinfo="skip",
                             line=dict(color="black", width=1, dash="dash")))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[K], y=[uc], mode="markers", name=label, marker=dict(color="black", size=11)))
    fig.add_annotation(x=K, y=0, text=label, showarrow=False, xanchor=anchor, yanchor="bottom", font=dict(size=14))


def add_arrow(fig, x0, y0, x1, y1, text=""):
    """Draw an arrow from (x0, y0) to (x1, y1), with an optional text at its tail; nothing if the points almost coincide."""
    if abs(x1 - x0) > 0.03 or abs(y1 - y0) > 0.005:
        fig.add_annotation(x=x1, y=y1, ax=x0, ay=y0, xref="x", yref="y", axref="x", ayref="y", text=text,
                           showarrow=True, arrowhead=3, arrowwidth=2, arrowcolor="black")

Figure 1: How much should a firm invest?

Goal. Find the desired capital stock of a firm, and see how the interest rate, depreciation, capital gains and productivity change it.

The marginal product of capital falls as the firm adds capital: the first pizza oven adds a lot of pizzas, the tenth adds few. The user cost does not depend on how much capital the firm has, so it is a flat line. The firm keeps investing as long as one more unit of capital earns more than it costs, so it stops where the two lines cross, at \(K^{\text{desired}}\).

Investment is what moves the firm toward that point. The capital accumulation equation says that next year’s capital is this year’s investment plus the capital that did not wear out:

\[K_{t+1} = I_t + (1-\bar{d})\,K_t \qquad (17.8)\]

So a young firm that starts with \(K_0\) and wants \(K^{\text{desired}}\) next year must invest \(I = K^{\text{desired}} - (1-\bar{d})K_0\). The title shows this number. The arrow points from \(K_0\) to the desired capital stock.

Python note. if K_star > 2.6: runs the indented lines below it only when the condition is true. Here it widens the x-axis (update_xaxes) in the rare case of a very low user cost, when the desired capital stock would otherwise fall off the chart; tickvals=None lets Plotly choose the tick marks. round(x, 2) rounds a number to two decimals, and adding 0.0 turns a rounded -0.0 into 0.0, so the title never shows “-0.00”.

def desired_capital_diagram(R=0.02, d=0.08, gpk=0.0, A=A_BAR, K0=0.5):
    """Figure 17.1: invest until the marginal product of capital falls to the user cost."""
    uc = user_cost(R, d, gpk)
    K_star = desired_capital(uc, A)
    I = round(K_star - (1 - d) * K0, 2) + 0.0        # investment that reaches K_star next year, equation (17.8)
    fig = capital_figure(f"User cost {uc:.1%}  |  desired capital {K_star:.2f}  |  investment from K0 = {K0:.1f}: {I:+.2f}")
    if K_star > 2.6:                                 # a very low user cost: widen the x-axis so K desired stays on it
        fig.update_xaxes(range=[0, 1.15 * K_star], tickvals=None)
    add_mpk(fig, A)
    add_level(fig, uc, "User cost: R + d − Δp/p", TEAL)
    add_point(fig, K_star, uc, "K<sup>desired</sup>")
    add_arrow(fig, K0, 0.03, K_star, 0.03, text="K<sub>0</sub>")
    return fig

What to notice.

  • In the baseline the user cost is 10 percent and the desired capital stock is 1. A firm that starts with \(K_0 = 0.5\) must invest 0.54: half a unit to grow, and 0.04 to replace the capital that wears out.
  • A higher interest rate or faster depreciation raises the user cost and moves \(K^{\text{desired}}\) to the left. Because the MPK curve flattens out, a rise of 1 point in the user cost lowers desired capital by more when the user cost is low.
  • A capital gain lowers the user cost. Structures, whose land gets scarcer, often gain in value; computers lose value quickly, so their user cost is high. Set \(\Delta p/p = -0.04\): desired capital falls to 0.60.
  • Higher productivity \(\bar{A}\) shifts the MPK curve up, so the firm wants more capital at the same user cost.

Figure 2: An increase in the corporate income tax

Goal. See why a corporate income tax lowers the capital stock that firms want.

The tax takes a share \(\tau\) of what each unit of capital earns, so the firm keeps only \((1-\tau)MPK\). To make an investment worthwhile, the marginal product before tax must now cover the user cost divided by \(1-\tau\) (equation 17.6). The user-cost line shifts up, and the firm moves up its MPK curve to a smaller capital stock. With the textbook’s numbers, a 35 percent tax raises the user cost from 10 to about 15 percent.

def corporate_tax_diagram(tau=0.35, R=0.02, d=0.08):
    """Figure 17.2: a corporate income tax raises the user cost and lowers the desired capital stock."""
    uc0, uc1 = user_cost(R, d), user_cost(R, d, tau=tau)
    K0, K1 = desired_capital(uc0), desired_capital(uc1)
    fig = capital_figure(f"User cost {uc0:.1%} → {uc1:.1%}  |  desired capital {K0:.2f} → {K1:.2f} ({K1 / K0 - 1:+.0%})")
    add_mpk(fig)
    add_level(fig, uc1, "User cost (with a tax)", TEAL)
    add_level(fig, uc0, "User cost (no tax)", GRAY, dash="dash")      # the old line, dashed
    add_point(fig, K0, uc0, "K<sup>initial</sup>")
    add_point(fig, K1, uc1, "K<sup>desired</sup>", anchor="right")
    add_arrow(fig, 0.06, uc0, 0.06, uc1)                              # the user cost shifts up
    add_arrow(fig, K0, 0.06, K1, 0.06)                                # desired capital falls
    return fig

What to notice.

  • With \(\tau = 0.35\) the user cost rises from 10.0 to 15.4 percent, and the desired capital stock falls by about half, from 1.00 to 0.52.
  • The capital stock falls by much more than 35 percent. Desired capital is proportional to \(uc^{-3/2}\), so it falls by the factor \((1-\tau)^{3/2} = 0.65^{1.5} \approx 0.52\).
  • A higher interest rate moves both user-cost lines up, and the tax multiplies every change in \(R\) by \(1/(1-\tau)\).

Key numbers: from desired capital to investment

After the tax, the firm has more capital than it wants. Equation (17.8) says it can simply stop investing and let depreciation do the work. How many years does that take?

Python note. A while loop repeats its indented lines as long as its condition is true. Here each pass is one year in which the capital stock shrinks by the depreciation rate, and years += 1 adds one to the counter.

K_initial = desired_capital(user_cost(0.02, 0.08))
K_target = desired_capital(user_cost(0.02, 0.08, tau=0.35))
K, years = K_initial, 0
while K > K_target:
    K = (1 - 0.08) * K                  # no new investment: equation (17.8) with I = 0
    years += 1

def tax_table(year=2022):
    """Table 17.1 for one year: tax rates and user costs, with the U.S. user cost fixed at 10 percent."""
    table = taxes[taxes.year == year].copy()
    tau_us = table[table.country == "United States"].cit_rate.iloc[0] / 100
    uc_no_tax = 0.10 * (1 - tau_us)                       # the user cost before taxes
    table["user_cost"] = 100 * uc_no_tax / (1 - table.cit_rate / 100)       # equation (17.6)
    table = book.merge(table[["country", "cit_rate", "user_cost"]], on="country")
    names = {"book_rate": "tax rate, book", "cit_rate": f"tax rate, {year}",
             "book_user_cost": "user cost, book", "user_cost": f"user cost, {year}"}
    return table.rename(columns=names).round(1)

Key numbers: house prices and leverage

The arbitrage equation also prices a house. An investor can keep the down payment in the bank, or buy a condominium with a mortgage, rent it out for a year and sell it. Equating the two returns gives

\[p^{\text{house}} = \frac{\text{rent}}{\tau\bar{x}R + (1-\tau)R + \bar{d} - \Delta p^{\text{house}}/p^{\text{house}}} \qquad (17.20)\]

where \(\bar{x}\) is the down payment as a share of the price and \(\tau\) the tax rate at which mortgage interest can be deducted. We use illustrative numbers: a rent of 6,000 dollars a year, \(R = 4\) percent, \(\bar{d} = 2\) percent, \(\tau = 0.3\) and expected price growth of 2 percent.

def house_price(rent, R, d, gp, tau, x):
    """Price of a house: rent / (tau x R + (1 - tau) R + d - gp)   (equation 17.20; gp is the expected price growth)."""
    return rent / (tau * x * R + (1 - tau) * R + d - gp)


base = house_price(6000, 0.04, 0.02, 0.02, 0.3, 0.2)
print(f"Baseline price (20% down payment, 2% price growth): {base:,.0f} dollars")
print(f"Down payment falls to 10%:          {house_price(6000, 0.04, 0.02, 0.02, 0.3, 0.1) / base - 1:+.1%}")
print(f"Expected price growth rises to 3%:  {house_price(6000, 0.04, 0.02, 0.03, 0.3, 0.2) / base - 1:+.1%}")

Summary

  • The arbitrage equation says that two equally safe investments must earn the same return. Applied to physical capital, it says that firms invest until the marginal product of capital falls to the user cost of capital, \(R + \bar{d} - \Delta p_k/p_k\).
  • A corporate income tax divides the user cost by \(1-\tau\). With diminishing returns, a 35 percent tax cuts the desired capital stock by about half and the investment rate by 35 percent.
  • Applied to stocks, the same equation gives \(p_s = \text{dividend}/(R-g)\): a stock is worth the present value of its dividends. The P/E ratio then depends on \(R-g\), which makes it very sensitive to expected growth and hard to use for spotting bubbles.
  • In the data, total investment and especially housing and inventories fall sharply in recessions.
