Work is the main source of income for most people. What sets its price, why do some people who want a job not find one, and why are some paid so much more than others?

Open with the number. Both rates come from the notebook’s Fig. 2 key numbers: 3.5 percent in February 2020 and 14.8 percent in April 2020, the highest peak in the chart of this act.

Ask the room: how many of you, or of your families, were affected? Most people live mainly on what they earn from work, so the labor market is the market that matters most to them.

Link back: Chapters 3 to 6 explained income per person, which is mostly the wage. This chapter looks inside the labor market itself: how many people work, at what wage, and why some cannot find a job. It is the last long-run chapter before inflation, and the rigid wages here come back in the short-run chapters.

Today: three questions

  • Who works, and at what wage? The facts on who has a job, and a supply-and-demand model of the labor market with taxes and demand shocks.
  • Why are some people unemployed? Recessions, wages that do not fall, the flows into and out of jobs, and Europe.
  • Why do some earn more? What a working life is worth, the rising college premium, manufacturing jobs and inequality.

These are the three acts of the deck and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide. Act I builds the supply-and-demand model and uses it for an income tax and a fall in labor demand. Act II adds the two ways the model produces unemployment: a wage that does not fall, and the constant churn of job losses and job findings. Act III uses the same model twice, in the markets for high school and college graduates, and ends with the data on inequality.

The headline result: wages and employment follow supply and demand; the natural rate of unemployment follows the flows of job losses and job findings; and the college premium rose because the demand for skills grew faster than their supply.

Who works, and at what wage

Act I

Every adult is employed, unemployed or out of the labor force

Ratio Formula What it measures
unemployment rate \(u = U/LF\) the share of the labor force that looks for work and has none
participation rate \(LF/N\) the share of adults who work or want to work
employment-population ratio \(E/N\) the share of all adults who have a job

The labor force is \(LF = E + U\). Students, retirees, carers and people who stopped searching are outside it.

The definitions follow the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics: N is the population aged 16 and over; E has a paid job; U has no job, has looked for one in the past four weeks and could start now. Everyone else is not in the labor force.

The key word is “searching”. A person who wants a job but has stopped looking is not counted as unemployed. These are the discouraged workers, and they matter for the next slide.

Ask: is a full-time student who works ten hours a week employed? (Yes. Any paid work counts.) Is a retiree who would take a job if offered one unemployed? (No, unless she is actively searching.)

Three statistics, one identity

\[\frac{E}{N} = (1-u)\times\frac{LF}{N}\]

100 adults Before One gives up searching
labor force \(LF\) 62 61
unemployed \(U\) 3 2
unemployment rate \(u\) 4.84% 3.28%
\(E/N\) 0.59 0.59
  • \(E/N\) falls if more of the labor force is jobless or if fewer adults are in it.
  • The unemployment rate sees only the first.
  • One more point of \(u\) lowers \(E/N\) by about 1%.

The identity comes from writing E/N = (E/LF)(LF/N) and E = LF − U. Taking logs, ln(E/N) = ln(1 − u) + ln(LF/N), and ln(1 − u) is about −u when u is small: a one-point rise in unemployment lowers the employment-population ratio by about 1 percent.

The example is the notebook’s Check 1: 100 adults, a labor force of 62 and 3 unemployed. Both sides of the identity give 0.59. When one jobless person stops searching, U falls to 2 and LF to 61: the unemployment rate drops from 4.84 to 3.28 percent, and nobody has found a job.

This is why the chapter, and the news, should track both statistics.

A lower unemployment rate does not always mean more jobs

When the unemployment rate falls, more people must have found work.

Not necessarily. In the example, unemployment fell from 4.84% to 3.28% because one person gave up searching. Nobody found a job, and \(E/N\) stayed at 0.59.

The myth confuses a falling rate with rising employment. The unemployment rate falls when the unemployed find jobs, but also when they leave the labor force: they retire, return to school, or stop looking because they see no chance.

In deep recessions, part of the fall in unemployment during the recovery comes from people leaving the labor force. That is why economists also watch the employment-population ratio, Fig. 1, and the participation rate.

For the policy students: a program that pushes people out of the labor force, such as easier early retirement or disability benefits, can lower the unemployment rate while lowering employment. Judge it by E/N, not by u alone.

In the supply-and-demand language of the next slides, the rise of women’s employment came from both sides. Supply: changing social norms, more education and the contraceptive pill (Goldin and Katz 2002, Journal of Political Economy; Goldin 2006, American Economic Review). Demand: less workplace discrimination and the growth of service jobs.

Men’s ratio has fallen for decades: longer schooling, earlier retirement, disability, and the decline of manufacturing (Act III).

The Great Recession cut the ratio by 4.9 points, from 63.4 percent in December 2006 to 58.5 percent in January 2010, and it took years to recover. One point of the ratio is about 2.7 million jobs.

Jobs are not a fixed pie

When women (or immigrants) enter the labor market, they take jobs that men would otherwise have held.

If the number of jobs were fixed, more women at work would mean fewer jobs in total. Instead the share of all adults with a job rose, from 56.1% in 1960 to a peak of 64.7% in 2000, while women’s ratio rose from 35.5% to 57.5%.

Economists call the myth the “lump of labor” fallacy: the belief that there is a fixed amount of work to share out. It shows up in debates on immigration, on retirement ages (“older workers should retire to make room for the young”) and on shorter working weeks.

In the model of the next slides, more people willing to work shift labor supply out. Employment rises, and the wage falls a little at first. But new workers also produce and spend, firms invest, and in the long run labor demand grows with productivity and capital.

Men’s ratio did fall over the same years, from 78.9 to 71.9 percent. Its causes (schooling, retirement, disability, the decline of manufacturing) are separate, and the fall began long before women’s employment surged.

Card (1990, ILR Review) studies the Mariel boatlift, which added many Cuban migrants to Miami’s labor force within months, and finds little effect on the wages or unemployment of other workers. The size of such effects is still debated.

Labor demand slopes down; labor supply slopes up

Demand: firms

  • Firms hire until the marginal product of labor, which falls as they hire, equals the wage.
  • \(w = \bar{a}_d - \bar{b}_d L\)

Supply: people

  • Leisure costs the wage it could earn, so a higher wage draws more people into work.
  • \(w = \bar{a}_s + \bar{b}_s L\)

\[L^* = \frac{\bar{a}_d - \bar{a}_s}{\bar{b}_d + \bar{b}_s}, \qquad w^* = \frac{\bar{b}_s\bar{a}_d + \bar{b}_d\bar{a}_s}{\bar{b}_d + \bar{b}_s}\]

The demand curve comes from the production function of Chapter 4: with Y = ĀK̄(1/3)L(2/3), the marginal product of labor is (2/3)ĀK̄(1/3)L(−1/3), which falls as L rises. The textbook draws the curves without equations; the straight lines are the simplest curves with the right slopes, so we can compute with them.

The equilibrium comes from setting the two wages equal. The wage is a weighted average of the two intercepts, each weighted by the slope of the other curve.

The book’s baseline values: ā_d = 100, b̄_d = 1, ā_s = 10 and b̄_s = 1. So L* = 90/2 = 45 and w* = 110/2 = 55. These are illustrative numbers, not data: they make the diagrams easy to read.

When firms value workers more

Firms come to value every worker more: the demand intercept rises from 100 to 120. What happens to the wage, and to employment?

Ask students to sketch the shift before the reveal: the demand curve moves up, parallel to itself.

Most will get the direction of both changes right. Ask the harder question: how much of the shift shows up in the wage, and how much in employment? (With equal slopes, half and half.)

A demand shift moves the wage and employment the same way

  2. Move to 120: w* = 65 and L* = 55. Each extra unit of the intercept adds half a unit to both.
  3. Move to 80: both fall, to 45 and 35.

Ask: which real-world changes shift labor demand up? (Higher productivity, more capital per worker, a higher price for what the firm sells.)

The signs are what matter, and they hold for any positive values. A demand shift moves the wage and employment in the same direction. A supply shift moves them in opposite directions: when people need more pay to work, the wage rises and employment falls.

Over the long run in the United States, real wages and employment have both grown. Demand did most of the shifting: productivity growth, the subject of Chapters 4 to 6, raised what firms would pay for each worker.

Experiment: a tax on labor income

The government starts taxing labor income at 25%. What happens to employment? To the wage firms pay? To the wage workers keep?

Collect predictions on all three numbers separately. A common mistake is to think the whole tax falls on workers, so the wage firms pay stays at 55. Another is to forget that employment changes at all.

Hint for the sketch: workers decide how much to work from what they keep. Drawn against the wage firms pay, the supply curve shifts up and to the left.

A labor tax lowers employment and drives a wedge between two wages

The last bullet uses the identity of Act I. People who stop working because of the tax and stop searching leave the labor force: E/N falls, u does not. People laid off because labor got dearer are unemployed while they search; if they give up, u falls back. So a labor tax lowers employment for sure, but the unemployment rate may hardly move.

Who bears a tax does not depend on who writes the check: whether firms or workers send it to the government, the outcome is the same.

This mechanism is one candidate explanation of why Europeans work fewer hours than Americans, as we will see in Act II.

A fall in labor demand

New firing rules or dearer oil make firms want fewer workers at every wage: labor demand falls by 20. What happens to the wage and to employment?

This is the textbook’s second shock. Both examples raise the cost of hiring a worker, or lower what a worker adds to profits, so firms are willing to pay less at every level of employment.

Ask students to predict the direction of both changes and to guess how the fall is shared between them.

A fall in demand lowers both the wage and employment

How to read it: A is the old equilibrium and B the new one, after demand shifts down by the amount on the slider.

  1. At 20: the wage falls from 55 to 45 and employment from 45 to 35.
  2. Move to 40: wage 35, employment 25.

\[\Delta L^* = -\frac{\Delta}{\bar{b}_d + \bar{b}_s}, \qquad \Delta w^* = -\frac{\bar{b}_s\,\Delta}{\bar{b}_d + \bar{b}_s}\]

A lower wage means some people choose to work less, so employment falls by less than the shift.

The formulas come from the closed form, which is linear in the intercepts: lowering ā_d by Δ lowers L* by Δ/(b̄_d + b̄_s) and w* by b̄_sΔ/(b̄_d + b̄_s). With both slopes equal to 1, each falls by half the shift.

The key word is “choose”. In this flexible-wage world the people who stop working do so because the wage they are offered is no longer worth it to them. Nobody is unemployed in the sense of wanting a job at the going wage and not getting one. Act II changes that by stopping the wage from falling.

Unemployment has a normal part and a cyclical part

\[u = \underset{\text{natural rate } u^n}{\big(u_{\text{frictional}} + u_{\text{structural}}\big)} + u_{\text{cyclical}}\]

  • Frictional: the time it takes to move between jobs, even in a well-working market.
  • Structural: the rules of the market, such as firing costs, unemployment benefits and the minimum wage.
  • Cyclical: \(u - u^n\), positive in recessions and negative in booms.

The natural rate is not fixed by nature: institutions and policies move it.

The natural rate is the unemployment rate of an economy that is neither booming nor in recession. The next two models explain where unemployment comes from: a wage that does not fall gives a cyclical story; the bathtub model gives the natural rate.

Ask: which kind of unemployment is a graduate who takes three months to find a first job? (Frictional.) A coal miner whose region has lost its mines? (Structural, if the skills or the location no longer match the jobs.) A waiter laid off in a recession? (Cyclical.)

Experiment: the wage does not fall

Labor demand falls by 20 again, but this time the wage stays at 55. Does employment fall by more or by less than with a flexible wage? Who is left out?

Collect predictions. Most students expect a larger fall; ask them by how much. Some will see that employment is now set by the demand curve alone.

The second question is the important one: at a wage of 55, how many people want to work? (Still 45, from the supply curve.)

A rigid wage turns a fall in demand into unemployment

Rigid wages and prices are the core of the short-run model in Chapters 9 to 13. This slide is the first appearance of the idea.

Why would the wage not fall?

Rules and contracts

  • A minimum wage above the market wage.
  • Union contracts that fix wages for several years.
  • Pay cuts can be illegal or need workers’ consent.

The firm’s own choice

  • Efficiency wages: paying above the market rate can raise effort, cut quitting and attract better applicants.
  • Morale: managers fear that pay cuts anger the workers who stay.

Whatever the reason, a wage above the market-clearing level leaves people who want work at that wage without a job.

The textbook’s case study is Henry Ford’s “five-dollar day”, which sharply raised pay at his car plants when he could have hired at the old wage. Ford said the higher pay cut costs: workers stayed, worked harder and turned up. Raff and Summers (1987, Journal of Labor Economics) study the episode and find that the higher wage did lower quitting and absenteeism.

Bewley (1999, Why Wages Don’t Fall during a Recession) interviewed many managers. Most said they avoided pay cuts because cuts hurt morale and productivity, and preferred to lay off a few workers instead.

Efficiency wages also explain why some unemployment persists in good times: if every firm pays above the market rate, the market never fully clears.

The bathtub: unemployment settles where job losses equal job findings

\[\Delta U_{t+1} = \underset{\text{jobs lost}}{\bar{s}E_t} - \underset{\text{jobs found}}{\bar{f}U_t} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad u^* = \frac{\bar{s}}{\bar{f}+\bar{s}}\]

  • With \(\bar{s} = 0.01\) and \(\bar{f} = 0.20\) a month: \(u^* = 0.01/0.21 \approx\) 4.8%.
  • Each month about 0.95% of the labor force loses a job and the same share finds one: large flows, a small stock.
  • The gap to \(u^*\) closes by 21% a month: from 10%, unemployment is 7.34% after 3 months and 5.07% after a year.
  • An average spell lasts \(1/\bar{f} = 5\) months.

The model: a fixed labor force L̄ = E + U. Each month a share s̄ of the employed lose their job (the job separation rate) and a share f̄ of the unemployed find one (the job finding rate). Job losses are the tap, job findings the drain, and the unemployed are the water in the tub.

In the steady state the inflow equals the outflow: s̄(L̄ − U) = f̄U, which gives u* = s̄/(f̄ + s̄). The size of the labor force does not matter, only the two rates.

The path: u_t − u* = (1 − s̄ − f̄)^t (u_0 − u*). The factor is 0.79, so the gap halves in about 2.9 months. The notebook simulates the model month by month and matches the formula: 7.3445 percent after 3 months, 5.0714 after 12, 4.7619 after 60.

The textbook stresses the gross flows: each month the U.S. economy creates and destroys millions of jobs, and the net change is small.

Experiment: making firing costly

A new law makes firing costly. Layoffs fall: the job separation rate drops from 1.0% to 0.8% a month. Firms also hire more cautiously. Does the natural rate of unemployment fall or rise?

Collect predictions. Most students say it falls, because fewer people lose their jobs. Push them: what does a firm that cannot easily fire do when it is unsure about the future? (It hires less.)

Left: the slider of the bathtub chart, with s̄ = 0.01. Right: the notebook’s firing-cost example. If the law only cuts layoffs, the natural rate falls to 3.85 percent. If it also slows hiring to 0.15, it rises to 5.06 percent, above where it started.

The elasticities come from logs: ln u* = ln s̄ − ln(f̄ + s̄), so the elasticity with respect to s̄ is f̄/(f̄ + s̄) = 1 − u*, about 0.95, and with respect to f̄ it is about −0.95 (the notebook’s 1 percent change gives −0.943).

Only the two flow rates move the natural rate. A policy aimed at unemployment has to work through how often people lose jobs or how fast the jobless find new ones.

Protecting jobs does not always lower unemployment

Making it harder to fire workers protects jobs, so it must lower unemployment.

A firing cost slows the tap but can also narrow the drain. If hiring slows as well, the natural rate rises, from 4.76% to 5.06% in our example, and the jobless wait longer for work.

The myth looks only at the inflow. Strict employment protection does cut layoffs, but firms that cannot easily adjust also hire less, so the unemployed find jobs more slowly.

Blanchard and Portugal (2001, American Economic Review) compare Portugal, with strict employment protection, and the United States. The two had similar unemployment rates, but Portugal’s flows into and out of unemployment were far smaller and its spells far longer: a stagnant pool rather than a fast-flowing stream. The same unemployment rate can mean very different lives for the unemployed.

The evidence across countries on the net effect of employment protection on the unemployment rate is mixed; its effect on flows and durations is clearer.

How generous should unemployment benefits be?

Benefits protect people who lose their jobs, but they can also slow the search for a new one. How should they be designed?

Insurance

  • Benefits let families keep spending while they search.
  • Workers short of cash may take the first job offered; a cushion allows a better match.
  • Without insurance, a job loss can hit the poorest hardest.

Incentives

  • Benefits lower the cost of staying unemployed, so the job finding rate \(\bar{f}\) falls.
  • Many people find a job just when their benefits run out.
  • Design matters: a lump sum at job loss keeps the incentive to search.

Left column. Gruber (1997, American Economic Review) finds that unemployment insurance protects household consumption after a job loss. Ganong and Noel (2019, American Economic Review) show with bank-account data that spending drops sharply when benefits run out. Chetty (2008, Journal of Political Economy) shows that much of the longer search under higher benefits comes from people with little cash, who use benefits to search for a better job rather than to take time off: a liquidity effect, which is a benefit, not a cost.

Right column. Card, Chetty and Weber (2007, American Economic Review Papers and Proceedings) document a spike in exits from the unemployment rolls at benefit exhaustion in Austria. Schmieder, von Wachter and Bender (2012, Quarterly Journal of Economics) find that longer benefits in Germany lengthen spells of non-employment, by less in recessions. Baily (1978, Journal of Public Economics) set out the trade-off that this literature measures: insurance value against longer spells.

In the bathtub model, benefits work through f̄. The textbook’s exercise compares two reforms: replacing 100 percent of income for 26 weeks, or paying a lump sum of ten weeks’ income at the start of the spell. Ask the policy students which they would choose, and why. (The lump sum gives insurance without paying people for staying unemployed, but it gives nothing to the long-term unemployed.)

Did Europe’s institutions cause its unemployment?

Generous benefits, firing costs and high labor taxes are often blamed for Europe’s high unemployment and short hours. Should Europe change them, and would that work?

Shocks meet institutions

  • Europe had these institutions when unemployment was low, so they cannot explain the rise alone.
  • The consensus: the shocks of the 1970s raised unemployment, and the institutions kept it high.
  • Taxes on labor income lower hours worked, as in our tax experiment.

Reforms and their limits

  • Several countries cut benefit durations and eased hiring rules; unemployment later fell in Spain, Ireland and the Netherlands.
  • Reforms shift risk onto workers.
  • Culture and unions may matter as much as taxes.

Left column. Blanchard and Wolfers (2000, Economic Journal) find that shocks alone and institutions alone each explain little of the rise in European unemployment, while the interaction of the two explains much of it. Prescott (2004, Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis Quarterly Review) argues that differences in labor taxes account for most of the gap in hours between Europe and the United States. This is the tax experiment of Act I, at the scale of a country.

Right column. Alesina, Glaeser and Sacerdote (2005, NBER Macroeconomics Annual) argue that union power and labor regulations, not taxes, explain most of the gap in hours, and that Europeans may value coordinated leisure. Germany’s Hartz reforms cut benefits for the long-term unemployed and reorganized job placement; Krause and Uhlig (2012, Journal of Monetary Economics) attribute a substantial part of the later fall in German unemployment to them. The textbook notes that reforms in Spain, Ireland and the Netherlands were followed by lower unemployment than in the 1980s.

For the policy students: a reform that raises the job finding rate by cutting benefits trades insurance for incentives, the same trade-off as on the unemployment insurance slide. Which countries would you study to decide?

A dollar in the future is worth less than a dollar today

\[pdv = \frac{\text{future value}}{(1+R)^T}, \qquad pdv = \text{payment}\times\frac{1-\left(\frac{1}{1+R}\right)^n}{1-\frac{1}{1+R}}\]

A payment of … Interest rate Worth today
$10,000 in 10 years 3% $7,441
$1,000 in 5 years 10% $621
$100 a year for 20 years 10% $936
$1 a year for 45 years 3% $25.25
$1 a year forever 3% $34.33

The first formula: a deposit of pdv today grows into pdv(1 + R)^T in T years, so the value today of a payment in T years is the payment divided by (1 + R)^T. Dividing by (1 + R)^T is called discounting.

The second formula adds up n equal payments, the first one today. With a = 1/(1 + R), the sum is 1 + a + a² + … + a^(n−1) = (1 − a^n)/(1 − a), the geometric-series trick: multiply the sum by a, subtract, and every middle term cancels.

The textbook’s choice: $1,000 in five years or $100 a year for 20 years, at 10 percent? The stream is worth $936 today and the single payment $621, so the stream wins.

The last two rows: 45 years of $1 are worth only $25.25 today, not $45, and even a payment that lasts forever is worth $34.33. The 45 years already give 74 percent of forever.

A college degree is worth several hundred thousand dollars

\[NPV = \underset{\approx\ 673{,}000}{(w_{\text{college}} - w_{\text{high school}})\times S_{45}\times\frac{1}{(1+R)^4}} \;-\; \underset{\approx\ 287{,}000}{(w_{\text{high school}} + \text{tuition})\times S_4}\]

The table is the slider of Fig. 9, with the supply shift fixed at 10. The formula follows from the closed form, which is linear in the intercepts: demand rising by x_d and supply shifting out by x_s change the wage by 0.6x_d − 0.4x_s with these slopes. The defaults give 9 − 4 = 5.

Skill-biased technical change: computers and software raise the productivity of workers who can use them, more than that of workers who do routine tasks. Globalization: trade with countries where skilled workers are scarce raises the value of skills here, and trade with countries rich in unskilled labor lowers the wages of the unskilled.

Katz and Murphy (1992, Quarterly Journal of Economics) estimate this supply-and-demand framework for the U.S. wage structure. Goldin and Katz (2008, The Race between Education and Technology) tell the long history: when education grew faster than the demand for skills, inequality fell; when it slowed, inequality rose.

More graduates do not have to mean a lower premium

If many more people earn college degrees, their wage advantage must shrink.

Only if demand for their skills stands still. From 1979 to 2020 the share of jobs held by graduates rose from 18.3% to 40.9%, and their premium rose from 25.7 to 48.1 log points. In the 1970s, supply did outrun demand, and the premium fell.

The myth applies supply-and-demand reasoning with only one curve. More supply alone does lower the price, which is what happened in the 1970s. Over the following four decades, demand for skilled workers grew faster still.

For policy, the lesson cuts both ways. Expanding college access does not have to destroy the return to a degree, as long as demand for skills keeps growing. And if the premium is high because supply lags demand, more education is one of the few policies that can lower wage inequality while raising incomes (Goldin and Katz 2008).

Note the recent slip: the premium fell from 49.2 log points in 2016 to 44.0 in 2025. Whether this is the start of a new 1970s is an open question.

Who should help the workers hurt by trade and automation?

Trade and new technology raise national income, but the losses are concentrated in particular places and workers. What should policy do for them?

What the evidence shows

  • Places more exposed to Chinese imports lost more manufacturing jobs.
  • Wages there fell and disability and other benefit claims rose.
  • Workers did not move away as much as models assumed.

Policy options

  • Retraining and job-search help: results vary by program.
  • Wage insurance and income support for displaced workers.
  • Place-based aid, or help to move to where jobs are.

Left column. Autor, Dorn and Hanson (2013, American Economic Review) compare U.S. local labor markets with different exposure to Chinese imports and find larger falls in manufacturing employment, lower wages and higher transfer payments in the more exposed ones. Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Price (2016, Journal of Labor Economics) estimate the national employment effects. Pierce and Schott (2016, American Economic Review) link the timing to the end of uncertainty about U.S. tariffs on Chinese goods. Autor, Dorn and Hanson (2016, Annual Review of Economics) review the slow adjustment of local labor markets. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2020, Journal of Political Economy) find similar local effects of industrial robots.

Right column. Card, Kluve and Weber (2018, Journal of the European Economic Association) review hundreds of evaluations of active labor market programs: training and job-search help do little in the short run and more in later years, with large differences across programs and groups. The U.S. Trade Adjustment Assistance program combines training with income support; its evaluations are mixed.

The trade-off for the policy students: help that targets trade victims only is hard to justify to workers who lost their jobs to other causes, such as automation. Help for all displaced workers is fairer but more costly.

Should top incomes be taxed more?

Top incomes have grown much faster than the rest since 1980 in the United States. Should top tax rates rise, and what would that do to work, innovation and revenue?

For higher top rates

  • Part of top pay may come from bargaining power, not extra output; taxing it costs little output.
  • Higher top rates in the past coincided with lower top shares.
  • Revenue could fund transfers that lift the bottom.

Against, or with limits

  • Top earners respond to taxes, partly by moving income into other forms.
  • Inventors and other high earners move across borders.
  • If top incomes reward innovation, taxing them may slow growth.

Left column. Piketty, Saez and Stantcheva (2014, American Economic Journal: Economic Policy) argue that when top earners can bargain for a larger share of the pie, high top rates reduce bargaining rather than work, which favors higher optimal rates. Diamond and Saez (2011, Journal of Economic Perspectives) lay out the case for progressive taxation from optimal-tax theory.

Right column. Saez, Slemrod and Giertz (2012, Journal of Economic Literature) review how taxable income responds to tax rates: much of the response is avoidance and timing, which depends on how broad the tax base is. Kleven, Landais and Saez (2013, American Economic Review) find that top football players move in response to tax rates across European countries; Akcigit, Baslandze and Stantcheva (2016, American Economic Review) find the same for star inventors. Jones and Kim (2018, Journal of Political Economy) model top incomes as the reward for entrepreneurial innovation, in which case taxing them can slow growth, the Chapter 6 link.

The textbook’s own position: whether rising inequality is good or bad is hard to say, and the profession has no consensus. Ask the policy students which evidence would change their minds.

“Robots and artificial intelligence will soon make most workers unnecessary”

A skeptic says: “Machines already do much of what factory workers did, and artificial intelligence will do what office workers do. Labor demand will collapse, and most people will have no job.”

Reply: technology shifts labor demand between tasks and skills rather than abolishing it. In the college market it raised the demand for some workers, and manufacturing lost jobs, but the share of U.S. adults with a job was 59.2% in September 2026, above the 56.1% of 1960, after decades of rapid productivity growth. The real risk is who gains and who loses.

Take the objection seriously: the losses are real. Manufacturing lost 5.84 million jobs between 2000 and 2010, and Acemoglu and Restrepo (2020, Journal of Political Economy) find that each additional industrial robot in a local labor market reduced employment and wages there.

The reply has three parts. First, the fear is old: Keynes (1930, “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren”) predicted “technological unemployment”, and employment kept growing. Second, technology creates new tasks as well as replacing old ones: Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019, Journal of Economic Perspectives) show that new tasks have offset automation for most of the past century, and Autor (2015, Journal of Economic Perspectives) explains why machines that replace some tasks raise the value of the tasks they complement. Third, in the model of this chapter, a technology that raises productivity shifts labor demand up for the workers it complements: that is the skill-biased technical change behind the rising college premium.

What the reply does not claim: that every worker will be fine. The transition can take decades, and the gains can go to a few. That is why the policy slides on displaced workers and on top incomes matter.

Ask: which tasks in your future job do you expect AI to do, and which new tasks might it create?

What to remember from this chapter

  • Read the employment-population ratio and the unemployment rate together: \(E/N = (1-u)\,LF/N\). Women drove the rise in employment.
  • In supply and demand, a demand shock moves the wage and employment together; a tax lowers employment; a rigid wage turns a fall in demand into unemployment.
  • The natural rate is set by job flows, \(u^* = \bar{s}/(\bar{f}+\bar{s})\), and policies that change \(\bar{s}\) can also change \(\bar{f}\).
  • Present values compare payments over time: a working life is worth about $2.53 million, a degree about $386,000.
  • The college premium rose with more graduates because demand for skills grew faster than supply; since 1980 the top has grown fastest.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: who works, why can’t everyone find a job, and why do some earn more? A good answer: employment and wages follow supply and demand; unemployment comes from wages that do not fall and from the time it takes to find a job; and workers whose skills are in growing demand earn more.
