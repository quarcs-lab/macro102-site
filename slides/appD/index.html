In the typical poor economy, most of the income gap with the United States is a productivity gap

0.71 The median share of an economy’s gap in output per worker with the United States that is due to TFP, 143 economies, 2023

Not machines, not schooling: the part that capital and workers leave unexplained. Today we measure it, for any economy you choose, with the Penn World Table and a few lines of Python.

Open with the number, then ask: what do you think explains why Nigeria produces less than a tenth of what the United States produces per worker? Most students say capital (machines, roads) or education. The data say those matter, but the residual, total factor productivity, carries most of the gap.

Three questions for today

  • Data. What does the Penn World Table measure, and which numbers can we compare across countries and over time?
  • Growth accounting. How much of an economy’s GDP growth came from more capital, more workers, and higher productivity?
  • Development accounting. How much of the income gap between a poor economy and the United States is due to capital, schooling, and productivity?

These are the three acts of the deck and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide: the Penn World Table and the framework (cyan), growth accounting (lavender), development accounting (green).

Both methods are accounting, not explanation. They say where the differences show up in the production function, not why an economy invested, schooled or innovated more. Chapters 5 and 6 explain the why.

The Penn World Table and the framework

Act I

One production function frames both methods

\[Y = A\,K^{\alpha}\,L^{1-\alpha}, \qquad \alpha = 0.33\]

  • \(Y\) output, \(K\) physical capital, \(L\) labour: measured in national accounts
  • \(A\) total factor productivity (TFP): not measured, found as the residual
  • \(\alpha\) the capital share, the same everywhere: about one third (Hall and Jones 1999)

Measure what you can, and call the rest productivity.

This is equation (1) of the appendix, the Cobb–Douglas production function of Chapters 4 to 6.

The capital share alpha = 0.33 is a classroom standard and the default of both the Growth Atlas and Caunedo’s code. It is not each country’s measured factor share; Section D.11 tests what happens with country labour shares.

Ask: why can’t we measure A directly? Because no statistical office measures “efficiency”. It is defined by the equation itself.

The Penn World Table makes output and inputs comparable across 185 economies

What it provides

  • GDP at purchasing power parity: a dollar buys the same basket everywhere
  • Capital stocks built from past investment
  • Workers, their hours and a human capital index from schooling
  • 185 economies, 1950–2023, prices of 2021 (version 11.0)

Two kinds of series

  • National prices (rgdpna, rnna): growth over time, within an economy → growth accounting
  • PPP (rgdpo, cgdpo, cn): levels across economies in one year → development accounting

Feenstra, Inklaar and Timmer (2015) describe the database; it is maintained at the Groningen Growth and Development Centre.

The distinction in the right column matters and students often miss it. PWT itself recommends the national-price series to compare growth rates over time, and the PPP series to compare levels across countries. That is why the two halves of the appendix use different variables.

Section D.3 of the notebook downloads the official file (pwt110.dta, about 4 MB, from DataverseNL) and checks that the book’s prepared file agrees with it: the largest relative difference in rgdpna is 4.9e-05, which is rounding.

Growth accounting

Act II

From the production function to the growth accounting equation

Take logs, then the change from one year to the next:

\[\ln Y_t = \ln A_t + \alpha \ln K_t + (1-\alpha)\ln L_t\]

The change in a log is, approximately, the growth rate:

\[g_Y = \underbrace{\alpha\,g_K}_{\text{capital}} + \underbrace{(1-\alpha)\,g_L}_{\text{labour}} + \underbrace{g_A}_{\text{TFP}}\]

\[g_A = g_Y - \alpha\,g_K - (1-\alpha)\,g_L \qquad \text{the Solow residual}\]

Equations (2) and (3) of the appendix. The full derivation is in Section D.4: take logs (a product becomes a sum, a power becomes a coefficient), write the same equation a year earlier, subtract, and use the approximation that a change in a log is a growth rate.

Because TFP growth is computed as the residual, the three contributions add up to GDP growth exactly, even though the log approximation is not exact. Like the Growth Atlas, we use simple yearly growth rates, 100 × (X_t/X_{t−1} − 1).

Intuition with alpha = 0.33: 3 percent more capital adds about 1 point to GDP growth; 3 percent more labour adds about 2 points.

In Python, the whole method is a groupby and a subtraction

growth = d.groupby("iso3")[["rgdpna", "rnna", "L"]].pct_change(fill_method=None) * 100
d["gdp"] = growth["rgdpna"]
d["capital"] = alpha * growth["rnna"]                 # alpha g_K
d["labour"] = (1 - alpha) * growth["L"]               # (1 - alpha) g_L
d["tfp"] = d["gdp"] - d["capital"] - d["labour"]      # g_A, the residual

groupby("iso3") keeps each economy’s growth rates separate, so the first year of one economy is never compared with the last year of another.

This is the core of the notebook’s growth_accounting function (Section D.5). The labour input L is persons engaged, or persons × hours, or persons × hours × the human capital index, depending on the labour argument.

The notebook first does the same thing by hand for the United States, column by column, and checks that the largest gap between the sum of contributions and GDP growth is 4e-16, zero up to rounding.

“TFP growth measures technological progress”

TFP growth is the rate of technological progress.

TFP growth is a residual. Over decades it reflects ideas, organization and reallocation; in any one year it also absorbs changes in hours, skills, capacity use, commodity prices and measurement error, as 2020 showed.

Solow (1957) called it technical change, and Abramovitz (1956) called the residual “a measure of our ignorance”. Both views are useful: the long-run average is informative; single years are not.

Mexico’s growth puzzle: lots of investment, no productivity growth

Mexico opened to trade, stabilized prices and invested, yet TFP contributed nothing to its growth since 1990. What should policy target?

What the accounting says

  • Capital 0.83 and labour 1.57 points a year: all of the 2.29 percent growth
  • TFP −0.11: the same inputs did not become more productive
  • So more investment alone would not have closed the gap

What it leaves out

  • Why TFP stagnated: Levy (2018) points to misallocation, as informality and tax and social insurance rules keep resources in low-productivity firms
  • Whether better measurement (informal output, hours) would change the residual

Evidence: Santiago Levy (2018), Under-Rewarded Efforts: The Elusive Quest for Prosperity in Mexico, Inter-American Development Bank, argues that the allocation of capital and labour toward small, informal, low-productivity firms explains the stagnation of Mexican productivity. This is one influential reading, not a settled consensus; others stress credit, rule of law or the China shock to Mexican manufacturing.

The accounting itself only says where the growth came from: inputs, not productivity.

Development accounting

Act III

Why is Nigeria so much poorer than the United States?

Nigeria’s output per worker is 8.5 percent of the U.S. level. How much of that gap is machines, how much is schooling, and how much is productivity?

Ask students to split the gap themselves first, in three percentages. Keep their answers on the board; the next slides compute them.

Add schooling, then write capital relative to output

Output with physical and human capital, \(H = hL\), divided by workers:

\[y = A\,k^{\alpha}\,h^{1-\alpha}\]

Substitute \(k = (K/Y)\,y\), collect \(y\), and raise to \(1/(1-\alpha)\):

\[y = \underbrace{A^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}_{\text{TFP term}} \times \underbrace{\left(\frac{K}{Y}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}}_{\text{capital term}} \times \underbrace{h}_{\text{human capital}}\]

Each term is then divided by its U.S. value: a ratio of 0.5 means half the U.S. level.

Equation (5) of the appendix; the derivation is in Section D.8: write k = (K/Y) y, so y = A (K/Y)^α y^α h^(1−α); divide by y^α, so y^(1−α) = A (K/Y)^α h^(1−α); raise both sides to 1/(1−α).

h is PWT’s human capital index (hc), built from years of schooling and the returns to schooling. Output per worker is rgdpo/emp, and K/Y is cn/cgdpo, both at PPPs.

Why the capital–output ratio, and not capital per worker?

  • A more productive economy produces more, saves more, and builds more capital: part of its high \(k\) is caused by TFP
  • In the Solow model, \(K/Y = s/(\bar{n} + \bar{d})\) in steady state: it does not depend on \(A\)
  • So the capital term measures only the capital that TFP does not explain

Credit TFP for the capital it induces, and capital for the rest.

This is the choice of Hall and Jones (1999) and of Caunedo’s code. Chapter 4’s development accounting, y = A k^(1/3), credits capital for all capital. Section D.10 shows how much the choice matters: the success of the inputs doubles in the capital-per-worker form.

Splitting the gap into three shares

\[\frac{y_{US}}{y_i} = \underbrace{T_i}_{\text{TFP gap}} \times \underbrace{K_i}_{\text{capital gap}} \times \underbrace{H_i}_{\text{schooling gap}}\]

\[s^{TFP}_i = \frac{T_i}{T_i + K_i H_i}\]

\[s^{K}_i = \frac{K_i}{K_i + H_i}\,(1 - s^{TFP}_i), \qquad s^{H}_i = \frac{H_i}{K_i + H_i}\,(1 - s^{TFP}_i)\]

If the TFP gap equals the gap the two inputs create together, TFP’s share is one half; if it is three times as large, three quarters.

Equation (8). Each gap factor is the U.S. level divided by economy i’s level. This is the split in Caunedo’s code (share_due_to_TFP, then the rest divided between human and physical capital in proportion to their gap factors). The three shares add up to one; the notebook checks it (largest error 1e-16).

The method in Python: three columns and a division

d["y"] = d["rgdpo"] / d["emp"]                                # output per worker
d["ky"] = (d["cn"] / d["cgdpo"]) ** (alpha / (1 - alpha))      # capital term
d["h"] = d["hc"]                                               # human capital
d["tfp"] = d["y"] / (d["ky"] * d["h"])                         # TFP term, the residual
for col in ["y", "ky", "h", "tfp"]:
    d[col + "_rel"] = d[col] / us[col]                         # United States = 1

Per hour instead of per worker? Divide by emp * avh. That is the only change.

The core of dev_levels and relative_to_us in Section D.9. The notebook also drops Venezuela, whose PWT output after 2017 is not plausible because hyperinflation broke its price data, and checks that the three ratios multiply to the output ratio (equation (7)).

“Poor countries are poor because they lack machines”

Poor countries are poor mainly because they have too little capital.

Relative to their output, poor economies have as much capital as the United States: Nigeria’s capital term is 1.73, India’s 1.12. Capital per worker is low because output is low, and output is low mainly because TFP is low.

This is the central lesson of development accounting since Hall and Jones (1999) and Caselli (2005). It does not mean capital is unimportant: TFP-induced capital is real capital; the accounting credits it to TFP because TFP caused it.

If productivity is the gap, what should policy target?

A finance ministry wants to close the gap with richer economies. Subsidize investment, expand schooling, or remove what keeps productivity low?

What the accounting suggests

  • Investment alone raises \(K/Y\), which is already near the U.S. level
  • Schooling closes part of the gap (Nigeria’s \(h\) is 0.56)
  • Most of the gap is TFP: how well inputs are used

What it leaves out

  • What TFP is: institutions and “social infrastructure” (Hall and Jones 1999), misallocation across firms (Hsieh and Klenow 2009), technology adoption
  • Whether hc measures skills well: Hendricks and Schoellman (2023) find a larger role for human capital

Hsieh and Klenow (2009) estimate that reallocating capital and labour across manufacturing plants to U.S. levels of efficiency would raise manufacturing TFP by 30–50 percent in China and 40–60 percent in India. Hall and Jones (1999) relate output per worker to institutions and government policies. Both are influential; the size of the effects is debated.

Development accounting itself prescribes nothing; it tells policymakers where the gap is.

A skeptic: “TFP is just a measure of our ignorance”

A skeptic says: “You call the residual productivity, but it is simply everything you failed to measure. Saying it explains 70 percent of the gap says nothing.”

Reply: the skeptic is half right, which is the point. Abramovitz (1956) called the residual “a measure of our ignorance”. Accounting shows how big that ignorance is, and robustness checks (hours, rents, schooling) show it does not go away when we measure more. That is why growth theory, from Solow to Romer, is about TFP.

Use this to motivate Chapters 5 and 6 and the research on institutions and misallocation. The residual is large and robust; explaining it is the open question.

What to remember from this appendix

  • I. The Penn World Table gives comparable output and inputs for 185 economies: national-price series for growth, PPP series for levels
  • II. Growth accounting splits GDP growth into capital, labour and TFP: since 1990, TFP did more than half of China’s growth, close to half of U.S. growth, and nothing in Mexico
  • III. Development accounting puts most of the income gap on TFP: the median TFP share is 0.71 in 2023, and inputs explain only 16 percent of the spread of incomes
  • Both are accounting: they say where the differences are, not why

One takeaway per act, in the order of the mind map’s branches. Ask students which number surprised them most.

Measured inputs explain much of growth but little of income gaps; productivity, the residual, carries most of the difference

Open the Appendix D notebook in Colab, choose your own countries, and redo every chart

The thesis of the appendix and of the mind map. Accounting splits output into what measured inputs explain and a residual, TFP. Since 1990, TFP explains more than half of China’s growth, close to half of U.S. growth and none of Mexico’s. Across 143 economies in 2023, the capital–output term is close to the U.S. level almost everywhere and schooling explains part of the gap, but TFP carries most of it. Accounting says where the differences are, not why.
