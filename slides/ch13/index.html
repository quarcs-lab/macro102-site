A one-line formula tracks the Fed’s interest rate with a correlation of 0.75

0.75 Correlation between the fed funds rate and the rule \(i = 1 + 1.5\,\pi\), 1960–2023

Could a robot chair the Federal Reserve? A recipe that looks only at inflation follows the Fed’s big swings. Yet in 2021–22 the Fed set its rate 6.7 points below that recipe. When is a rule good enough, and when does judgment matter?

Open with the number. It comes from the notebook’s Fig. 16 check: the correlation of the actual fed funds rate with John Taylor’s simple rule, quarterly, from 1960Q1 to 2023Q1, is 0.75. The rule uses only one input, core PCE inflation, and three numbers that are all 2: r̄ = 2, m̄ = 1/2, π̄ = 2. Then i = 1 + 1.5π.

The textbook opens with a provocative question: could we replace the chair of the Fed with a robot? Nobody proposes it seriously, but the question is useful. If one line of algebra describes decades of policy, what are the hundreds of economists at the Fed doing? And what goes wrong when the Fed departs from the line, as in the 1970s or in 2021–22?

Ask the room: who sets interest rates where you live? Do they follow a formula? Would you trust one?

Today: three questions

  • What does a policy rule do to the short-run model? It turns the IS and Phillips curves into one AS/AD diagram.
  • How does the economy react to shocks under the rule? An inflation shock, a disinflation and a demand boom, period by period.
  • Does the rule describe real central banks, and what makes low inflation cheap? The Taylor rule, inflation-output loops, forecasts, credibility and three centuries of inflation.

These are the three acts of the deck and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide.

The headline: a central bank that follows a rule turns the short-run model into one diagram. Because expectations are sticky, every shock fades only slowly and lowering inflation costs a recession; a credible target is what makes low inflation cheap.

Chapters 11 and 12 built the pieces: the IS curve and the Phillips curve, with the central bank choosing the real interest rate case by case. Here the bank follows a fixed recipe.

A rule and one diagram

Act I

The short-run model so far has three pieces

\[\text{IS:}\quad \widetilde{Y}\!_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R_t - \bar{r})\]

\[\text{Phillips:}\quad \Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\,\widetilde{Y}\!_t + \bar{o}\]

  • In Chapter 12 the central bank picked \(R_t\) for each shock, one at a time.
  • Now it commits to a rule: a fixed recipe from what it sees to the rate it sets.
  • \(\widetilde{Y}\!_t\): output’s percent gap from potential. Bars mark parameters.

A rule replaces many case-by-case decisions with one equation.

The IS curve (Chapter 11): spending falls below normal when the real interest rate R is above the marginal product of capital r̄; ā is the aggregate demand shock and b̄ the interest sensitivity. The Phillips curve (Chapter 12): inflation rises when output is above potential, plus any inflation shock ō, such as an oil price jump.

Notation of the book chapter: a bar marks a parameter (ā, b̄, m̄, ν̄, ō, r̄, π̄), a tilde marks short-run output, the subscript t counts years.

The baseline numbers used in every figure: target π̄ = 2 percent, Taylor’s policy response m̄ = 1/2, interest sensitivity b̄ = 2 (Chapter 11’s example), Phillips-curve slope ν̄ = 1. The shocks: ō = 2 for one period, ā = 2 for nine periods, and a cut in the target from 4 to 2 percent.

Ask: what does the central bank actually observe each month? (Inflation, quickly; the output gap, only roughly and with revisions. Act III comes back to this.)

The aggregate demand curve

What happens to output when inflation rises, if the central bank follows a rule?

The rule sets the real rate \(\bar{m} = 1/2\) point above \(\bar{r}\) for each point of inflation above the 2% target, and \(\bar{b} = 2\). Inflation is 3%. What is output? Now add a demand shock \(\bar{a} = 2\): which way does the curve move?

Ask for numbers. A good answer: the real rate rises by 0.5 × (3 − 2) = 0.5 points, the IS curve turns that into 2 × 0.5 = 1 point of lost output, so Ỹ = −1 percent. A demand shock moves the curve to the right by 2 points.

Common wrong answer: “output does not depend on inflation”. Ask students to follow the chain: inflation → the rule → the interest rate → spending.

Higher inflation means lower output, because the central bank raises rates

The title writes the equation with the current numbers. The slider sets the demand shock ā; the baseline curve stays behind as a dashed gray line.

Do live:

  1. At ā = 0 the dot is at (0, 2): inflation on target, output at potential.
  2. Read the curve at π = 3: output is −1 percent. Each point of inflation above target costs b̄m̄ = 1 point of output.
  3. Move ā to +2: the whole line shifts 2 points to the right, and the dot moves to (2, 2). Move to −2: it shifts left.

Point out that a change in inflation itself is a movement along the line, not a shift.

The AD curve describes the central bank, not shoppers

  • The answer: at 3% inflation the rule adds 0.5 points to the real rate, and output falls to −1%. Each point of inflation costs \(\bar{b}\bar{m} = 1\) point of output.
  • A demand shock \(\bar{a}\) shifts AD right; a higher target \(\bar{\pi}\) shifts it up. The dot always sits at \((\bar{a}, \bar{\pi})\).
  • \(\bar{b}\) and \(\bar{m}\) enter only as a product: a tougher bank and a more rate-sensitive economy draw the same curve.

AD slopes down because the bank leans against inflation with the interest rate.

Numbers: the Model overview’s chain for 3 percent inflation, 0.5 × (3 − 2) = 0.5 and 0 − 2 × 0.5 = −1, and the Fig. 1 readout. The notebook’s key numbers: inflation 2 points above target raises the real rate by 1 point and costs b̄ × 1 = 2 points of output (the textbook’s example); at π = 4 the AD curve gives Ỹ = −2 percent.

Second fact from the Model overview: when inflation is on target and there is no demand shock, the bank wants output at potential, whatever m̄ is.

The comparative statics table of the book chapter: a rise in π is a movement along AD; ā shifts it right by one point per point; π̄ shifts it up by one point per point.

Two equations make the AD curve

\[\text{IS:}\ \ \widetilde{Y}\!_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R_t - \bar{r}) \qquad \text{rule:}\ \ R_t - \bar{r} = \bar{m}\,(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})\]

\[\text{AD:}\quad \widetilde{Y}\!_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{m}\,(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})\]

\[\text{in the diagram:}\quad \pi_t = \bar{\pi} + \frac{\bar{a} - \widetilde{Y}\!_t}{\bar{b}\bar{m}} \qquad \text{slope } -\frac{1}{\bar{b}\bar{m}}\]

The interest rate drops out: AD links the two outcomes, output and inflation.

The rule gives exactly the interest-rate gap R − r̄ that appears in the IS curve, so substitute it. The result has no interest rate in it: AD is a relation between two endogenous variables.

Because inflation goes on the vertical axis, solve for π: move the inflation term left and Ỹ right, divide by b̄m̄. The line passes through (ā, π̄) with slope −1/(b̄m̄); in the baseline b̄m̄ = 2 × 0.5 = 1, so the slope is −1.

Full derivation: the book chapter’s Model overview, “The policy rule and the AD curve”.

The AD curve is not a demand curve for goods

The AD curve slopes down because people buy less when prices are high.

The vertical axis is the inflation rate, not the price level. AD slopes down because the central bank raises the real interest rate when inflation is above target, and spending falls. With a different rule, the curve would have a different slope.

The textbook’s review question: how is the AS/AD graph like a supply-and-demand diagram, and how is it different? Like one: two curves, one sloping down and one up, crossing at an equilibrium; shocks shift them. Different: the axes are output relative to potential and the inflation rate; AD summarizes the central bank’s reaction plus the IS curve; AS is the Phillips curve, firms’ pricing.

Later in the act: a rule that does not raise the real rate with inflation (m̄ < 0) would make AD slope up, and the economy would be unstable.

An aggressive central bank

What changes when the central bank reacts more strongly to inflation?

Inflation rises from 2% to 3%. With \(\bar{m} = 1/2\), output fell 1%. The bank now uses \(\bar{m} = 1\). How far does output fall? Is the new AD curve steeper or flatter?

Collect answers. Many students guess “steeper”, because a tougher bank sounds like a steeper line. Ask them to think in the diagram: a small change in inflation now brings a large change in output, so the line is flatter.

A tougher rule flattens the AD curve and deepens the recession

How to read it: the same axes. A is the steady state (0, 2); B is where the economy lands when inflation is 3 percent. The dashed gray line is the baseline AD curve with m̄ = 0.5. The title follows the chain: inflation, then the rise in the real rate R − r̄, then output. The slider sets m̄.

  1. Start at m̄ = 1: R − r̄ = 1.00 and output falls to −2.00 percent.
  2. Move to 0.5: the baseline case of the textbook’s Fig. 13.2, R − r̄ = 0.50 and Ỹ = −1.00. (This is why the deck skips that figure.)
  3. Move to 0.25: the curve is steep, and the bank barely reacts: −0.50 percent.

A flatter AD curve means a bank that fights inflation harder

  • The answer: with \(\bar{m} = 1\) the same 1-point rise in inflation lowers output by 2% instead of 1%. The curve is flatter.
  • At \(\bar{m} = 0.25\) the fall is only 0.5%: a steep AD curve is a bank that barely reacts.
  • Every curve passes through \(A\): with inflation on target and no shock, the bank wants output at potential.

The slope of AD is a policy choice: it measures how much output the bank will give up per point of inflation.

Numbers: the Fig. 3 readouts for m̄ = 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 1.75 (output −0.50, −1.00, −2.00, −3.50 percent at π = 3).

The slope of the line in the diagram is −1/(b̄m̄): with b̄ = 2, it is −1 at m̄ = 0.5 and −0.5 at m̄ = 1.

Act II will show the trade-off: a flatter AD curve means a deeper but shorter recession after a shock, and, surprisingly, the same total loss of output.

The Phillips curve becomes the aggregate supply curve

\[\text{AS:}\quad \pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + \bar{\nu}\,\widetilde{Y}\!_t + \bar{o}\]

  • Firms expect last year’s inflation, \(\pi^e_t = \pi_{t-1}\), which is known when the year starts.
  • In a boom they pay overtime and run machines harder, so prices rise faster than expected: AS slopes up with slope \(\bar{\nu}\).
  • AS crosses \(\widetilde{Y} = 0\) at \(\pi_{t-1} + \bar{o}\): whenever inflation changes, next year’s AS curve moves.

The AS curve carries expectations into the diagram. That is the engine of every event in Act II.

Write out Δπ = π_t − π_(t−1) and add π_(t−1) to both sides of the Phillips curve. With adaptive expectations the same line reads π_t = π^e_t + ν̄Ỹ_t + ō: inflation is what firms expected, plus a push from a hot economy, plus any shock.

The book chapter’s AS table: Ỹ up one point raises π by ν̄ (a movement along); last year’s inflation up one point shifts AS up one point; a shock ō shifts it up while it lasts; a larger ν̄ turns it steeper.

The notebook has a separate AS figure (Fig. 4, with a slider for last year’s inflation). We skip it: the next figure has the same AS curve and the same slider, now called expected inflation.

A check from the notebook: with π_(t−1) = 2 and ν̄ = 1, inflation reaches 3.5 percent when output is 1.5 percent above potential.

The AS/AD diagram

Where do the two curves cross, and is that point a resting place?

Expected inflation equals the 2% target. Where do AS and AD cross? Now firms expect 4% instead: what happens to inflation and output this year? Will the economy stay there?

Ask for the crossing point in both cases. Good answers: at (0, 2) when expectations are on target; with 4 percent expected inflation the AS curve shifts up, inflation rises and output falls. Whether the economy stays there is the key question; most students say yes.

AS and AD cross at potential output and the target, when expectations agree

Model figure: AS \(\pi_t = \pi^e_t + \bar{\nu}\widetilde{Y}\!_t\) and AD with \(\bar{m} = 0.5\), \(\bar{b} = 2\), \(\bar{\nu} = 1\), \(\bar{\pi} = 2\). Adapted from Fig. 13.5 in Jones (2024), Macroeconomics, 6th ed.

How to read it: AS (rising) and AD (falling) on the same axes. The slider sets expected inflation, which is last year’s inflation. The title says where the curves cross and whether that point is a steady state.

Do live:

  1. At 2 percent: the curves cross at Ỹ = 0.00, π = 2.00, “the steady state”.
  2. Move to 4 percent: the crossing moves to Ỹ = −1.00, π = 3.00, “not a steady state”.
  3. Move to 1 percent: Ỹ = +0.50, π = 1.50, again not a steady state.

Ask: in the 4 percent case, inflation turned out to be 3 percent. What will firms expect next year? (3 percent: the AS curve moves again.)

Only on target is the crossing a steady state

  • The answer: with expectations at the target the curves cross at \(\widetilde{Y} = 0\), \(\pi = 2\%\).
  • With 4% expected: \(\pi = 3\%\), \(\widetilde{Y} = -1\%\). Inflation differs from what firms expected, so next year’s AS curve shifts.
  • A steady state needs \(\pi_t = \pi_{t-1}\): the AS curve then forces \(\widetilde{Y}^* = 0\), and AD then forces \(\pi^* = \bar{\pi}\).

In the long run output is at potential and the bank’s target becomes the economy’s inflation rate.

Numbers: the Fig. 5 readouts and the notebook’s steady-state check: for (m̄, ν̄) = (0.25, 0.5), (0.5, 1) and (2, 2), the solution with expected inflation at the target is Ỹ = 0.00 percent and π = 2.00 percent every time, equation (4).

Derivation, step 1 of the Model overview: no shocks and π_t = π_(t−1) in AS give ν̄Ỹ* = 0, so Ỹ* = 0; then AD with ā = 0 gives π* = π̄.

The inputs of the diagram are the parameters (π̄, m̄, ν̄, b̄) and expected inflation; the outcomes are this year’s output and inflation.

Solving AS and AD gives this year’s output and inflation

\[\widetilde{Y}\!_t = \frac{\bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{m}\,(\pi_{t-1} - \bar{\pi} + \bar{o})}{1 + \bar{b}\bar{m}\bar{\nu}}\]

\[\pi_t - \bar{\pi} = \lambda\left[(\pi_{t-1} - \bar{\pi}) + \bar{o} + \bar{\nu}\bar{a}\right], \qquad \lambda \equiv \frac{1}{1 + \bar{b}\bar{m}\bar{\nu}}\]

  • The bracket is the inflation pressure. Only the share \(\lambda\) reaches inflation; the rule removes the rest by holding output down.
  • Baseline: \(\bar{b}\bar{m}\bar{\nu} = 1\), so \(\lambda = 1/2\).

Half of every inflation push reaches inflation; the other half is paid for in output.

Step 2 of the Model overview: substitute AS for π_t in AD, collect the Ỹ_t terms, (1 + b̄m̄ν̄)Ỹ_t, and divide. For inflation, subtract π̄ from AS and insert the result; the bracket simplifies to 1/(1 + b̄m̄ν̄).

The bracket is the inflation pressure that reaches the economy in period t: the gap inherited from last period, plus the inflation shock, plus the push from a demand shock.

λ is the book chapter’s shorthand, not the textbook’s. It lies between 0 and 1 and falls when m̄ or ν̄ rises.

The notebook checks the formula: with ō = 2 hitting the steady state, the solution gives Ỹ = −1.00 percent and π = 3.00 percent, and the share of the gap left each period is 1/(1 + b̄m̄ν̄) = 0.50.

Act II

Event 1: an inflation shock

An oil price jump pushes inflation up for one year. What does the central bank’s rule do to output?

The economy is in its steady state at 2% inflation. A shock \(\bar{o} = 2\) hits for one year only. Where do inflation and output land this year: does inflation reach 4%?

Ask students to draw it: the AS curve shifts up by 2 points. If output stayed at potential, inflation would rise to 4 percent. Does it? Use the solution of the last slide: λō = 1.

Ask also for the sign of output. Some students expect output to rise “because prices rise”.

The shock raises inflation and lowers output at the same time

How to read it: AS is the original curve, AS₁ the curve shifted up by the shock, AD the unchanged policy curve. A is the old steady state, B the economy in period 1. The slider sets the size of the shock; the title gives period-1 inflation and output.

  1. At ō = 2: B is at π = 3.00, Ỹ = −1.00.
  2. Move to 4: π = 4.00, Ỹ = −2.00. Half the shock, again.
  3. Move to 0.5: π = 2.25, Ỹ = −0.25.

A price shock brings stagflation: higher inflation and a recession

  • The answer: inflation rises only to 3%, not 4%, because output falls to −1%.
  • Half of the shock shows up as inflation, half as recession, since \(\bar{b}\bar{m}\bar{\nu} = 1\).
  • A tougher bank (higher \(\bar{m}\)) gets less inflation and a deeper recession; a fall in oil prices gives the mirror image.

Stagflation, the U.S. experience after the oil shocks of the 1970s, is what a rule does with a price shock.

Numbers: the notebook’s check (ō = 2: Ỹ = −1.00, π = 3.00) and the Fig. 6 readouts. Formulas: π₁ − π̄ = λō = 1 and Ỹ₁ = −b̄m̄λō = −1.

Why a recession? The rule sees inflation above target and raises the real rate. That is the bank’s choice: it accepts lower output to keep inflation from rising one for one.

A notebook exercise: with ō = 3, B is at π = 3.50, Ỹ = −1.50 (the Fig. 6 slider); with m̄ = 1, inflation rises less and output falls more.

After the shock

The shock is over in year 2. Does the AS curve jump back to where it started?

In year 2 the shock is gone, \(\bar{o} = 0\). Firms now expect last year’s 3% inflation. Where are inflation and output in year 2, and in year 3?

Most students say the AS curve returns to its old position because the shock is gone. Push back: what do firms expect in year 2? Students who use the formula get π₂ − 2 = ½ × 1 = 0.5 and Ỹ₂ = −0.5.

Sticky expectations keep inflation high, and the same stickiness keeps the economy in a slump.

Numbers: the notebook’s simulation table (period 1 to 5) and its formula check: 3.0000, 2.5000, 2.2500, 2.1250, 2.0625 percent; output −1, −0.5, −0.25, −0.125, −0.0625. From period 2 on, the ratio of successive gaps is exactly 0.5.

Equation (7) of the Model overview is a difference equation: applied again and again, the gap after s periods is λ^s times the starting gap. Because λ < 1, the gap always dies out.

The fall in the gap from one year to the next is (1 − λ) times the gap, proportional to the gap: the principle of transition dynamics from the Solow model (Chapter 5) holds here too.

The notebook asks: how many periods until inflation is within 0.1 point of the target? (Period 5: 2.0625.)

The whole episode

How long does the stagflation last, and how much output does it cost in total?

The shock added 2 points to inflation once. Add up the output lost over all the years that follow. Then make prices less responsive to the slump (a flatter AS curve, smaller \(\bar{\nu}\)): is the total loss larger or smaller?

Ask for a number for the total. Some students will add −1 − 0.5 − 0.25 − … and get −2. Ask them to guess the effect of a flatter AS curve before the reveal: does it spread the same loss over more years, or raise the loss?

The total cost of the shock is \(\bar{o}/\bar{\nu}\), whatever the bank does

  • The answer: the losses add up to 2% of a year’s output with \(\bar{\nu} = 1\).
  • A flatter AS curve makes it costlier: \(\bar{\nu} = 0.5\) doubles the loss to 4%; \(\bar{\nu} = 2\) halves it to 1%.
  • Prices that react little to a slump need a longer slump to come down.

Price shocks are costly twice: once in inflation, then in the recession needed to bring it down.

The Model overview’s prediction “shocks to prices hurt twice”.

A one-time shock to prices has lasting effects

An oil shock lasts a year, so it raises inflation for a year.

The shock raises inflation in year 1, firms then expect higher inflation, and the AS curve stays up. Inflation is still 2.25% in year 3, and the slump needed to undo the shock adds up to 2% of a year’s output.

Numbers: the Fig. 8 path (π₃ = 2.25 percent) and the total cost of −2.00 from the notebook.

This is the textbook’s point that price shocks are insidious. It is also why central banks watch measures of expected inflation so closely: if a shock does not move expectations, the AS curve can return at once and the slump is not needed (Fig. 20 in Act III).

Adding up the AS curve gives the cost of any episode

\[\pi_T - \pi_0 = \bar{\nu}\textstyle\sum_t \widetilde{Y}\!_t + \sum_t \bar{o}_t\]

\[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{Y}\!_t = \frac{(\pi_T - \pi_0) - \sum_t \bar{o}_t}{\bar{\nu}}\]

  • Only the move in inflation, the shocks and \(\bar{\nu}\) matter. The policy response \(\bar{m}\) is not in it.
  • Each point of disinflation costs \(1/\bar{\nu}\) points of a year’s output: the sacrifice ratio.

A tougher bank changes the shape of the slump, not its total size.

Step 4 of the Model overview. Add the AS curve, π_t − π_(t−1) = ν̄Ỹ_t + ō_t, over the periods 1 to T. On the left every middle term cancels (a telescoping sum), leaving π_T − π₀. Take T large enough for the economy to be back in a steady state.

Inflation shock: π_T − π₀ = 0 and the shocks add up to 2, so the sum is −2/ν̄. Disinflation from 4 to 2: π_T − π₀ = −2 with no shocks, so the sum is −2/ν̄ again. Demand boom: inflation starts and ends at 2 with no inflation shocks, so the sum is 0.

Chapter 9 measured the U.S. Phillips curve slope at about 1/3; the baseline ν̄ = 1 here is illustrative.

Event 2: a disinflation

The central bank lowers its target from 4% to 2%. Does a tougher bank make the disinflation cheaper?

Inflation has been 4% for years, and firms expect 4%. The bank cuts its target to 2%. Where is inflation in year 1? Then compare a gentle bank (\(\bar{m} = 0.25\)) with a tough one (\(\bar{m} = 2\)): which loses less output in total?

This is Paul Volcker’s Fed in the 1980s, in the textbook’s numbers. Ask for the year-1 inflation rate and for the comparison. Most students expect the tough bank to finish the job faster and so to lose less. Keep the guesses on the board for the reveal.

A tough central bank gets inflation down at a lower cost.

Each point of inflation lifts the nominal rate 1.5 points, so the real rate rises too.

Add π_t to both sides of the rule R_t − r̄ = m̄(π_t − π̄); the left side becomes R_t + π_t = i_t, by the Fisher equation of Chapter 8. Collect the π terms: i_t = (r̄ − m̄π̄) + (1 + m̄)π_t. With Taylor’s numbers, 2 − 1 = 1 and 1 + 0.5 = 1.5.

A limit: the rule asks for a negative nominal rate when inflation falls below −2/3 percent, which banks can hardly set. Chapter 14 returns to this zero lower bound.

The full Taylor rule also reacts to output, R − r̄ = m̄(π − π̄) + x̄Ỹ. Substituted into IS, it gives an AD curve of the same shape, steeper; with b̄ = 2 and x̄ = 1/2 the bank cancels half of a demand shock at once (notebook check: Ỹ = 1.00 instead of 2.00 for ā = 2 at the target).

A nominal rate that rises less than inflation makes inflation explode

When inflation rises, raising the interest rate a bit is enough to fight it.

If the nominal rate rises by less than inflation, the real rate falls (\(\bar{m} < 0\)), AD slopes up, and the gap grows each year. With \(\bar{m} = -1/4\) the inflation gap goes 2, 4, 8, 16 points. A stable rule raises the nominal rate more than one for one.

Numbers: the notebook’s check with m̄ = −0.25 in the baseline: b̄m̄ν̄ = −1/2, λ = 1/(1 − 0.5) = 2, and the inflation gap is 2.00, 4.00, 8.00 and 16.00 points in periods 1 to 4.

This is often called the Taylor principle. Clarida, Galí and Gertler (2000) estimate that the Fed’s response to inflation was below one for one before 1979 and well above one afterwards, one explanation for the high and unstable inflation of the 1970s (Fig. 16 shows the actual rate far below the rule then).

Should the Fed follow a rule?

Congress gives the Fed its goals, not its methods. Should the Fed commit to a formula like Taylor’s, or keep full discretion?

What the model says

  • A rule is predictable, so it anchors expectations.
  • The rule must raise rates more than one for one.
  • Taylor’s line tracks decades of policy.

What it leaves out

  • New shocks: 2008, 2020.
  • \(\bar{r}\) is unknown, and it moves.
  • Inflation and the gap are revised later.

The left column is Act I and the Fig. 16 test. The right column: no rule could have foreseen the financial crisis or the pandemic, when the Fed’s discretion (emergency lending, asset purchases) mattered a lot; the rule’s answer depends on r̄, as the Fig. 16 slider shows (a 2-point change in r̄ moves the rule by 2 points every quarter); and real-time data get revised (Fig. 17).

The research. Taylor (1993, Carnegie-Rochester Conference Series on Public Policy) proposed the rule as a description and a guide, not as a formula to follow mechanically. Kydland and Prescott (1977, Journal of Political Economy) show why commitment can beat discretion: a bank free to surprise the public with inflation ends up with higher inflation and no gain in output (the time consistency problem; Nobel Prize 2004). Barro and Gordon (1983, Journal of Monetary Economics) add that reputation can do part of a rule’s work. The Fed’s Monetary Policy Report to Congress has compared its rate with several simple rules since 2017.

The textbook’s answer: no robot, but rules as guidelines. Bernanke calls the middle ground “constrained discretion”: flexibility in the short run, a firm commitment to the inflation target over the long run.

Ask the policy students: if a rule is published, who gains from it: the central bank, markets or voters?

When expectations look ahead, the AS curve moves with the target

\[\text{AS:}\quad \pi_t = \pi^e_t + \bar{\nu}\,\widetilde{Y}\!_t + \bar{o}\]

\[\pi^e = c\,\bar{\pi}' + (1-c)\,\bar{\pi} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \widetilde{Y}\!_1 = -\bar{b}\bar{m}\lambda\,(1-c)(\bar{\pi} - \bar{\pi}')\]

  • Rational expectations: firms use all their information, including the target the bank announces.
  • \(c\) is the credibility of the announcement, between 0 (adaptive) and 1 (fully believed).

The recession of a disinflation shrinks in proportion to \(1-c\).

Equation (11) keeps expected inflation in the AS curve instead of last year’s inflation. Robert Lucas developed the idea of rational expectations (Nobel Prize 1995).

The credibility share c is the book chapter’s addition, not the textbook’s: after a cut in the target from π̄ to π̄′, firms build the share c of the cut into their expectations. The algebra of step 2 goes through with π^e in place of π_(t−1); expected inflation is above the new target by (1 − c)(π̄ − π̄′).

The notebook’s check: c = 0 gives Ỹ₁ = −1.00 and π₁ = 3.00 (Event 2), c = 1/2 gives −0.50 and 2.50, c = 1 gives 0.00 and 2.00.

The time consistency problem (Kydland and Prescott): once firms and workers have set prices and wages on the basis of low expected inflation, a central bank is tempted to stimulate for a quick boom. Firms anticipate the temptation and build it into their prices: high inflation, no gain in output. A commitment, like Ulysses tied to the mast so as not to steer toward the Sirens, can be worth more than discretion.

The Lucas critique: a model with fixed expectations gives wrong answers about a new policy, because people change their expectations when the policy changes.

Can a central bank make a disinflation cheap?

A central bank must bring inflation down. Can announcements, targets and reputation do part of the work of a recession?

What the model says

  • The slump shrinks with \(1-c\).
  • Full credibility: no slump at all.
  • Targets anchor what firms expect.

What the evidence adds

  • Credibility must be earned first.
  • Regime changes ended hyperinflations fast.
  • Targeting’s own gains are debated.

The left column is Fig. 20. The right column: in practice credibility is earned, and disinflations have rarely been free.

The research. Goodfriend and King (2005, Journal of Monetary Economics), “The incredible Volcker disinflation”, argue that the 1979–84 disinflation was costly because the public did not believe the Fed at first; expected inflation, measured in long-term interest rates, stayed high for years. Sargent (1982, “The ends of four big inflations”, in Hall, ed., Inflation: Causes and Effects, University of Chicago Press) shows that the hyperinflations of the 1920s in Austria, Hungary, Poland and Germany stopped abruptly when fiscal and monetary regimes changed, with smaller output costs than the adaptive model predicts. Ball (1994, “What determines the sacrifice ratio?”, in Mankiw, ed., Monetary Policy, University of Chicago Press) finds that quick disinflations cost less output per point than slow ones. On inflation targeting itself, Ball and Sheridan (2005, in Bernanke and Woodford, eds., The Inflation-Targeting Debate) find that OECD countries that adopted targets did not perform much better than those that did not, once other factors are accounted for; others find larger gains in emerging economies. Name it as contested.

The U.S. adopted an explicit 2 percent target in 2012, Japan in 2013. Bernanke calls it “constrained discretion”.

Ask the policy students: would you rather have a central bank with a published target and less discretion, or a trusted chair with more?

“Real people are not that naive”

A skeptic says: “This whole model assumes firms simply expect last year’s inflation. Real price setters read the news and listen to the central bank. With rational expectations, the adaptive AS/AD model is just wrong: that is the Lucas critique.”

Reply: the model needs only that expectations move slowly, and the data agree. Disinflations have cost real recessions, and the U.S. loops turn as the model says. Fig. 20 spans the range from adaptive to fully credible, and the lesson holds either way: the more the public believes the target, the cheaper low inflation is.

Take the objection seriously. Lucas (1976, Carnegie-Rochester Conference Series on Public Policy) showed that a model with fixed expectations can give wrong answers about a new policy, and modern models build rational expectations in. The textbook itself presents rational expectations as a second benchmark.

The reply. First, sticky expectations are an empirical fact more than a theory: the Volcker disinflation cost a deep recession (Goodfriend and King 2005 attribute it to low credibility), and the counterclockwise loops of Fig. 17 appear only if inflation adjusts slowly. Second, the textbook’s case study raises the question whether people can compute the implications of a policy change, and whether the cost of doing so keeps inflation sticky; research continues. Third, the two benchmarks bracket reality: the credibility slider of Fig. 20 is a way to reason between them.

Ask: what measure of expected inflation would you watch if you were a central banker? (Surveys of households and firms, professional forecasts, the gap between nominal and inflation-protected bond yields.)

What to remember from this chapter

  • A policy rule turns the IS curve into a downward AD curve; the Phillips curve is the upward AS curve. In the steady state \(\widetilde{Y}^* = 0\) and \(\pi^* = \bar{\pi}\).
  • Sticky expectations make every shock fade slowly: the gap halves each year in the baseline.
  • Price shocks bring stagflation; disinflation costs \(1/\bar{\nu}\) per point whatever \(\bar{m}\); booms are paid for with slumps, in counterclockwise loops.
  • Taylor’s rule tracks the Fed (0.75), with big gaps in the 1970s, after 2008 and in 2021–22.
  • Credibility makes low inflation cheap: targets, independence and reputation.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: what happens to output and inflation when the central bank follows a rule? A good answer: the rule makes AD slope down, the economy always returns to the target with output at potential, but because expectations are sticky the return is slow and costs output; a credible target makes it cheaper.

For the policy students, three habits: ask whether a shock is to prices or to demand (opposite or same signs); judge a disinflation by its total cost, not its depth; and ask how much the public believes the central bank.
