Learning goals

Governments spend between a third and more than half of GDP in the rich countries, and most of them have borrowed a lot. This chapter looks at the data on spending, taxes, deficits and debt, and at the accounting rules that tie them together. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • read the U.S. federal budget: where the money goes, where it comes from, and how large the deficit is, in percent of GDP and in dollars per person (Table 18.1);
  • use the flow budget constraint and the intertemporal budget constraint to compute next year’s debt, the primary and total deficits, and the taxes a given path of spending requires;
  • explain why the debt-GDP ratio falls when GDP grows faster than the interest rate, and split the changes in the U.S. ratio into deficits and growth;
  • compare government spending and debt across countries, and describe the fiscal problem of the twenty-first century: the rise of health spending;
  • in Python: write small accounting functions, reshape tables with melt and merge, draw a chart with two vertical axes, animate a bar chart, and compare two vintages of the same data.

The model in code

This chapter has no new model to solve: it describes government budgets with data. But behind the data there are a few accounting equations, which hold in every year and in every country. We turn each of them into a small Python function here, once, and the figures use them later.

The flow budget constraint (equations 18.1 and 18.2)

In every period the government’s uses of funds must equal its sources of funds:

\[\underbrace{G_t + Tr_t + iB_t}_{\text{uses}} = \underbrace{T_t + \Delta B_{t+1} + \Delta M_{t+1}}_{\text{sources}}. \tag{18.1}\]

The government buys goods and services \(G_t\), pays transfers \(Tr_t\) (Social Security, unemployment insurance, welfare) and pays interest \(i\) on its debt \(B_t\). It gets the money from taxes \(T_t\), from new borrowing \(\Delta B_{t+1} = B_{t+1} - B_t\) and from printing money \(\Delta M_{t+1}\). As in the textbook, we set \(\Delta M_{t+1} = 0\) and count transfers inside \(G_t\). Then the constraint says how the debt moves:

\[B_{t+1} = (1+i)B_t + \underbrace{G_t - T_t}_{\text{primary deficit}}. \tag{18.2}\]

The primary deficit \(G_t - T_t\) leaves out the interest payments. The total deficit \(G_t + iB_t - T_t\) includes them, and it is the deficit that newspapers (and Table 18.1) report.

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. The text in triple quotes is a one-line description. We write the interest rate as a decimal number, so 5 percent is 0.05.

def next_debt(B, G, T, i):
    """Equation (18.2): next period's debt, (1 + i) B + G - T."""
    return (1 + i) * B + G - T


def primary_deficit(G, T):
    """Primary deficit: spending minus taxes, leaving out the interest payments."""
    return G - T


def total_deficit(G, T, i, B):
    """Total deficit: spending plus the interest on the debt, minus taxes."""
    return G + i * B - T

The intertemporal budget constraint (equations 18.5 and 18.6)

In a world that lasts two periods, all debt must be repaid by the end: \(B_3 = 0\). Combining the flow constraints of the two periods gives the intertemporal budget constraint:

\[\underbrace{G_1 + \frac{G_2}{1+i}}_{\text{pdv of spending}} + \underbrace{(1+i)B_1}_{\text{initial debt}} = \underbrace{T_1 + \frac{T_2}{1+i}}_{\text{pdv of taxes}}. \tag{18.5}\]

A future amount is worth less today, so we divide it by \(1+i\): that is its present discounted value (pdv). Moving the taxes to the left gives equation (18.6): the pdv of the budget balances \(T - G\) must pay off the initial debt \((1+i)B_1\). The budget must balance, not period by period, but in present value.

Here is a two-period example with the textbook’s initial debt of 25 trillion dollars. The government spends 7 trillion in each period, collects 5 trillion of taxes in period 1, and must find the taxes of period 2.

def pdv(first, second, i):
    """Present discounted value of an amount in period 1 and one in period 2 (equation 18.5)."""
    return first + second / (1 + i)

The debt-GDP ratio

What matters for a country’s ability to pay is not the debt in dollars but the debt relative to GDP, the tax base. Let nominal GDP grow at the rate \(g\), and write \(b = B/Y\) for the debt-GDP ratio and \(d\) for the primary deficit as a share of GDP. Dividing equation (18.2) by GDP gives

\[b_{\text{next}} = \frac{1+i}{1+g}\, b + d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_{\text{next}} - b \approx d + (i - g)\, b.\]

The ratio rises with the primary deficit \(d\). It also rises when the interest rate is above the growth rate, and it falls when GDP grows faster than the interest rate, even with a balanced primary budget.

Python note. path = [] starts an empty list, and path.append(b) adds one value at its end. For a pandas Series with years as row labels, deficit.loc[1951:1974] keeps the years 1951 to 1974 (both included), and .sum() adds them up.

def next_debt_ratio(b, i, g, d):
    """Equation (18.2) divided by GDP: next year's debt ratio, (1 + i) / (1 + g) b + d."""
    return (1 + i) / (1 + g) * b + d


def debt_ratio_path(b, i, g, deficits):
    """The debt ratio after each year of a list of primary deficits (all in percent of GDP)."""
    path = []
    for d in deficits:
        b = next_debt_ratio(b, i, g, d)
        path.append(b)
    return path


def debt_change_parts(debt, deficit, start, end):
    """Split the change in the debt ratio from start to end into primary deficits and the rest."""
    change = debt[end] - debt[start]
    from_deficits = deficit.loc[start + 1:end].sum()
    return change, from_deficits, change - from_deficits

The part of a change that is not explained by primary deficits, change - from_deficits, is the effect of interest minus growth, \((i-g)\,b\), summed over the years. We use it on the U.S. data in Figure 2.

Saving and investment (equation 18.7)

Adding and subtracting taxes in the national income identity splits total saving into three parts:

\[\underbrace{(Y - T - C)}_{\text{private saving}} + \underbrace{(T - G)}_{\text{government saving}} + \underbrace{(IM - EX)}_{\text{foreign saving}} = I. \tag{18.7}\]

Investment is financed by households and firms, by the government and by foreigners. A budget deficit is negative government saving, so private or foreign saving must fill the gap, or investment falls. That fall is called crowding out.

def private_and_foreign_saving(investment, government_saving):
    """Equation (18.7) solved for the saving of households, firms and foreigners: I - (T - G)."""
    return investment - government_saving

Tools for the data

Four small tools help with the figures. period_mean averages a series over some years, and add_period_means draws those averages on a chart. per_person turns a share of GDP into dollars per person. actual_and_projection and one_legend_entry prepare the long-run projections of Figures 6 and 7, where actual data (solid lines) are followed by projections (dashed lines).

Python note. zip(columns, colors) hands over one (column, color) pair at a time to the loop. fig.add_vrect shades a band between two x values, and fig.add_shape(type="line", ...) draws a straight line between two points.

def period_mean(series, start, end):
    """Average of a Series with years as row labels, from start to end (both included)."""
    return series.loc[start:end].mean()


def add_period_means(fig, data, columns, colors, start, end):
    """Shade the years start-end and draw each column's average over them (Figures 1 and 5)."""
    fig.add_vrect(x0=start - 0.5, x1=end + 0.5, fillcolor=GRAY, opacity=0.15, line_width=0)
    for column, color in zip(columns, colors):
        mean = period_mean(data[column], start, end)
        fig.add_shape(type="line", x0=start - 0.5, x1=end + 0.5, y0=mean, y1=mean,
                      line=dict(color=color, width=3, dash="dot"))
    return fig


def per_person(percent_of_gdp, gdp_billions, population_millions):
    """Dollars per person of a budget line given in percent of GDP (Table 18.1)."""
    return percent_of_gdp / 100 * gdp_billions * 1e9 / (population_millions * 1e6)

Python note. melt turns a wide table (one column per series) into a long one (one row per year and series), which is the shape Plotly Express likes. .map({0: ..., 1: ...}) replaces each value by its label, .copy() makes an independent copy, and pd.concat([a, b]) stacks two tables. trace.name.rsplit(", ", 1) cuts a text such as "Revenues, actual" at its last comma, into its two parts.

def actual_and_projection(data, names):
    """Long table for Figures 6 and 7: one row per year and series, actual data and CBO projection.

    FY2025 is repeated in the projection, so that the dashed line starts where the solid line ends."""
    long = data.rename(columns=names).melt(id_vars=["year", "projection"], value_vars=list(names.values()),
                                           var_name="series", value_name="percent")
    long["segment"] = long.projection.map({0: "actual", 1: "CBO projection"})
    joint = long[long.year == 2025].copy()
    joint["segment"] = "CBO projection"
    return pd.concat([long, joint]).sort_values(["series", "segment", "year"])


def one_legend_entry(fig):
    """Plotly Express names each trace 'series, segment': keep one legend entry per series."""
    for trace in fig.data:
        series, segment = trace.name.rsplit(", ", 1)
        trace.update(name=series, legendgroup=series, showlegend=(segment == "actual"))
    return fig

What to notice.

  • In the 1930s the federal government was small: revenue averaged 5.9 percent of GDP. World War II changed that. Spending peaked at 42.7 percent of GDP in FY1944, almost all of it for national defense.
  • In the two decades after the war, spending and revenue were both close to 17–18 percent of GDP, and the budget was roughly balanced (choose FY1950–1969).
  • From 1970 spending stayed above revenue in almost every year. FY1998–2001 were the exceptions, the only surpluses since 1969. Spending jumped in the Great Recession (FY2009) and in the Covid-19 pandemic (FY2020).
  • The textbook’s figure note says both averaged “about 20 percent” in recent decades. Choose FY1975–2024: spending averaged 21.1 percent, but revenue only 17.3 percent. That gap is the deficit of Figure 2.

What the projection means for the debt

The projected primary deficits are not zero, so the debt keeps growing. How fast the ratio grows depends on the interest rate \(i\) and on the growth rate of nominal GDP \(g\), through debt_ratio_path. The sliders also let you cut the primary deficit in every year from FY2026, by raising taxes or cutting spending: the two “possible solutions” of the textbook.

Summary

  • The U.S. federal government spends about 23 percent of GDP and collects about 17 percent. The deficit, about 6 percent of GDP in recent years, is financed by selling bonds, and the debt held by the public is back near 100 percent of GDP, the level of 1946.
  • The flow budget constraint links each year’s deficit to the change in the debt. The intertemporal budget constraint says that the budget must balance in present value: borrowing today means surpluses, and higher taxes, later.
  • What matters is the debt relative to GDP. When GDP grows faster than the interest rate, the ratio falls even without surpluses: that is how the United States paid down its World War II debt.
  • U.S. government spending is low compared with other rich countries, and its debt ratio is typical. Large deficits have not lowered the U.S. investment rate, because private and foreign saving rose.
  • The fiscal problem of the twenty-first century is the rise of health spending, in the U.S. and in every rich country. Chapter 19 turns to the international economy, including the foreign saving that has financed much of the U.S. deficit.
