Today: three questions

  • How do we measure inflation? The growth rate of a price index, U.S. inflation since 1960, prices across the years, and the world.
  • Where does inflation come from? The quantity theory of money, neutrality, and two tests with U.S. history and 165 economies.

These are the three acts of the deck and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide.

The headline result: in the long run, inflation is money growth beyond the growth of real GDP; nominal interest rates carry inflation with them; and very high inflation comes from governments that pay their bills by printing money.

Two famous one-liners frame the chapter. Milton Friedman: inflation is a monetary phenomenon. Thomas Sargent: persistent high inflation is a fiscal one. By the end of the deck students should see why both are right.

Measuring inflation

Act I

Inflation is the growth rate of the price level

\[\pi_t = \frac{P_{t+1} - P_t}{P_t} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P_T = P_0\,(1+\pi)^T\]

  • \(P_t\) is a price index: the cost of a basket of goods, set to 100 in a base year. The CPI uses a household’s basket; the GDP deflator uses all of GDP.
  • When \(\pi_t < 0\), prices fall: deflation.
  • Small rates add up. By the rule of 70, at 2% a year prices double in about 35 years.

Inflation is about the change in prices, not their level.

The left equation is the definition. Multiplying by P_t and adding P_t gives P_{t+1} = (1 + π_t)P_t; chaining the years gives the right equation when the rate is constant. Fig. 1 builds the U.S. price level in exactly this way from the inflation rates.

Rule of 70: set P_T/P_0 = 2 and take logs, T ln(1 + π) = ln 2, and ln 2 is about 0.70. So the doubling time is about 70 divided by inflation in percent. The notebook’s Check 1 confirms it: a price index growing at 2 percent for 35 years ends at 200.

The same algebra gives average inflation over many years: π̄ = (P_T/P_0)^{1/T} − 1, the formula Fig. 3 uses for decades.

Ask: a pension of 1,000 dollars a year that is never raised: what does it buy after 35 years at 2 percent? (What 500 dollars bought when it started.)

Inflation is not the same as high prices

Prices are high today, so inflation must be high.

The U.S. price level in 2025 was the highest ever, about 4 times its 1980 level. But inflation was 2.63% in 2025, against 13.50% in 1980. Inflation measures how fast prices change.

The myth mixes up a level and a growth rate, the distinction of Chapter 3. In the CPI of Table 8.1, rescaled to 2025 = 100, the price level of 1980 is 25.59: a quarter of today’s. So prices are far higher now, but they are rising much more slowly.

When people say “prices are still high” after an inflation burst, they are right about the level. When inflation falls back to 2 percent, prices keep rising, only more slowly. They do not fall back, unless there is deflation, and the 2009 episode shows that deflation is rare and small.

For the policy students: a government that promises to “bring prices down” after a burst is promising deflation, which central banks avoid. What it can promise is lower inflation.

Currency is the narrowest measure: paper money and coins. The monetary base adds the reserves banks keep at the central bank. M1 adds checking accounts, and M2 adds savings and money market accounts.

The data-hygiene lesson: M1 more than triples in one month in 2020. Nobody printed 11 trillion dollars. In May 2020 the Federal Reserve moved savings deposits into M1. Always check a jump against the definitions before you interpret it.

Today’s money is not backed by gold: it is fiat money, valued because others accept it. Digital payment apps move existing money between accounts; they do not create new money.

The quantity equation: money times velocity equals nominal GDP

\[M_t\,V_t = P_t\,Y_t\]

  • \(V_t\), the velocity of money, counts how many times a year each dollar is spent.
  • Nominal GDP, \(P_tY_t\), is all the spending on final goods in a year.
  • Example: money of 100 and nominal GDP of 200 mean each dollar changes hands twice a year, \(V = 2\).

On its own this is almost a definition of \(V\). It becomes a theory once we add three assumptions.

The example is the chapter’s illustrative economy: money 100, velocity 2, real GDP 200, so the price level is 1.

The equation holds by construction if we measure V as PY/M. The content of the quantity theory lies in the extra equations of the next slide, above all the assumption that V and Y do not respond to M.

Ask: when you pay by card instead of cash, does velocity change? (It can: the same money supports more spending. That is why velocity drifts slowly with payment habits and technology.)

Four equations pin down money, velocity, prices and output

Equation Name What it takes as given
\(M_tV_t = P_tY_t\) quantity equation nothing
\(Y_t = \bar{Y}_t\) classical dichotomy real GDP, from the growth models of Chapters 4 to 6
\(V_t = \bar{V}\) constant velocity payment habits and technology
\(M_t = \bar{M}_t\) money supply the central bank’s choice

The classical dichotomy: in the long run, real GDP is set by real forces alone. Money and prices do not enter the growth models.

As everywhere in the book, a bar marks a variable taken as given from outside the model. Three of the four equations simply set a variable equal to something given; the fourth links them.

The classical dichotomy is an assumption we have been making all along. The Solow and Romer models determine output from capital, labor, ideas and productivity. There is no price level in them.

Constant velocity is the shakiest assumption, and Fig. 3’s velocity chart tests it. The money supply as a choice of the central bank is a simplification: Chapter 12 models how central banks decide.

Solving the model: the price level and inflation

\[P^*_t = \frac{\bar{M}_t\,\bar{V}}{\bar{Y}_t}\]

  • The spending power of money over the goods there are to buy.
  • With \(\bar{M} = 100\), \(\bar{V} = 2\), \(\bar{Y} = 200\): \(P^* = 1\).

\[\pi^* = \bar{g}_M - \bar{g}_Y\]

  • Money that grows as fast as real GDP only keeps up with the extra goods.
  • With \(\bar{g}_M = 5\%\) and \(\bar{g}_Y = 3\%\): \(\pi^* = 2\%\) (exactly 1.94%).

Inflation is money growth beyond the growth of real GDP: “too much money chasing too few goods”.

Left: substitute the three given variables into the quantity equation and divide by Ȳ.

Right: take logs of the quantity equation in two consecutive years and subtract. Each change in a log is approximately a growth rate, so g_M + g_V = g_P + g_Y. With constant velocity g_V = 0, and π = g_P.

The exact discrete-time version is π = (1 + g_M)/(1 + g_Y) − 1 = 1.05/1.03 − 1 = 1.94 percent. The notebook’s Check 3 simulates the economy year by year and gets 1.942 percent in every year. For the moderate rates of Figs. 1 to 5 the shortcut is close; for hyperinflations it is not (with 100 percent money growth: 94 percent exact, 97 by the shortcut), but the message is the same.

Changes in levels move prices once; changes in growth rates move inflation

A change in … the price level \(P^*\) inflation \(\pi^*\)
money \(\bar{M}\), up 1% once +1% no lasting change
velocity \(\bar{V}\), up 1% once +1% no lasting change
real GDP \(\bar{Y}\), up 1% once −1% no lasting change
money growth \(\bar{g}_M\), up 1 point rises faster every year +1 point
real GDP growth \(\bar{g}_Y\), up 1 point rises more slowly −1 point

Real GDP growth stays within a few percent; money growth can be anything. So big, lasting differences in inflation must come from money growth.

Taking logs of P* = MV/Y gives ln P* = ln M + ln V − ln Y: every elasticity is one in size. The notebook’s Check 2 confirms it: 1 percent more money or velocity raises P* by 1.000 percent; 1 percent more real GDP lowers it by 0.990 percent (1/1.01, an elasticity describes small changes).

The bottom rows are the key prediction. Growth models keep real GDP growth in a narrow band, a few percent a year. Money growth is a policy choice and has ranged from 2 percent to thousands of percent. Figs. 3 and 4 test exactly this.

Ask: an oil shock raises prices for a year. Is that inflation in this model? (It is a one-time jump in the price level if it lowers Ȳ once; only lasting money growth keeps inflation high.)

Money is neutral in the long run

\[\frac{\lambda\,p_{\text{bread}}}{\lambda\,p_{\text{book}}} = \frac{p_{\text{bread}}}{p_{\text{book}}} \qquad\qquad \frac{\lambda W}{\lambda P} = \frac{W}{P}\]

  • Multiply money by \(\lambda\), say 2: the price level doubles, from 1 to 2.
  • If every price and wage doubles, every relative price and the real wage stay put: still 10 loaves for an hour of work.
  • Nobody has a reason to work, buy or produce differently: real GDP stays at \(\bar{Y}\).

Neutrality holds in the long run. In the short run, prices and wages adjust slowly, and money can move real GDP: Chapters 9 to 13.

The numbers are from the notebook’s Check 2: doubling money doubles P*, and a real wage of 10 loaves an hour stays at 10 loaves.

Relative prices and the real wage are the signals people act on. If money changes all nominal values in proportion, the signals are unchanged. A currency reform that replaces every old note with λ new ones is the same experiment, and countries that have done it saw no change in real output from the reform itself.

Why only the long run? If some prices are slow to adjust, a burst of money first changes relative prices, and firms produce more for a while. The short-run model is built around this idea.

Printing money does not make a country richer

If the government printed more money, everyone could buy more.

Doubling the money supply doubles the price level, from 1 to 2. The real wage stays at 10 loaves an hour, and real GDP stays where the growth models put it. Only more goods make a country richer.

The myth treats money as wealth. For one person it is: a person who finds a hundred-dollar bill is richer. For a whole country, more money with the same goods only raises the prices of the goods.

The argument is the neutrality slide: with V and Y given, P rises in proportion to M. Venezuela and the hyperinflations of Act III are the extreme proof: money grew by thousands of percent, and real GDP fell.

The subtle point for the policy students: printing money does transfer resources, from the people who hold money to the government that spends it. That is the inflation tax of Act III. It does not create resources.

Interest rates and deficits

Act III

The Fisher equation: the nominal rate pays the real return plus inflation

\[1 + R = \frac{1+i}{1+\pi}\]

\[i \approx R + \pi \qquad R = i - \pi\]

  • \(i\) is paid in dollars, \(R\) in goods.
  • \(R = 2\%\) and \(\pi = 2\%\) give \(i = 4\%\) (exactly 4.04%).
  • In the long run \(R\) is the marginal product of capital, so \(i^* = R + \bar{g}_M - \bar{g}_Y\): one more point of money growth adds one point to \(i\).

The real rate cannot be looked up: we compute it from the nominal rate and inflation.

The derivation follows one dollar. Today it buys 1/P_t goods. Saved, it returns 1 + i dollars next year, which buy (1 + i)/P_{t+1} goods. The ratio is the return in goods, 1 + R = (1 + i)/(1 + π). Multiplying out, 1 + i = 1 + R + π + Rπ, and dropping the tiny product Rπ gives the Fisher equation.

The long-run version combines it with the quantity theory: with R = 2 percent and 3 percent real growth, money growth of 5 percent gives i* = 4 percent, and 6 percent gives 5 percent (notebook Check 4).

When inflation is high the shortcut fails: the exact formula is R = (1 + i)/(1 + π) − 1. We see a case on the myth slide.

The second bullet is the textbook’s argument. In the long run the real rate equals the marginal product of capital, which cannot be negative. A negative real rate for years means something else is going on: the central bank holding the nominal rate down, or inflation surprising lenders. Both are short-run stories.

A high interest rate is not a high return

A savings account paying 14% a year is a great deal.

It depends on inflation. In Turkey in 2022, a 14% rate with 72% inflation was a real return of −34%: savers lost a third of their purchasing power. And taxes on nominal interest make it worse.

The Turkey numbers are from Fig. 5 and the notebook’s Check 4. The shortcut R = i − π gives −58 percent; at rates this high it is poor. The exact formula, (1 + i)/(1 + π) − 1 = 1.14/1.72 − 1, gives −33.7 percent.

The U.S. nominal peak of 14 percent in 1981 is the same lesson in milder form: inflation was also high, so the real return was modest.

The tax point is on the next slide: a tax on nominal interest also taxes the part that only makes up for inflation.

For the policy students: an interest rate is only informative next to the inflation rate. The same holds for wage growth, pension growth or a budget that grows “by 5 percent”.

Surprise inflation and nominal taxes move wealth around

A surprise

\[R = R^e - (\pi - \pi^e)\]

  • A loan is set at 4%, expecting 2% inflation.
  • Inflation turns out 8%: the lender earns −4% in real terms; the borrower gains.

A tax on nominal interest

\[(1-\tau)\,i - \pi = (1-\tau)R - \tau\pi\]

  • \(\tau = 25\%\), \(R = 2\%\): at 2% inflation the after-tax real return is +1.00%; at 8% it is −0.50%.
  • The effective tax rate rises from 50% to 125%.

Left: lender and borrower set i = R^e + π^e. Once the rate is fixed, the realized real rate is i − π. Every point of unexpected inflation takes a point of real return from the lender and gives it to the borrower. Unexpected disinflation does the opposite. The numbers are from the notebook’s Check 5.

Right: the tax applies to all of i, including the part that only compensates for inflation. The after-tax real return is (1 − τ)R − τπ. As a share of the real return, the tax paid is τ(1 + π/R): 50 percent at 2 percent inflation, 125 percent at 8 percent. Even fully expected inflation then lowers the return to saving.

These are two of the textbook’s costs of inflation, made into numbers. Martin Feldstein argued that the interaction of inflation with taxes discouraged investment in the 1970s.

Inflation does not hit everyone equally

Inflation is a general rise in prices, so it hurts everyone the same way.

Surprise inflation moves wealth from lenders, savers and fixed pensions (the $10,000 pension of 1970 lost 61% by 1983) to borrowers. And the inflation tax falls on holders of cash, not on owners of land, houses or shares.

The myth looks only at prices. Inflation is also a change in the real value of every contract written in dollars. Whoever owes dollars gains when they lose value; whoever is owed dollars loses.

Who holds cash? Disproportionately poorer households, who have no bank account or few other assets. So the inflation tax is often regressive.

Who gains? Borrowers with fixed-rate debt: homeowners with fixed mortgages, and governments with debt in their own currency.

For the policy students: the distribution of the costs depends on how contracts are written. The next policy slides ask whether contracts and taxes should be indexed, and whether controls on prices can help.

The costs of inflation come from surprises and from rules written in dollars

When inflation is a surprise

  • Wealth moves from lenders to borrowers.
  • Fixed incomes and fixed-rate contracts lose value.
  • Banks that borrow short and lend long at fixed rates can fail.

Even when it is expected

  • Taxes on nominal returns discourage saving and investment.
  • Prices that adjust at different speeds distort relative prices.
  • Shoe-leather costs of holding less cash; menu costs of changing prices.

If inflation were steady, known and built into every contract and tax, it would be little more than a nuisance.

This is the textbook’s list in two columns. The left column needs a surprise; the right column does not.

The bank example: a bank that pays market rates on deposits but holds old fixed-rate loans sees its costs rise with inflation while its income does not. This is the U.S. savings-and-loan problem of the late 1970s and 1980s.

Relative prices matter because they tell people what to produce. If some prices adjust every week and others once a year, inflation makes the slow ones look cheap for a while, and resources go to the wrong uses.

Shoe-leather and menu costs are small at low inflation and large at high inflation: in a hyperinflation people spend hours a day getting rid of cash.

The takeaway leads to the policy question of the next slide: why not index everything?

Should contracts and taxes be indexed to inflation?

Indexing ties payments to the price level: pensions, wages, bonds and tax brackets rise with inflation. Should governments index more of them?

For indexing

  • It removes the redistribution of surprise inflation.
  • Taxing real, not nominal, interest ends the tax on saving.
  • Indexed bonds reveal the inflation markets expect.

Against, or with care

  • Indexed wages pass a supply shock on to all prices.
  • Wide indexing can lock in inertia and make disinflation harder.
  • Tax rules in real terms are complex to run.

Left column. Feldstein (1976, American Economic Review) shows how taxing nominal interest raises the effective tax on capital income when inflation rises, and argues for indexing the tax base. Shiller (1997, Brookings Papers on Economic Activity) documents how little indexation people choose for themselves and calls it a puzzle. Campbell and Shiller (1996, NBER Macroeconomics Annual) score the case for inflation-indexed government bonds: they protect savers and let economists read expected inflation from the gap between nominal and indexed yields. The U.S. Treasury now issues such bonds (TIPS), and Social Security benefits rise automatically with the CPI.

Right column. Gray (1976, Journal of Monetary Economics) shows that wage indexation shields the economy from monetary shocks but amplifies real shocks such as an oil-price rise. Economies with near-universal indexing, such as Brazil and Israel before their stabilizations, found that indexing kept yesterday’s inflation in today’s prices and made it hard to stop.

Ask the policy students: which payments in your country are indexed? Pensions? The minimum wage? Tax brackets? Who loses from the ones that are not?

Can price controls stop inflation?

In August 1971, President Nixon froze U.S. wages and prices to break a “wage-price spiral”. Can a government stop inflation by forbidding prices to rise?

What happened

  • Inflation slowed while the freeze lasted.
  • Controls came back in 1973; shortages and gasoline lines followed.
  • Prices jumped when the controls ended in 1974.

The lesson of the model

  • Controls treat a symptom: with \(\pi^* = \bar{g}_M - \bar{g}_Y\), the cause is money growth.
  • Pent-up inflation returns when controls end.
  • Meanwhile, frozen prices stop signalling scarcity.

The story is the textbook’s case study. Blinder and Newton (1981, Journal of Monetary Economics) estimate that the 1971–1974 controls held the price level down while they were in force and that the effect was largely undone after decontrol. Rockoff (1984, Drastic Measures, Cambridge University Press) reviews the history of U.S. wage and price controls, including the two world wars, and finds the same pattern of temporary success when the underlying money growth is not addressed.

The stabilization plans of the 1980s in Argentina (the Austral plan) and Brazil (the Cruzado plan) froze prices without fixing the budget, and inflation soon came back. Israel’s plan of the same decade also used temporary controls, but together with a large cut in the deficit, and it lasted. The difference is the subject of the budget-constraint slides next.

For the policy students: today’s version of the question is price caps on energy or rents. The same trade-off applies: a cap can protect households from a spike, but it blunts the price signal that brings new supply.

The government budget constraint: spending is paid by taxes, borrowing or new money

\[\underset{\text{uses}}{G} \;=\; \underset{\text{taxes}}{T} \;+\; \underset{\text{new debt}}{\Delta B} \;+\; \underset{\text{new money}}{\Delta M}\]

  • Spending 25% of GDP, taxes 20%, new borrowing 2%: new money must be 3% of GDP.
  • If lenders stop lending: 5% of GDP.
  • The revenue from new money is seigniorage, the inflation tax: it is paid by everyone who holds money.

A government that cannot tax or borrow has one source left: the printing press.

The numbers come from the notebook’s budget-constraint cell. Solving for the new money: ΔM = G − T − ΔB.

Why is new money a tax? When the government doubles the money supply and spends the new half, the price level doubles. The currency people already held now buys half as much. They paid for the government’s spending through the fall in the value of their cash. A 200-dollar holding buys what 100 dollars bought before.

Who escapes it? People who hold their wealth in land, houses or shares: those prices rise with the others.

This is Sargent’s point: if the root cause of high inflation is money growth, the root cause of fast money growth is usually a government budget that cannot be balanced any other way.

The printing press raises little, and it has a ceiling

\[\frac{\Delta M}{PY} = \frac{\bar{g}_M}{(1+\bar{g}_M)\,\bar{V}} \approx \frac{\pi^* + \bar{g}_Y}{\bar{V}}\]

Money growth Seigniorage, % of GDP
5% 0.40
100% 4.17

ceiling \(1/\bar{V}\) 8.33
  • Velocity of currency \(\bar{V} = 12\): with 2% inflation and 3% growth, new money pays for about 0.4% of GDP.
  • A deficit of 5% of GDP needs money growth of 150% and inflation of about 143%.
  • U.S. new currency: 0.40% of GDP in 1975, 0.96% at most, in 2020.

The formula divides new money by nominal GDP and uses the quantity equation, M = PY/V. Here money means currency, the money the government itself issues; for the United States its velocity is about 12.

The table is the notebook’s Check 7. Even 10,000 percent money growth raises less than the ceiling of 1/V = 8.33 percent of GDP, because g/(1 + g) is always below 1. In practice the ceiling is lower: in a hyperinflation people flee from money, velocity rises, and the base of the tax shrinks.

The deficit row solves the formula for g_M: σV/(1 − σV) with σ = 5 percent and V = 12 gives 0.6/0.4 = 150 percent money growth. A deficit close to the ceiling needs explosive money growth: that is the arithmetic of hyperinflation.

The U.S. numbers are new currency each year as a share of GDP, from the notebook (1975: 0.40, 1980: 0.38, 2000: 0.34, 2020: 0.96, 2025: 0.15). Even the Great Inflation raised very little, which is why the textbook says the fiscal story does not explain the U.S. 1970s.

Hyperinflations

A hyperinflation is inflation above about 500% a year. How high can it go, and once it starts, does it fade slowly over decades or end within a few years?

Ask students to guess the highest annual rate they think a country has had since 1960, and how long a hyperinflation typically lasts.

Remind them of the arithmetic: a deficit close to the seigniorage ceiling needs ever faster money growth.

The quantity theory says a hyperinflation ends when money growth falls. The budget constraint says money growth can fall only if spending falls, taxes rise or lending resumes. Sargent (1982, “The Ends of Four Big Inflations”) studied Austria, Hungary, Poland and Germany after World War I: each ended abruptly once a credible fiscal reform and an independent central bank were in place.

The second difficulty is coordination. If everyone expects high inflation, firms raise prices and workers demand raises in anticipation, and inflation continues. Everyone has to be convinced at once that it will stop.

Both difficulties explain why high inflation recurs: Argentina had high inflation for decades.

The difference between high inflation and hyperinflation is a matter of degree, not of kind. The quantity theory works the same way at 100 and at 10,000 percent. What makes the extreme cases explode is the seigniorage ceiling: as people hold less money, the government needs ever faster money growth for the same revenue.

Israel is the counterexample to the “controls never work” lesson: its stabilization plan used temporary price controls together with a large fiscal adjustment and a fixed exchange rate, and inflation stayed down.

Should central banks be independent of the government?

Every government is tempted to print money to pay its bills. Should the central bank be shielded from the treasury, and can that shield hold when the budget breaks?

The case for independence

  • It closes the printing-press option before a crisis.
  • A bank that cannot be ordered to print makes low inflation credible.
  • More independence has gone with lower inflation.

Its limits

  • The bank is still part of the state; politicians appoint its head.
  • If deficits persist, pressure on the central bank builds.
  • Independence asks who holds unelected officials to account.

Left column. Kydland and Prescott (1977, Journal of Political Economy) and Barro and Gordon (1983, Journal of Monetary Economics) show why a policymaker with discretion ends up with too much inflation: the time-inconsistency problem. Rogoff (1985, Quarterly Journal of Economics) proposes delegating policy to a “conservative” central banker. Alesina and Summers (1993, Journal of Money, Credit and Banking) find that rich countries with more independent central banks had lower and more stable inflation, with no cost in growth. Cukierman, Webb and Neyapti (1992, World Bank Economic Review) build an index of legal independence and find that in developing countries the turnover of central bank governors matters more than the law on paper.

Right column. Sargent and Wallace (1981, Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis Quarterly Review) show that if the fiscal authority does not adjust, a tight central bank only postpones inflation: “some unpleasant monetarist arithmetic”. Posen (1995, NBER Macroeconomics Annual) argues that the link between independence and low inflation partly reflects the political support for low inflation that produces both.

In the euro area the separation is sharper still: national governments tax and spend, and a multinational central bank sets monetary policy.

Ask the policy students: what should a central bank do if the government it depends on cannot pay its debts?

“Velocity is not constant, so the quantity theory is useless”

A skeptic says: “U.S. M2 velocity rose from 1.85 in 1990 to 2.18 in 1997 and fell to 1.16 in 2021. In the 2010s money grew 7.4% a year and inflation was 1.7%. A theory built on constant velocity cannot guide policy.”

Reply: velocity drifts by a few percent a year; money growth differs by hundreds of percent. Over decades and across 165 economies the band holds (correlation 0.85), and in every hyperinflation money growth came first. Velocity explains the gap, not the slope.

Take the objection seriously: it is right about low-inflation economies in the short and medium run. Central banks stopped targeting money growth in the 1980s and 1990s for exactly this reason, and today they set interest rates instead (Chapter 12).

The reply in three parts. First, scale: the quantity theory is a theory of large and lasting differences, and those are dominated by money growth. Lucas (1980, American Economic Review) shows that once short-run noise is filtered out, U.S. inflation moves one for one with money growth. McCandless and Weber (1995, Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis Quarterly Review) find a correlation close to one between long-run money growth and inflation across a large sample of countries. Second, the gap: velocity changes shift the points off the line by roughly the same amount, as the 4-point U.S. gap shows; they do not remove the slope. Third, the limits: Teles, Uhlig and Valle e Azevedo (2016, Economic Journal) find that the one-for-one relation is strong in high-inflation periods and weaker when inflation is low and stable, which is what the theory with drifting velocity predicts.

Ask: in which countries would you trust the quantity theory most for a forecast? (Those with high and variable money growth.)

What to remember from this chapter

  • Inflation is the growth rate of the price level. A price index moves prices across years: \(X_b = X_a\,P_b/P_a\).
  • The quantity theory: \(P^* = \bar{M}\bar{V}/\bar{Y}\) and \(\pi^* = \bar{g}_M - \bar{g}_Y\). In the long run money is neutral, and U.S. decades and 165 economies line up as predicted.
  • Velocity is not constant: it explains the gap between money growth and inflation, not the link.
  • The Fisher equation, \(i = R + \pi\): real rates are often negative, surprise inflation helps borrowers, and nominal taxes tax saving.
  • \(G = T + \Delta B + \Delta M\): very high inflation comes from governments that cannot tax or borrow. Hyperinflations end with a fiscal fix.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: what makes prices rise, and why do some countries let inflation explode? A good answer: in the long run prices rise when money grows faster than output, and governments let money grow fast when they cannot pay their bills any other way.

For the policy students, three habits: compare every interest rate and growth rate with inflation; ask who holds the contracts written in dollars; and ask how a government pays for its spending.
