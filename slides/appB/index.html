A map of the data

Act I

Most numbers start at a national statistical office, then get collected

  • Producers: national statistical offices, such as the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) and Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), measure GDP, prices and jobs.
  • Collectors: FRED, the World Bank, the IMF, the OECD, Eurostat, the ECB and the BIS gather these numbers, check them and put them in one place.
  • Research datasets: the Penn World Table and the Maddison Project rebuild the numbers to compare countries and centuries.
  • You: one line of Python per series.

That is why the same series often appears in several sources, and why the copies can differ.

The supply chain of a macroeconomic number. The BEA computes U.S. GDP; FRED copies it within days; the World Bank and the IMF copy it into their cross-country databases; the Penn World Table and Maddison rework it with their own prices and population figures every few years.

Why write code when every site has a Download button?

A skeptic says: “I can click Download and open the file in Excel. Why learn five libraries?”

Reply: code is a record. It says exactly which series, which countries and which dates you used; it reruns in seconds when the data are revised; and the same three lines work for 197 countries as for one.

The Devil’s advocate of this deck comes early, because it motivates everything else.

Three arguments for code:

  1. Reproducibility: a notebook documents every step. A spreadsheet built by hand does not say where its numbers came from.
  2. Updating: statistical offices revise their numbers. Rerunning a cell gets the latest vintage; redoing clicks by hand is slow and error-prone.
  3. Scale: in Act III a single wbgapi call downloads two indicators for 197 countries.

Concede the point that matters: for a one-off look at one series, the website is fine. FRED’s website is excellent for browsing and for finding codes.

Idea 1: every series has a short code, and codes are precise

  • Names are vague (“GDP per capita” has seven versions at the World Bank); codes are exact.
  • Find the code first: the source’s search box, or a search function in Python.

Step one of every download: find the right code.

The first of three ideas from B.1. The FRED code is the end of the series’ web address: fred.stlouisfed.org/series/UNRATE.

The “seven versions” is a preview of Act III, where wb.series.info(q=“GDP per capita”) returns 7 indicators: current dollars, local currency, constant 2015 dollars, growth, constant local currency, PPP current and PPP constant.

Idea 2: read the units before you compute anything

  • Percent or fraction? 4.2 or 0.042
  • Dollars or thousands of dollars? Maddison counts population in thousands, the Penn World Table in millions.
  • Current or constant prices? Only constant prices remove inflation.
  • Monthly, quarterly or annual? FRED’s GDP is quarterly; its unemployment rate is monthly.
  • Seasonally adjusted or not?

A wrong unit gives a wrong answer that looks right.

The second idea. The population example is real and comes from the notebook outputs in Act V: Zimbabwe in 2022 has a population of 15204.66 in Maddison (thousands of people) and 16.07 in the Penn World Table (millions). Dividing GDP by the wrong one is an error of a factor of 1,000.

FRED’s notes on every series page state the units, the frequency and the seasonal adjustment. At the World Bank, the units are in the indicator name (“constant 2015 US$”).

Idea 3: numbers are revised, so each download is a vintage

  • Statistical offices revise their numbers as better information arrives.
  • The growth rate you download today for 2024 may differ from the one published in 2025.
  • Each release is a vintage. The IMF even keeps its old forecast editions as separate datasets.
  • So: always write down when you downloaded your data.

A small function turns one line into a reusable tool

def read_fred(code):
    """Download one FRED series as a table with the columns date and <code>."""
    url = "https://fred.stlouisfed.org/graph/fredgraph.csv?id=" + code
    table = pd.read_csv(url, parse_dates=["observation_date"])
    return table.rename(columns={"observation_date": "date"})

The CPI is an index (1982–84 = 100), a price level: 336.298 in September 2026. It is not inflation. That is the next slide.

Inflation is the 12-month growth rate of the price level

\[\text{inflation}_t = \left(\frac{\text{CPI}_t}{\text{CPI}_{t-12}} - 1\right) \times 100\]

cpi["inflation"] = (cpi["CPIAUCSL"] / cpi["CPIAUCSL"].shift(12) - 1) * 100
  • .shift(12) moves the column down by 12 rows, so each row sees the value of 12 months earlier.
  • The formula on the slide and the line of code are the same thing.

What will the inflation column hold in the first 12 rows of the table?

The predict question: the first 12 rows have no value 12 months earlier, so .shift(12) gives NaN (“not a number”, pandas’ mark for a missing value), and so does the inflation rate. Inflation starts in January 1948 + 12 months.

Why 12 months: with monthly data, comparing with the same month a year earlier removes seasonal patterns and gives the rate that the news reports.

The IMF’s World Economic Outlook comes with forecasts

  • Published twice a year, in April and October.
  • GDP growth, inflation, unemployment and debt for about 190 countries.

FRED is quarterly: average the four quarters of each year

# FRED: quarterly data, so we average the four quarters of each year
fred_pc = read_fred("A939RX0Q048SBEA")
fred_pc["year"] = fred_pc["date"].dt.year
fred_pc = fred_pc[fred_pc["year"] < pd.Timestamp.today().year]     # complete years only
fred_pc = fred_pc.groupby("year")["A939RX0Q048SBEA"].mean().rename("FRED")

Why drop the current year before averaging?

In October 2026 only the first quarters of 2026 exist: their average is not an annual value comparable with the other sources.

.dt.year extracts the year from each date; groupby(“year”).mean() averages the quarters of each year; .rename(“FRED”) names the result after its source, so that it becomes a column name later.

The incomplete year is a frequency pitfall: averaging two quarters of a growing series would bias the 2026 value. The notebook keeps complete years only.

Index each source to 2000 = 100, then compute average growth

\[\text{index}_t = 100 \times \frac{y_t}{y_{2000}} \qquad g = \left(\frac{y_{\text{end}}}{y_{2000}}\right)^{1/(\text{end} - 2000)} - 1\]

END = int(sources.dropna().index.max())       # the last year with all five sources
index = 100 * sources.loc[2000:END] / sources.loc[2000]
growth = 100 * ((sources.loc[END] / sources.loc[2000]) ** (1 / (END - 2000)) - 1)
print(f"Average annual growth of U.S. real GDP per person, 2000-{END}, percent:")
print(growth.round(2).to_string())
  • An index removes the units: every source starts at 100.
  • \(g\) is the constant yearly growth rate that takes \(y_{2000}\) to \(y_{\text{end}}\).

sources.dropna() keeps the years with all five values; .index.max() is the last of them, 2022. Dividing a table by one row (sources.loc[2000]) divides each column by its own 2000 value.

The growth formula is the average annual growth rate of Chapter 3: the constant rate g such that y_2000 × (1 + g)^(end − 2000) = y_end.

Why sources differ: vintages, population and prices

  • Vintages. Each source copied the official U.S. numbers at a different time, and the BEA revises GDP every year. FRED updates within days; the Penn World Table and Maddison every few years.
  • Population. GDP per person divides by population, and the sources use different estimates (Census Bureau, United Nations, their own).
  • Prices and methods. Different base years and, for the Penn World Table and Maddison, their own international prices.

Always say where a number comes from, and compare sources when a result matters.

The three reasons of B.10, which are the three ideas of Act I in action: vintages (Idea 3), units and prices (Idea 2), and the exact series behind a name (Idea 1).

Different sources do not mean one of them is wrong

The sources disagree, so one of them must have made a mistake.

All five can be right about what they measure. They copied the official data at different dates, divided by different population estimates and used different prices. The error would be to report a number without its source.

The misconception of the act. Policy students in particular will meet conflicting numbers in reports and the press.

The practical rule: pick the source that fits the question (official national accounts for recent U.S. growth; the Penn World Table for cross-country comparisons in PPP; Maddison for the very long run), say which one you used, and check whether the conclusion survives another source.

Five habits make a download trustworthy

  • Save what you download, with the date in the file name.
  • Write down the source, the series code and the date next to every result.
  • Check the units and the latest date before you compute anything: .tail() is your friend.
  • Keep keys secret: Colab Secrets, never the notebook.
  • Be polite to the servers: download once and reuse the table.
