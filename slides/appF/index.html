A country that invests twice as much is about three times as rich

×3 What doubling the investment share of GDP goes with, across 98 countries in 1985

The Solow model with a capital share of one third predicts about ×1.4. Today: why the gap, and how human capital closes it.

Open with the number. In MRW’s non-oil sample, the slope of log income on the log investment rate is about 1.6, so twice the investment share goes with about 2^1.6 ≈ 3 times the income. Ask the room: is that big or small? Then reveal what the textbook Solow model predicts: an elasticity of α/(1−α) = 0.5 with α = 1/3, so doubling investment should raise income by only 2^0.5 ≈ 1.41, about 41 percent.

The whole appendix is about that gap: Mankiw, Romer and Weil (1992), “MRW”, showed that adding human capital to the Solow model closes it.

The number comes from the notebook, Fig. 1 (elasticity 1.57 in the non-oil sample).

Three questions for today

  • Does the textbook Solow model fit the differences in income across countries?
  • What changes when schooling counts as a second kind of capital?
  • Do poor countries catch up, and do MRW’s answers survive today’s data?

The three questions are the three acts of the mind map: the textbook model tested; the augmented model; convergence and today’s data.

Students have seen the Solow model in Chapter 5. Here we confront it with data from 98 countries, as MRW did in 1992, and then repeat the test with the Penn World Table 11.0 for 1990–2023.

The Solow model turns saving and population growth into a regression

\[\dot{k} = \bar{s}_K\,k^{\alpha} - (\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\,k \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y^* = \left(\frac{\bar{s}_K}{\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\]

\(k\) and \(y\): capital and output per effective worker, \(K/AL\) and \(Y/AL\)

\[\ln\frac{Y}{L} = a + \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\ln\bar{s}_K - \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\ln(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}) + \varepsilon\]

Two predictions: equal and opposite coefficients, each about 0.5.

The steps, briefly (the full derivation is in Section F.4 of the appendix): capital per effective worker k̂ = K/(AL) grows with investment s_K ŷ and is diluted by depreciation, population growth and technology growth. At the steady state the two forces balance, which gives k̂* and ŷ* = (k̂*)^α. Taking logs and moving from effective workers to workers gives the regression.

The constant a holds ln A(0) and ḡt; the error ε is the country-specific part of A(0): institutions, geography, climate. The key assumption for OLS is that saving and population growth are unrelated to ε. MRW, like the Solow model, treat them as given from outside. Come back to this in the Devil’s-advocate slide.

MRW set ḡ + d̄ = 0.05 in every country.

The estimates imply a capital share of 0.60, not one third

0.60 The capital share \(\alpha\) implied by the restricted regression, non-oil sample (intermediate 0.59)

National accounts pay capital about one third of income (Chapter 4). Saving and population growth matter far more than that share allows.

With the restriction imposed, the coefficient c on ln s_K − ln(n+g+d) equals α/(1−α), so α = c/(1+c). The non-oil estimate c = 1.49 gives α = 0.60; the intermediate sample 0.59; the OECD 0.36, but imprecisely.

This is MRW’s puzzle. Ask: what could make saving matter more than the capital share says? Let students guess: externalities, missing variables, endogeneity. MRW’s answer is a missing kind of capital.

A coefficient that is too large is a clue, not a refutation

The coefficients are far too large, so the Solow model is wrong.

The signs and the fit are right. Too-large effects are what we expect if a second kind of capital, correlated with saving, is missing from the regression.

Omitted-variable bias: if countries that invest in machines also invest in schooling, a regression without schooling credits the machines with the effect of the schools. The fix is not to discard the model but to add the missing variable, which is Act II.

Good question to ask: which way would the bias go if schooling were negatively related to saving? (Downward.)

Adding human capital

Act II

Schooling is a second kind of capital, built by giving up consumption

\[Y = K^{\alpha}H^{\beta}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta}, \qquad \alpha + \beta < 1\]

\[\dot{k} = \bar{s}_K\,y - (\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\,k \qquad \dot{h} = \bar{s}_H\,y - (\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\,h \qquad \text{(per effective worker)}\]

\[\ln\frac{Y}{L} = a + \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\beta}\ln\bar{s}_K + \frac{\beta}{1-\alpha-\beta}\ln\bar{s}_H - \frac{\alpha+\beta}{1-\alpha-\beta}\ln(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}) + \varepsilon\]

Human capital H is the stock of skills of the workforce. Like machines, it is built by investing a share s_H of output (schooling), and it depreciates and is diluted by population growth.

α + β < 1 means diminishing returns to all capital together, so there is still a steady state.

The derivation (Section F.6): at the steady state ĥ/k̂ = s_H/s_K; substitute into the condition for k̂ and solve. The exponents of the steady-state income equation are α/(1−α−β), β/(1−α−β) and −(α+β)/(1−α−β). The three coefficients add up to zero: the restriction we test.

We measure s_H with MRW’s SCHOOL: the share of the working-age population in secondary school. Ask the room what it misses: primary and university education, quality, learning on the job, health.

With human capital, saving and population growth matter more

the investment rate \(\bar{s}_K\) 0.5 1
the investment rate in schooling \(\bar{s}_H\) — 1
\(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}\) −0.5 −2

More saving raises income, which raises schooling, which raises income again.

The mechanism: with a fixed share of output going to schooling, a richer economy also invests more in skills. So a rise in s_K raises income twice: directly through machines, and indirectly through the extra human capital it pays for.

Population growth now dilutes both kinds of capital, so its effect is four times larger.

A second point: if s_H is correlated with s_K and n, the textbook regression credits their coefficients with part of the schooling effect. That is the omitted-variable story of the misconception slide.

Does investing in schools pay off like investing in machines?

A finance ministry can fund more schooling or more public investment. Which raises long-run income more?

What the model predicts

  • With \(\alpha = \beta\), the two elasticities are equal
  • A rise of 0.05 is 25% of \(\bar{s}_K = 0.20\) but 50% of \(\bar{s}_H = 0.10\), so the same spending share does more in schooling
  • Either gain arrives slowly, over decades

What it leaves out

  • School quality: years in school are not skills learned
  • Costs and lags: a pupil enters the labour force years later
  • Whether more schooling causes growth, or growth pays for more schooling

The model gives a clean answer only because it assumes the same share and depreciation for both kinds of capital. The policy debate is about the right-hand column.

Evidence: Hanushek and Woessmann (2012) find that test scores (cognitive skills) are much more strongly related to growth than years of schooling; the causal interpretation is debated. Bring this up as a contested question, not a settled fact.

Ask the MPP students: which column would a ministry of finance care most about, and how would they measure school quality?

Convergence and today’s data

Act III

Human capital halves the speed of convergence

\[\lambda = (\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})(1-\alpha-\beta)\]

\[\ln y(t) - \ln y^* = e^{-\lambda t}\left[\ln y(0) - \ln y^*\right], \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}\]

Textbook (\(\beta = 0\)) Augmented (\(\alpha=\beta=1/3\))
Speed \(\lambda\), with \(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d} = 0.06\) 4% a year 2% a year
Half-life about 17 years about 35 years

The speed of convergence is the share of the remaining log gap to the steady state that closes each year. The derivation (Section F.10) linearizes the two laws of motion around the steady state: the 2×2 system has two speeds, D and D(1−α−β) with D = n+g+d. The fast one closes gaps in the ratio of the two capitals; the slow one governs income.

Note on notation: Appendix E writes this speed as β, following Barro and Sala-i-Martin. Here β is human capital’s share, as in MRW, so the speed is λ.

  • The formula is a local approximation: from a quarter of the steady state the simulated half-lives are 6 and 27 years
  • Faster population growth speeds up convergence: it strengthens the pull back to the steady state

The second bullet is a teaching point about approximations: far below the steady state the returns to capital are very high, and the economy grows faster than the straight line says. The speed λ describes the last part of the journey. The notebook’s check cell prints all four numbers.

Catch-up depends on the poor country’s own steady state

  • Same investment rates: growth of 6.9% a year over the first 25 years, against 2.0%, and the gap closes
  • Half the rates: its steady state is \(0.5^2\), one quarter of the rich country’s; it grows fast at first, then settles below
  • Below about 0.32 of the rich rates, its path lies below where it starts: it grows slower and falls behind

Poverty persists when the steady state is low, not because convergence fails.

Answer the prediction: partly. Its steady state is the share squared times the rich one, because the elasticities to s_K and s_H add up to 2 with α = β = 1/3.

The threshold: with a starting income of 0.1, the poor country grows at the rich rate when its steady state equals its starting income, share² = 0.1, so share ≈ 0.32.

Link to panel A of the previous chart: countries that fell behind between 1960 and 1985 are what this model predicts for low steady states.

Poor countries not catching up does not refute the Solow model

Poor countries did not grow faster than rich ones, so the Solow model’s convergence prediction failed.

The model predicts convergence to each country’s own steady state. Holding saving, schooling and population growth constant, poor countries did grow faster: about 2 percent of the gap closed each year.

This is the most common mistake about convergence. Unconditional convergence needs the same steady state everywhere. In the OECD, where steady states are similar, we do see it.

To lift a poor country’s path, change its steady state

A development bank asks: will poor countries catch up on their own if we just wait?

What the model predicts

  • Only with similar steady states: convergence is conditional
  • Raising \(\bar{s}_K\) or \(\bar{s}_H\), or lowering \(\bar{n}\), raises the path
  • About 2% of the remaining gap closes each year: half-lives of decades

What it leaves out

  • \(A(0)\): technology, institutions, geography, the unexplained fifth
  • Why saving and schooling differ in the first place
  • Ideas and long-run growth (Chapter 6)

The model’s policy message: waiting is not enough when the steady state is low; the levers are investment, schooling and population growth, and their effects take decades.

The right column is where the debate is. Appendix D’s development accounting finds that TFP carries most of today’s income gaps; Klenow and Rodríguez-Clare (1997) make the same point against MRW. Present the size of the TFP share as contested.

Saving and schooling may be high because countries are rich

A skeptic says: “Rich countries save and school more because they are rich, and good institutions raise both income and saving. The regressions measure correlation, not the model’s causal effect.”

Reply: A fair point, and the main caveat. MRW assume saving and population growth are unrelated to \(A(0)\). Panel-data studies that let each country have its own \(A(0)\) (Islam 1995) find faster convergence and a smaller role for capital. The model’s predictions, signs and orders of magnitude still hold.

Use this to build ethos: the key identifying assumption is strong, and we said so on the regression slide.

Two directions of bias: reverse causality (income raises saving and schooling) and omitted variables (institutions raise both). Both push the coefficients up. Bernanke and Gürkaynak (2001) re-run MRW on later data: the levels results hold, but long-run growth rates are correlated with saving, which the exogenous-growth Solow model cannot explain.

What to remember from this appendix

  • The textbook Solow model gets the signs right, but the effects of saving and population growth imply a capital share of about 0.6
  • Counting schooling as capital doubles the effect of saving; MRW find \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) near one third and explain 78% of income differences
  • Convergence is conditional: about 2% of the gap to each country’s own steady state closes each year, half the textbook speed
  • With PWT 11.0 for 1990–2023 the results survive, but the measure of human capital decides the shares

One takeaway per act, plus the data lesson. If time is short, the second bullet is the one to keep.

Walk the three branches in one minute each: the textbook model tested (signs right, sizes wrong); adding human capital (elasticity doubles, shares near one third, slower transitions); convergence and today’s data (2 percent a year, conditional, results survive, measurement matters).
