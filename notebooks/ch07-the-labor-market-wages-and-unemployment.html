    """Precompute make_figure(name=value) for every value and join the results with one slider.

The model in code

The model of this chapter is supply and demand in the labor market. Employment \(L\) goes on the horizontal axis and the wage \(w\) on the vertical axis, so we write each curve as “the wage at each level of employment”:

\[\text{labor demand: } w = \bar{a}_d - \bar{b}_d\,L \qquad\qquad \text{labor supply: } w = \bar{a}_s + \bar{b}_s\,L\]

  • Labor demand slopes down. Firms hire until the extra output of the last worker (the marginal product of labor, Chapter 4) equals the wage, and each extra worker adds less than the one before.
  • Labor supply slopes up. A higher wage makes an hour of leisure more expensive, so more people choose to work.

The textbook draws these curves without numbers. We use simple illustrative values: \(\bar{a}_d = 100\), \(\bar{b}_d = 1\), \(\bar{a}_s = 10\), \(\bar{b}_s = 1\). Setting demand equal to supply gives the equilibrium

\[L^* = \frac{\bar{a}_d - \bar{a}_s}{\bar{b}_d + \bar{b}_s} \qquad\qquad w^* = \bar{a}_d - \bar{b}_d\,L^*\]

so \(L^* = 90/2 = 45\) and \(w^* = 55\).

Later we add an income tax at rate \(\tau\): firms pay \(w\), but workers keep only \((1-\tau)w\). Workers decide how much to work from the wage they keep, so supply becomes \((1-\tau)w = \bar{a}_s + \bar{b}_s L\). Solving with demand gives

\[L^* = \frac{(1-\tau)\,\bar{a}_d - \bar{a}_s}{(1-\tau)\,\bar{b}_d + \bar{b}_s}\]

which is the formula above when \(\tau = 0\). One function, equilibrium, covers both cases.

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. Writing a_d=100.0 gives the argument a default value, used whenever we do not pass one. A function can return two results at once, separated by a comma.

def labor_demand(L, a_d=100.0, b_d=1.0):
    """Figure 7.3: the wage firms are willing to pay when they employ L, w = a_d - b_d L."""
    return a_d - b_d * L


def labor_supply(L, a_s=10.0, b_s=1.0):
    """Figure 7.3: the wage workers need to receive to supply L, w = a_s + b_s L."""
    return a_s + b_s * L


def equilibrium(a_d=100.0, b_d=1.0, a_s=10.0, b_s=1.0, tau=0.0):
    """Figures 7.3-7.4: employment L* and the wage firms pay w*, when workers keep (1 - tau) w."""
    L_star = ((1 - tau) * a_d - a_s) / ((1 - tau) * b_d + b_s)
    w_star = labor_demand(L_star, a_d, b_d)
    return L_star, w_star


def demand_at_wage(w, a_d=100.0, b_d=1.0):
    """Figure 7.6: the employment firms want at a given wage w (labor demand solved for L)."""
    return (a_d - w) / b_d


def supply_at_wage(w, a_s=10.0, b_s=1.0):
    """Figure 7.6: the number of people who want to work at a given wage w (labor supply solved for L)."""
    return (w - a_s) / b_s

Two small drawing helpers save us from repeating the same lines in every figure. add_curve draws one curve; add_point marks an equilibrium with a dot, a label and dotted lines down to the axes.

def add_curve(fig, w, name, color, dash="solid", row=None, col=None):

def shade_recessions(fig, start):
    """Shade every NBER recession (peak to trough) that ends after the date start."""
    for peak, trough in zip(recessions.peak, recessions.trough):
        if trough >= pd.Timestamp(start):
            fig.add_vrect(x0=peak, x1=trough, fillcolor=BLUE, opacity=0.15, line_width=0)

Figure 3: The labor market

Goal. Draw the supply-and-demand diagram of the labor market and see how its equilibrium moves.

Labor demand \(w = \bar{a}_d - \bar{b}_d L\) slopes down and labor supply \(w = \bar{a}_s + \bar{b}_s L\) slopes up. They cross at \((L^*, w^*)\). If the population is a fixed number \(N\), the model also gives the employment-population ratio \(L^*/N\). When you move a slider, the starting curves stay on the chart as dashed gray lines, so you can see the shift.

def labor_market(a_d=100.0, b_d=1.0, a_s=10.0, b_s=1.0):
    """Figure 7.3: labor supply, labor demand and the equilibrium (L*, w*)."""
    L_star, w_star = equilibrium(a_d, b_d, a_s, b_s)
    fig = go.Figure()
    if (a_d, b_d, a_s, b_s) != (100.0, 1.0, 10.0, 1.0):          # keep the starting curves for comparison
        add_curve(fig, labor_supply(L_grid), "Starting curves", GRAY, "dash")
        add_curve(fig, labor_demand(L_grid), "", GRAY, "dash")

    add_curve(fig, labor_demand(L_grid, a_d, b_d), "Labor demand, Lᵈ", BLUE)
    add_point(fig, L_star, w_star, "")

    return fig

The bathtub model of unemployment

The textbook’s Section 7.4 explains the natural rate of unemployment with a simple model. The labor force \(\bar{L}\) is fixed, and each month a share \(\bar{s}\) of the employed lose their job (the job separation rate) while a share \(\bar{f}\) of the unemployed find one (the job finding rate):

\[E_t + U_t = \bar{L} \qquad (7.2) \qquad\qquad \Delta U_{t+1} = \bar{s}E_t - \bar{f}U_t \qquad (7.3)\]

Unemployment is like water in a bathtub: job losses are the faucet and job findings the drain. The level stops changing when the two flows are equal, which gives the steady-state rate

\[u^* = \frac{\bar{s}}{\bar{f} + \bar{s}} \qquad (7.4)\]

Python note. range(61) counts 0, 1, …, 60, one step per month. Each pass of the loop stores the current rate with .append and then applies equations (7.2) and (7.3) once. interact_manual adds a button, so nothing happens until you press it.

def bathtub_path(s=0.01, f=0.20):
    """Section 7.4: simulate the bathtub model for 60 months, starting at 10% unemployment."""
    L_bar, U = 100.0, 10.0                     # labor force and unemployed (only the ratio matters)
    months, rates = [], []
    for t in range(61):
        months.append(t)
        rates.append(100 * U / L_bar)
        E = L_bar - U                          # equation (7.2)
        U = U + s * E - f * U                  # equation (7.3)
    u_star = 100 * s / (f + s)                 # equation (7.4)
    path = pd.DataFrame({"month": months, "rate": rates})
    fig = px.line(path, x="month", y="rate", hover_name="month",
                  labels={"month": "Months from the start", "rate": "Unemployment rate (percent)"},
                  title=f"s = {s:.3f}, f = {f:.2f}: unemployment settles at u* = s/(f+s) = {u_star:.1f}%")
    fig.update_traces(line=dict(color=BLUE, width=3))
    fig.add_hline(y=u_star, line_dash="dash", line_color=GRAY)
    fig.update_yaxes(range=[0, 25])
    return fig

Key numbers: present discounted value

To value a college degree we need a tool from the textbook’s Section 7.6. A payment received \(T\) years from now is worth less than the same payment today, because money today can earn interest at rate \(R\). Its present discounted value is

\[pdv = \frac{\text{future value}}{(1 + R)^T} \qquad (7.7)\]

For a payment that arrives every year for \(n\) years, starting today, we add up the present values of all the payments. The geometric-series formula gives the sum directly:

\[pdv = \text{payment} \times \frac{1 - \left(\frac{1}{1+R}\right)^n}{1 - \frac{1}{1+R}} \qquad (7.14)\]

Python note. The loop in pdv adds one discounted payment per year, so we can check the formula. ** is a power: (1 + R) ** t is \((1+R)^t\). 100_000 is simply 100000; Python ignores the underscore, which only makes large numbers easier to read. :,.0f prints a number with thousands separators and no decimals.

def pdv(payment, R, years):
    """Equation (7.14): present value of `payment` received every year for `years` years, starting today."""
    total = 0.0
    for t in range(years):                       # t = 0 is today, t = 1 next year, ...
        total = total + payment / (1 + R) ** t
    return total

Summary

  • The employment-population ratio rose for decades as women joined the labor force, while men’s ratio fell. Both the ratio and the unemployment rate swing sharply in recessions.
  • In the supply-and-demand model of the labor market, an income tax shifts supply back and lowers employment; a fall in labor demand lowers both the wage and employment. If the wage does not fall, employment falls even more and some people who want a job cannot find one.
  • The bathtub model gives the natural rate of unemployment, \(u^* = \bar{s}/(\bar{f} + \bar{s})\): about 5 percent with U.S. job finding and separation rates. Europe’s higher unemployment since the 1980s reflects adverse shocks combined with labor market institutions.
  • Present discounted values compare payments at different dates: a working life is worth millions of dollars today, and a college degree several hundred thousand.
  • The college premium rose while college graduates became more numerous, so demand for skills must have grown faster than supply. Inequality at the very top has risen sharply in the United States since 1980.
