Learning goals

Two shocks hit the world economy in less than 15 years: the global financial crisis of 2008–09 and the Covid-19 pandemic of 2020. This chapter looks at both in the data. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • tell the story of the financial crisis with data: the housing bubble, the Fed’s interest rate, the spreads between interest rates and the fall of the stock market;
  • compare the Covid-19 recession across countries, in lives lost and in GDP lost;
  • measure how deep the two recessions were (employment, short-run output, unemployment, Table 10.1) and describe the inflation that followed the pandemic;
  • use a balance sheet to compute net worth and leverage, and explain why leverage turns small falls in asset prices into bankruptcies;
  • in Python: find peaks and troughs with idxmax and idxmin, shade recessions with one small function, reshape a table with melt, compare two vintages of the same data, and turn log changes into percent changes.

The model in code

This chapter has no new economic model. It describes two recessions with data, and it adds a few tools from financial economics that later chapters use. We put the tools here once and reuse them.

Measuring a crash. Most figures ask the same question: how far did a series fall from its peak to its trough? The answer is a percent change,

\[\%\Delta = 100 \times \left(\frac{X_{\text{trough}}}{X_{\text{peak}}} - 1\right).\]

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. The text in triple quotes is a short description. For a pandas Series with dates as row labels, series[start:end] keeps the rows between two dates, .idxmax() gives the date of the largest value and .idxmin() the date of the smallest. A function can return several results at once, separated by commas.

def percent_change(new, old):
    """Percent change from old to new: 100 (new / old - 1)."""
    return 100 * (new / old - 1)


def peak_to_trough(series, start, end):
    """The highest point between start and end, the lowest point after it, and the fall in percent."""
    window = series[start:end]
    peak = window.idxmax()                      # date of the highest value
    trough = window[peak:].idxmin()             # date of the lowest value after the peak
    return peak, trough, percent_change(window[trough], window[peak])

def shade_recessions(fig, start="2000-01-01"):
    """Shade every NBER recession that ends after `start` (Figures 2, 3, 7, 8 and 9)."""
    for peak, trough in zip(recessions.peak, recessions.trough):

    return fig

Balance sheets and leverage (Table 10.3)

A balance sheet lists what an institution owns, its assets, and what it owes, its liabilities. The difference is its equity, or net worth: the value that belongs to its owners. Leverage is the ratio of liabilities to net worth:

\[\text{net worth} = \text{assets} - \text{liabilities}, \qquad \text{leverage} = \frac{\text{liabilities}}{\text{net worth}}.\]

The textbook’s hypothetical bank (Table 10.3, billions of dollars) is a small table.

Python note. pd.Series({...}) builds a list of numbers with labels from a dictionary, a set of key: value pairs in curly braces. .sum() adds up all the values.

def net_worth(assets, liabilities):
    """Equity (net worth) = total assets - total liabilities (Table 10.3)."""
    return assets - liabilities


def leverage(liabilities, equity):
    """Leverage ratio = total liabilities / net worth."""
    return liabilities / equity


assets = pd.Series({"Loans": 1000, "Investments": 900, "Cash and reserves": 100})
liabilities = pd.Series({"Deposits": 1000, "Short-term debt": 400, "Long-term debt": 400})
equity = net_worth(assets.sum(), liabilities.sum())
print(f"Total assets {assets.sum():,}, total liabilities {liabilities.sum():,}, net worth {equity:,}")
print(f"Leverage = {liabilities.sum():,} / {equity} = {leverage(liabilities.sum(), equity):.0f}")
print(f"Capital ratio = {100 * equity / assets.sum():.0f}% of assets; reserves = "
      f"{100 * assets['Cash and reserves'] / liabilities['Deposits']:.0f}% of deposits")
Total assets 2,000, total liabilities 1,800, net worth 200
Leverage = 1,800 / 200 = 9

With a leverage of 9, each 10 dollars of assets is financed by 9 dollars of debt and only 1 dollar of the owners’ money. Now let the investments gain or lose value. The liabilities do not change, so all of the gain or loss lands on net worth:

for new_investments in [1000, 600]:
    new_equity = net_worth(assets.sum() - assets["Investments"] + new_investments, liabilities.sum())
    print(f"Investments {assets['Investments']} -> {new_investments}: return "
          f"{percent_change(new_investments, assets['Investments']):+.1f}%, net worth {equity} -> {new_equity}, "
          f"return to the owners {percent_change(new_equity, equity):+.1f}%")
Investments 900 -> 1000: return +11.1%, net worth 200 -> 300, return to the owners +50.0%
Investments 900 -> 600: return -33.3%, net worth 200 -> -100, return to the owners -150.0%

A gain of 11 percent on the investments is a gain of 50 percent for the owners. A loss of a third of the investments, only 15 percent of all assets, turns net worth negative: the bank is insolvent. The same arithmetic works for a house bought for 500,000 dollars with 20 percent down:

house, mortgage = 500_000, 400_000
for price_change in [10, -10, -20]:
    new_equity = net_worth(house * (1 + price_change / 100), mortgage)
    print(f"House price {price_change:+d}%: equity {house - mortgage:,} -> {new_equity:,.0f} "
          f"({percent_change(new_equity, house - mortgage):+.0f}%)")
House price +10%: equity 100,000 -> 150,000 (+50%)
House price -10%: equity 100,000 -> 50,000 (-50%)
House price -20%: equity 100,000 -> 0 (-100%)

How small a fall wipes out the owners? Net worth is the share \(1/(1+\text{leverage})\) of assets, so a fall in asset values of that size leaves nothing. Bear Stearns had a leverage of about 35 when it collapsed in 2008:

for L in [9, 35]:
    print(f"Leverage {L}: assets need to fall by only {100 / (1 + L):.1f}% to wipe out net worth")
Leverage 9: assets need to fall by only 10.0% to wipe out net worth
Leverage 35: assets need to fall by only 2.8% to wipe out net worth

With a leverage of 35, a fall of less than 3 percent in the value of its assets was enough. The rest of the chapter shows how far house and stock prices actually fell.

What to notice.

  • Choose the 1970s and 1980s episodes: those busts were 12.2 and 14.4 percent, less than half of the 2006–2012 bust. That is why the textbook calls the decline after 2006 “by far the largest” since 1953.

Key numbers: the bubble in numbers

The textbook gives three numbers: a decline of 36 percent from the middle of 2006 to the first quarter of 2012, a next-largest decline of 14 percent, and a rise “by a factor of nearly 3 between 1996 and 2006”. house.cummax() is the highest value reached so far, so house / house.cummax() - 1 is the fall from the latest record, called the drawdown.

What to notice.

  • After the 2001 recession, the Fed kept the rate at about 1 percent until mid-2004, then raised it step by step to above 5 percent by mid-2006. Higher rates made mortgages more expensive just as house prices peaked.
  • From August 2007 the Fed cut, and by December 2008 the rate was close to zero. It stayed there for seven years: zero is a floor for interest rates, the zero lower bound of Chapters 11 and 14.
  • In 2020 the rate went back to zero. In 2022 the Fed raised it again, quickly, to fight inflation (Figure 10), to 5.33 percent by August 2023.

Figure 3: Liquidity and risk shocks from interest rate spreads

Goal. See the financial crisis in the price banks paid to borrow from one another.

The TED spread is the 3-month LIBOR rate (what banks charged one another) minus the 3-month Treasury bill rate (what the U.S. government pays). Lending to the government is safe, so the spread is a risk premium: it rises when banks fear that other banks might not repay. LIBOR was discontinued in 2022, so the data file adds a second, similar spread that continues: AA-rated financial commercial paper minus the Treasury bill.

def low_to_high(series, start, end):
    """The highest point between start and end, the lowest point before it, and the rise in points."""
    window = series[start:end]
    high = window.idxmax()
    low = window[:high].idxmin()
    return low, high, window[high] - window[low]

Python note. If \(L = 100 \ln(P_t/P_{t-12})\), then \(P_t/P_{t-12} = e^{L/100}\), and the percent change is \(100\,(e^{L/100} - 1)\). np.exp is the exponential function \(e^x\), and it works on a whole column at once.

def percent_from_log(log_change):
    """Turn a log change 100 ln(P_t / P_t-12) into a percent change 100 (P_t / P_t-12 - 1)."""
    return 100 * (np.exp(log_change / 100) - 1)

The U.S. house and stock prices fell about as much as in the typical financial crisis. Unemployment rose a little less, and real GDP fell much less: by our NBER-quarter measure 3.8 percent, against 9.3 percent in the average crisis. A deep recession, then, but milder than the crises Reinhart and Rogoff studied.

Summary

  • The global financial crisis began with a housing bubble: real U.S. house prices rose for a decade and then fell by more than a third, the largest decline since 1953.
  • Banks held many of the mortgage-backed securities, with high leverage. Falling asset values threatened their net worth, interest rate spreads exploded in 2007–08, and stock prices lost about half their value.
  • The Great Recession was deep and long: 8.7 million jobs lost, and output below potential for most of the 2010s. The Covid-19 recession was even deeper but very short, with a fast recovery in most countries.
  • Inflation returned in 2021–22, above 8 percent in the U.S. and 10 percent in the euro area. Core inflation shows that food and energy prices drove much of it, above all in Europe.
