21.1 Model overview

For twenty chapters we have followed Islandia, our small imaginary island country. We watched its tools pile up and wear out and new ideas spread, and we followed its jobs, money, booms and slumps, its households, Harbor Boatworks, its Treasury and its trade with Mainland. This overview builds no new model. It lays the book’s models side by side, so that we can look back at Islandia’s whole story before five lessons test it with data.

NoteThe big idea

The question. Looking back at the whole book, what makes an economy like Islandia rich or poor, what makes it grow, and what causes its booms and slumps?

The answer in one sentence. Over decades, real forces (tools, skills and above all ideas) set the level and the growth of income while money sets only prices; over a year or two, sticky inflation lets demand and the central bank move output.

What goes in and what comes out. Each block takes the parameters of its own chapters and gives back its headline results, numbered (1) to (20), which the code and the five lessons reuse.

21.1.1 Long run: the production model

Back to Chapter 4.

Building blocks

Islandia makes one good with capital \(K\), its tools, and labor \(L\). Total factor productivity \(\bar{A}\), how well the islanders combine the two, is a parameter:

\[Y = F(K, L) = \bar{A}K^{1/3}L^{2/3}.\]

Firms such as Harbor Boatworks rent capital at the price \(r\) and hire workers at the wage \(w\) to maximize profit \(\Pi = F(K,L) - rK - wL\). The inputs are given, \(K = \bar{K}\) and \(L = \bar{L}\), so the prices adjust until firms want exactly those amounts.

Key results

Factor prices. Profit peaks where one more unit of an input adds just as much output as it costs (Appendix C.8). That extra output is the marginal product, the derivative of \(F\) with respect to the input. The power rule (Appendix C.7) brings the exponent down and lowers it by one:

\[MPK = \tfrac{1}{3}\bar{A}K^{1/3-1}L^{2/3} = \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{\bar{A}K^{1/3}L^{2/3}}{K} = \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{Y}{K}, \qquad MPL = \tfrac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{Y}{L}.\]

So \(r^* = \frac{1}{3}Y^*/\bar{K}\) and \(w^* = \frac{2}{3}Y^*/\bar{L}\). Multiply each price by its input to get the factor shares: capital receives \(r^*\bar{K}/Y^* = 1/3\) of output and labor receives \(w^*\bar{L}/Y^* = 2/3\). The shares sum to one, so profit is zero: constant returns to scale at work.

Output per person. Divide the production function by \(\bar{L}\), use \(\bar{L}^{2/3}/\bar{L} = \bar{L}^{-1/3}\) (Appendix C.3), and write capital per person as \(\bar{k} \equiv \bar{K}/\bar{L}\). The result is equation (1) in the box below.

Comparing two economies. Write equation (1) for economy 1 and for economy 2, then divide the first by the second. The result is equation (2).

ImportantKey result: output per person and development accounting

\[y^* = \bar{A}\bar{k}^{1/3}. \tag{1}\]

\[\frac{y_1^*}{y_2^*} = \frac{\bar{A}_1}{\bar{A}_2}\times\left(\frac{\bar{k}_1}{\bar{k}_2}\right)^{1/3}. \tag{2}\]

In equation (2), the second factor is the relative income that capital alone predicts; the first is relative TFP.

Islandia in numbers. Compare young Islandia (economy 1) with today’s (economy 2), as in Check 1 of the code: with half of today’s TFP and \(1/27\) of its tools per person, \(y_1^*/y_2^* = 0.5 \times (1/27)^{1/3} \approx 0.167\), about a sixth.

In the data, economy 2 is the United States (for Islandia, the richer Mainland). We can measure \(y^*\) and \(\bar{k}\), but not \(\bar{A}\), so TFP is found as the part that capital leaves unexplained, \(\bar{A} = y^*/\bar{k}^{1/3}\). This is development accounting, and Lesson 1 applies it to the world.

TipIn words: why tools cannot explain big gaps

Hand an islander from young Islandia today’s tools, and only part of the income gap closes. With the exponent \(1/3\), being ten times richer through tools alone takes \(10^3 = 1{,}000\) times as many tools. These strong diminishing returns mean that large gaps come mostly from know-how, TFP.

Comparative statics. In logs (Appendix C.4), \(\ln y^* = \ln\bar{A} + \frac{1}{3}\ln\bar{k}\). One percent more TFP gives 1 percent more output per person; one percent more capital per person gives only a third of a percent more. The wage \(w^* = \frac{2}{3}y^*\) moves in proportion to output per person.

Tools raise income, but with strong diminishing returns, so large income gaps point mostly to gaps in productivity.

NoteCheck your understanding

1. Young Islandia had the same TFP as today but only one eighth of today’s tools per person. How many times richer is Islandia today?

Answer

By equation (2) with equal TFP, \(y_{\text{today}}^*/y_{\text{young}}^* = 8^{1/3} = 2\). Eight times the tools buy only twice the income.

2. Why is TFP measured as a residual?

Answer

We observe income and tools per person, but not know-how. Solving equation (1) for it, \(\bar{A} = y^*/\bar{k}^{1/3}\), assigns to TFP whatever tools leave unexplained.

21.1.2 Long run: the Solow model

Back to Chapter 5.

Building blocks

Now Islandia builds its own tools: capital is endogenous. Five equations hold in every year \(t\):

Equation Meaning
\(Y_t = \bar{A}K_t^{1/3}L_t^{2/3}\) production
\(C_t + I_t = Y_t\) output is eaten or invested
\(\Delta K_{t+1} = I_t - \bar{d}K_t\) tools grow by investment minus the tools that break
\(L_t = \bar{L}\) a constant labor force
\(I_t = \bar{s}Y_t\) a constant share \(\bar{s}\) of output is invested

Key results

One equation for capital. Substitute \(I_t = \bar{s}Y_t\) into the accumulation equation and divide every term by \(\bar{L}\). With \(k_t \equiv K_t/\bar{L}\) and \(y_t = \bar{A}k_t^{1/3}\), as in equation (1):

\[\Delta k_{t+1} = \bar{s}\bar{A}k_t^{1/3} - \bar{d}k_t.\]

The terms are new tools and broken tools per person.

The steady state. Capital stops changing when the two terms are equal. Divide \(\bar{s}\bar{A}(k^*)^{1/3} = \bar{d}k^*\) by \(\bar{d}(k^*)^{1/3}\) to get \((k^*)^{2/3} = \bar{s}\bar{A}/\bar{d}\), then raise both sides to the power \(3/2\): \(k^* = (\bar{s}\bar{A}/\bar{d})^{3/2}\). Putting \(k^*\) into the production function multiplies the exponents, \(\frac{3}{2}\times\frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{2}\), so \(y^* = \bar{A}(\bar{s}\bar{A}/\bar{d})^{1/2}\). Collecting the powers of \(\bar{A}\) gives equation (3) below.

Transition dynamics. Divide the capital equation by \(k_t\) to get the growth rate of capital per person, \(g_{k,t} = \bar{s}\bar{A}k_t^{-2/3} - \bar{d}\). The steady state says \(\bar{s}\bar{A} = \bar{d}(k^*)^{2/3}\). Substituting it leaves equation (4), which depends only on how far the economy is from its steady state.

ImportantKey result: the Solow steady state and transition dynamics

\[y^* = \left(\frac{\bar{s}}{\bar{d}}\right)^{1/2}\bar{A}^{3/2}. \tag{3}\]

\[g_{k,t} = \bar{d}\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k_t}\right)^{2/3} - 1\right]. \tag{4}\]

Islandia in numbers. With \(\bar{s} = 0.25\), \(\bar{d} = 0.05\) and \(\bar{A} = 1\) (Check 2 of the code), \(\bar{s}/\bar{d} = 5\), so \(y^* = 5^{1/2} \approx 2.24\). With half its steady-state tools, Islandia adds \(0.05\times(2^{2/3}-1) \approx 2.9\%\) to its capital per person in a year.

By the power rule for growth rates (Appendix C.5), output per person grows about a third as fast, \(g_{y,t} \approx \frac{1}{3}g_{k,t}\). An economy below its steady state grows, faster the larger the gap; an economy above it shrinks. This is the simplest form of the principle of transition dynamics behind Lesson 2.

TipIn words: the race between new and broken tools

With few tools, each new tool adds a lot of output, so new tools outnumber broken ones. As tools pile up, each adds less, while breakage keeps pace. The race ends in a tie at the steady state. After the storm destroyed part of Islandia’s tools, the island rebuilt fast at first, then more slowly.

Comparative statics. In logs, \(\ln y^* = \frac{1}{2}\ln\bar{s} - \frac{1}{2}\ln\bar{d} + \frac{3}{2}\ln\bar{A}\):

A 1% rise in … changes \(y^*\) by why
investment rate \(\bar{s}\) \(+0.5\%\) more capital, but with diminishing returns
depreciation rate \(\bar{d}\) \(-0.5\%\) more of each year’s investment only replaces worn-out capital
TFP \(\bar{A}\) \(+1.5\%\) a direct effect of 1, plus 1/2 through the extra capital it pays for

No growth in the long run. At the steady state \(k^*\) and \(y^*\) are constant. Each extra tool adds less output than the one before but breaks just as often, so building tools alone cannot keep income per person rising. With population growth at rate \(\bar{n}\), every step goes through with \(\bar{n} + \bar{d}\) in place of \(\bar{d}\).

Building tools raises income only until new tools just replace broken ones; after that, tools alone bring no growth.

NoteCheck your understanding

1. Islandia doubles its investment rate from 0.25 to 0.5, with \(\bar{d} = 0.05\) and \(\bar{A} = 1\). What is the new \(y^*\), and by what percentage does it rise?

Answer

Now \(\bar{s}/\bar{d} = 10\), so equation (3) gives \(y^* = 10^{1/2} \approx 3.16\), up from about 2.24. That is a rise of about 41 percent: doubling the investment rate multiplies income by only \(2^{1/2}\).

2. The storm destroyed part of Islandia’s tools. Does its long-run income change? Why does its growth slow down over the following years?

Answer

No: the starting stock of tools is not in equation (3). By equation (4), growth fades as \(k_t\) approaches \(k^*\) and the ratio \(k^*/k_t\) falls toward one.

21.1.3 Long run: ideas, the Romer model and the combined model

Back to Chapter 6.

Building blocks

The Romer model explains where TFP comes from. Islandia’s population \(N_t\) splits between making goods (\(L_{yt}\)) and doing research (\(L_{at}\)), and \(A_t\) is the stock of ideas:

Equation Meaning
\(Y_t = A_t^{\gamma}L_{yt}\) goods are made with ideas and workers
\(\Delta A_{t+1} = \bar{z}L_{at}\) each researcher adds \(\bar{z}\) new ideas a year
\(L_{yt} + L_{at} = N_t\) everyone works in one of the two sectors
\(\Delta N_{t+1}/N_t = \bar{n}\) the population grows at rate \(\bar{n}\)
\(L_{at} = \bar{\ell}N_t\) a constant share \(\bar{\ell}\) of people do research

The parameter \(\gamma > 0\) measures how strongly ideas raise output. Ideas are nonrival: every worker can use the whole stock \(A_t\) at once, so doubling ideas and workers multiplies output by \(2^{1+\gamma}\): increasing returns.

Key results

Income per person depends on the total stock of ideas. With \(L_{yt} = (1-\bar{\ell})N_t\), divide output by \(N_t\); this gives equation (5) in the box below.

Ideas grow as fast as the population. Divide the idea equation by \(A_t\): \(g_{At} = \bar{z}L_{at}/A_t\). This growth rate stays constant only if the numerator and the denominator grow at the same rate (the ratio rule of Appendix C.5). Researchers \(L_{at} = \bar{\ell}N_t\) grow at \(\bar{n}\), so in the long run \(g_A^* = \bar{n}\). The economy is pulled there: if ideas grew faster than \(\bar{n}\), the ratio \(L_{at}/A_t\) would fall and drag \(g_{At}\) down, and the reverse if they grew more slowly. Solving \(\bar{n} = \bar{z}\bar{\ell}N_t/A_t\) for the stock gives \(A_t^* = \bar{z}\bar{\ell}N_t/\bar{n}\): more researchers support a larger stock of knowledge.

Long-run growth. Put \(A_t^*\) into equation (5): \(y_t^* = (\bar{z}\bar{\ell}N_t/\bar{n})^{\gamma}(1-\bar{\ell})\). Only \(N_t\) changes over time, and the growth rate of a power is the power times the growth rate. This gives equation (6).

ImportantKey result: the Romer model

\[y_t \equiv \frac{Y_t}{N_t} = A_t^{\gamma}(1-\bar{\ell}). \tag{5}\]

\[g_y^* = \gamma\bar{n}. \tag{6}\]

Islandia in numbers. With \(\gamma = 0.5\) and \(\bar{n} = 0.02\) (Check 3 of the code), \(g_y^* = 0.5 \times 0.02 = 0.01\), or 1 percent a year.

TipIn words: ideas are shared by everyone at once

Islandia’s researchers keep adding blueprints to a library that never wears out, and every islander can use the whole library at once. A growing population means more researchers, so new blueprints, and income per person, keep coming.

Comparative statics. A higher research productivity \(\bar{z}\) or research share \(\bar{\ell}\) lifts the level of income per person on the long-run path, but not its long-run growth rate, which depends only on \(\gamma\) and \(\bar{n}\). A higher \(\bar{\ell}\) also costs something today: fewer people make goods, the factor \(1-\bar{\ell}\). (In Romer’s original version, \(\Delta A_{t+1}/A_t = \bar{z}L_{at}\), the same changes raise the growth rate itself.)

The combined Solow–Romer model. Now let ideas and tools work together: \(Y_t = A_t^{\gamma}K_t^{1/3}L_{yt}^{2/3}\), with \(\Delta K_{t+1} = \bar{s}Y_t - \bar{d}K_t\) and ideas, people and researchers as above. Per person, \(y_t = A_t^{\gamma}k_t^{1/3}(1-\bar{\ell})^{2/3}\), and the growth rules for products and powers give

\[g_{yt} = \gamma g_{At} + \tfrac{1}{3}g_{kt}.\]

On a balanced growth path the capital-output ratio is constant, so capital and output grow at the same rate, \(g_k^* = g_y^*\). Substituting this and \(g_A^* = \bar{n}\) gives \(g_y^* = \gamma\bar{n} + \frac{1}{3}g_y^*\), so \(\frac{2}{3}g_y^* = \gamma\bar{n}\): equation (7) below. Growth beats the Romer model alone because part of the output that ideas add is invested in tools.

The level of the path. Divide the capital equation by \(K_t\): \(g_{Kt} = \bar{s}Y_t/K_t - \bar{d}\). On the balanced growth path \(g_K^* = g_Y^*\), so \(K_t^*/Y_t^* = \bar{s}/(g_Y^* + \bar{d})\), where \(g_Y^* = g_y^* + \bar{n}\) is the growth rate of total output. Write the production function as \(Y_t = A_t^{\gamma}(K_t/Y_t)^{1/3}Y_t^{1/3}L_{yt}^{2/3}\), move \(Y_t^{1/3}\) to the left to get \(Y_t^{2/3}\), and raise both sides to the power \(3/2\): \(Y_t = A_t^{3\gamma/2}(K_t/Y_t)^{1/2}L_{yt}\). Dividing by \(N_t\) gives equation (8). Investment, depreciation and the research share set the level of the path, as in the Solow model; ideas set its slope. (With \(\gamma = 1\) the exponent on \(A_t^*\) is \(3/2\), as in equation (3).)

Transition dynamics in the combined model. Subtract the balanced-growth version of the growth equation, \(g_y^* = \gamma\bar{n} + \frac{1}{3}g_y^*\), from the general one, \(g_{yt} = \gamma\bar{n} + \frac{1}{3}g_{kt}\). This gives equation (9), where the growth of capital per person comes from dividing the capital equation by \(K_t\) and subtracting \(\bar{n}\).

ImportantKey result: the combined Solow–Romer model

\[g_y^* = \tfrac{3}{2}\gamma\bar{n}. \tag{7}\]

\[y_t^* = \left(\frac{\bar{s}}{g_Y^*+\bar{d}}\right)^{1/2}A_t^{*\,3\gamma/2}(1-\bar{\ell}). \tag{8}\]

\[g_{yt} - g_y^* = \tfrac{1}{3}\left(g_{kt} - g_y^*\right), \qquad g_{kt} = \bar{s}\frac{Y_t}{K_t} - \bar{d} - \bar{n}. \tag{9}\]

Islandia in numbers. Add \(\bar{s} = 0.2\) and \(\bar{d} = 0.05\) (Check 4 of the code). Then \(g_y^* = 1.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.02 = 0.015\), or 1.5 percent a year, total output grows at \(0.015 + 0.02 = 0.035\), and the path’s capital-output ratio is \(0.2/(0.035 + 0.05) \approx 2.35\).

With less capital per unit of output than on the path, \(Y_t/K_t\) is high, so capital and income per person grow faster than \(g_y^*\) until the gap closes. Countries below their own path grow fast, those above it slowly: the version of transition dynamics that Lesson 2 checks with Japan’s catch-up.

Ideas, which everyone can use at once, are the source of lasting growth; tools amplify that growth but cannot sustain it alone.

NoteCheck your understanding

1. In the combined model with \(\gamma = 0.5\), Islandia’s population growth rises from 2 to 3 percent a year. What is the new long-run growth rate of income per person?

Answer

By equation (7), \(g_y^* = 1.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.03 = 0.0225\), or 2.25 percent a year, up from 1.5 percent. More people means more researchers and so more new ideas.

2. Islandia moves more people into research, a higher \(\bar{\ell}\). Does its long-run growth rate change?

Answer

No: by equation (7) it depends only on \(\gamma\) and \(\bar{n}\). More ideas raise the level of the path, but fewer islanders make goods, the factor \(1-\bar{\ell}\) in equation (8).

21.1.4 Long run: unemployment, money and inflation

Back to Chapter 7 and Chapter 8.

Building blocks

Labor market flows. Islandia’s labor force \(\bar{L}\) is split into employed and unemployed islanders, \(E_t + U_t = \bar{L}\). Each year a share \(\bar{s}\) of the employed lose their jobs (here \(\bar{s}\) is the job-losing rate, not the investment rate) and a share \(\bar{f}\) of the unemployed find one:

\[\Delta U_{t+1} = \bar{s}E_t - \bar{f}U_t.\]

The quantity theory of money. Money times its velocity, the number of times each unit of money is spent in a year, equals nominal GDP: \(M_tV_t = P_tY_t\). Velocity is constant, \(V_t = \bar{V}\); real GDP \(\bar{Y}_t\) is set by the real side of the economy (the models above); and the central bank, the Bank of Islandia, chooses the money supply \(\bar{M}_t\).

Interest rates and the government’s sources of funds. The nominal interest rate \(i\) is the real rate \(R\) plus inflation \(\pi\). The government pays for its spending \(G\) with taxes, new borrowing or new money, \(G = T + \Delta B + \Delta M\).

Key results

The natural rate of unemployment. Unemployment is steady when as many people find jobs as lose them, \(\bar{s}E^* = \bar{f}U^*\). Replace \(E^*\) with \(\bar{L} - U^*\), multiply out and collect the \(U^*\) terms: \(\bar{s}\bar{L} = (\bar{s}+\bar{f})U^*\). Dividing by \(\bar{L}\) gives equation (10) below.

The price level and inflation. Solving the quantity equation for prices gives \(P_t^* = \bar{M}_t\bar{V}/\bar{Y}_t\). The growth rate of a product is the sum of the growth rates (Appendix C.5), so \(\bar{g}_M + \bar{g}_V = g_P + \bar{g}_Y\). Velocity is constant, \(\bar{g}_V = 0\), and inflation is the growth rate of the price level, \(\pi = g_P\). This gives equation (11).

The Fisher equation. Writing the link between the two interest rates as an equation gives (12).

ImportantKey result: the natural rate, the quantity theory and the Fisher equation

\[u^* \equiv \frac{U^*}{\bar{L}} = \frac{\bar{s}}{\bar{s}+\bar{f}}. \tag{10}\]

\[\pi^* = \bar{g}_M - \bar{g}_Y. \tag{11}\]

\[i = R + \pi. \tag{12}\]

Islandia in numbers. With job-losing and job-finding rates of 0.02 and 0.3 (Check 5 of the code), \(u^* = 0.02/0.32 = 6.25\) percent. With money growing 7 percent a year and real GDP 3 percent, inflation is 4 percent.

Unemployment is high where jobs end often (high \(\bar{s}\)) or take long to find (low \(\bar{f}\)). Institutions such as firing costs and unemployment insurance work through these two rates, not through money.

One more point of money growth means one more point of inflation; one more point of real growth means one point less. Real GDP was settled before money entered the story, so money changes prices but not real output: the classical dichotomy.

Lenders want compensation for the purchasing power inflation takes away. In the long run \(R\) is set by the marginal product of capital, so inflation passes one for one into nominal rates.

Where high inflation comes from. Rearranged, the sources of funds give \(\Delta M = G - T - \Delta B\): a government with a large deficit that cannot borrow prints money, and equation (11) turns it into inflation.

TipIn words: real forces and the price tag

Each year some islanders lose jobs at Harbor Boatworks and elsewhere, and some are hired. Unemployment settles where the two flows match, whatever money does. Money sets only the price tag: if the Bank of Islandia adds money faster than the island adds goods, prices rise.

In the long run, real forces set jobs and output, while money sets only prices and the inflation rate.

NoteCheck your understanding

1. Jobs on Islandia become harder to find: the job-finding rate falls from 0.3 to 0.18, with a job-losing rate of 0.02. What is the new natural rate of unemployment?

Answer

By equation (10), \(u^* = 0.02/(0.02 + 0.18) = 0.1\), or 10 percent, up from 6.25 percent.

2. The Bank of Islandia raises money growth from 7 to 9 percent a year, and real growth stays at 3 percent. What happens in the long run to inflation and to the nominal interest rate?

Answer

By equation (11), inflation rises to 6 percent. The real rate does not change (the classical dichotomy), so by equation (12) the nominal rate rises by two points too.

21.1.5 Short run: the IS curve, the MP curve and the Phillips curve

Back to Chapter 9, Chapter 11 and Chapter 12.

Building blocks

Short-run output. Potential output \(\bar{Y}_t\) is the long-run path of the models above, and short-run output is the percent gap \(\tilde{Y}_t \equiv (Y_t - \bar{Y}_t)/\bar{Y}_t\). Okun’s law links it to unemployment: \(u_t - \bar{u} = -\frac{1}{2}\tilde{Y}_t\).

Demand. Output is spent on \(Y_t = C_t + I_t + G_t + EX_t - IM_t\). Consumption, government purchases, exports and imports are constant shares of potential output, \(C_t = \bar{a}_c\bar{Y}_t\), \(G_t = \bar{a}_g\bar{Y}_t\), \(EX_t = \bar{a}_{ex}\bar{Y}_t\) and \(IM_t = \bar{a}_{im}\bar{Y}_t\). Investment falls when the real interest rate \(R_t\) rises above the marginal product of capital \(\bar{r}\): \(I_t/\bar{Y}_t = \bar{a}_i - \bar{b}(R_t - \bar{r})\).

Monetary policy (the MP curve). The central bank sets the nominal interest rate \(i_t\). Inflation is sticky, so it barely moves in the short run, and by the Fisher equation (12) the real rate \(R_t = i_t - \pi_t\) moves with \(i_t\): in effect, the central bank chooses \(R_t\).

Price setting (the Phillips curve). Inflation equals expected inflation, plus a term for demand, plus a price shock \(\bar{o}\): \(\pi_t = \pi_t^e + \bar{\nu}\tilde{Y}_t + \bar{o}\). Expectations are adaptive: people expect last year’s inflation, \(\pi_t^e = \pi_{t-1}\).

Key results

The IS curve. Divide the spending identity by \(\bar{Y}_t\) and use the shares: \(Y_t/\bar{Y}_t = \bar{a}_c + \bar{a}_i - \bar{b}(R_t - \bar{r}) + \bar{a}_g + \bar{a}_{ex} - \bar{a}_{im}\). Subtract 1 from both sides; the left side becomes \((Y_t - \bar{Y}_t)/\bar{Y}_t = \tilde{Y}_t\), and the result is equation (13) below.

The Phillips curve. Substitute adaptive expectations into the price equation and subtract \(\pi_{t-1}\) from both sides. The result is equation (14).

ImportantKey result: the IS curve and the Phillips curve

\[\tilde{Y}_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}(R_t - \bar{r}), \qquad \bar{a} \equiv \bar{a}_c + \bar{a}_i + \bar{a}_g + \bar{a}_{ex} - \bar{a}_{im} - 1. \tag{13}\]

\[\Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\tilde{Y}_t + \bar{o}. \tag{14}\]

Islandia in numbers. With \(\bar{a} = 0\), \(\bar{b} = 2\) and \(\bar{r} = 2\) percent (Check 6 of the code), a real rate of 5 percent gives \(\tilde{Y}_t = -2 \times 3 = -6\) percent. With \(\bar{\nu} = 0.5\) (Check 7), inflation then falls by \(0.5 \times 6 = 3\) points.

The demand shock \(\bar{a}\) collects everything else that shifts spending; it is zero in the long run, when also \(R_t = \bar{r}\), so that \(\tilde{Y}_t = 0\). If consumption also rises with current output, \(C_t/\bar{Y}_t = \bar{a}_c + \bar{x}\tilde{Y}_t\), the same steps give \(\tilde{Y}_t = \bar{a} + \bar{x}\tilde{Y}_t - \bar{b}(R_t - \bar{r})\), and solving for \(\tilde{Y}_t\) multiplies the right side of equation (13) by the multiplier \(1/(1-\bar{x})\). By equation (14), inflation rises in a boom and falls in a slump.

TipIn words: a rate hike on Islandia

The Bank of Islandia raises its rate. Inflation hardly moves, so the real rate climbs too. Borrowing now costs Harbor Boatworks more than a new boat shed would earn, so it waits. Output falls below potential, and with slack, firms raise prices more slowly.

The chain of monetary policy. Line the three curves up: a higher \(i_t\) raises \(R_t\) (MP curve), which lowers \(\tilde{Y}_t\) (IS curve), which lowers the change in inflation (Phillips curve) and raises unemployment (Okun’s law).

A change of +1 in … moves … by
the real interest rate \(R_t\) (points) short-run output \(\tilde{Y}_t\) \(-\bar{b}\) percent
the demand shock \(\bar{a}\) short-run output \(\tilde{Y}_t\) \(+1\) percent (the IS curve shifts right)
short-run output \(\tilde{Y}_t\) (percent) the change in inflation \(\Delta\pi_t\) \(+\bar{\nu}\) points
short-run output \(\tilde{Y}_t\) (percent) unemployment \(u_t\) \(-\frac{1}{2}\) point
the price shock \(\bar{o}\) the change in inflation \(\Delta\pi_t\) \(+1\) point (the Phillips curve shifts up)

Because inflation is sticky, the central bank moves the real interest rate, and with it spending, output and next year’s inflation.

21.1.6 Short run: stabilization and the AS/AD framework

Back to Chapter 13 and Chapter 14.

Building blocks

Now the central bank follows a monetary policy rule: it raises the real rate above \(\bar{r}\) when inflation is above its target \(\bar{\pi}\),

\[R_t - \bar{r} = \bar{m}(\pi_t - \bar{\pi}), \qquad \bar{m} > 0.\]

The IS curve (13) and the Phillips curve (14) stay as they are. Written as \(\pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + \bar{\nu}\tilde{Y}_t + \bar{o}\), the Phillips curve is the aggregate supply (AS) curve. In a financial crisis firms borrow at the policy rate plus a risk premium, \(R_t = R_t^{ff} + \bar{f}\).

Key results

The AD curve. Replace \(R_t - \bar{r}\) in the IS curve by the policy rule; this gives equation (15) below. The curve slopes down: when inflation is high, the central bank raises the real rate, and spending falls.

The steady state. With no shocks (\(\bar{a} = \bar{o} = 0\)), AD gives \(\tilde{Y}_t = 0\) only at \(\pi_t = \bar{\pi}\), and AS keeps inflation there: output at potential, inflation on target.

The path of inflation. Put the AD curve into the AS curve: \(\pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + \bar{\nu}\bar{a} - \bar{\nu}\bar{b}\bar{m}(\pi_t - \bar{\pi}) + \bar{o}\). Subtract \(\bar{\pi}\) from both sides and move the \(\pi_t\) term to the left: \((1 + \bar{\nu}\bar{b}\bar{m})(\pi_t - \bar{\pi}) = (\pi_{t-1} - \bar{\pi}) + \bar{\nu}\bar{a} + \bar{o}\). Without shocks, dividing by \(1 + \bar{\nu}\bar{b}\bar{m}\) gives equation (16).

ImportantKey result: the AD curve and the path of inflation

\[\tilde{Y}_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{m}(\pi_t - \bar{\pi}). \tag{15}\]

\[\pi_t - \bar{\pi} = \frac{\pi_{t-1} - \bar{\pi}}{1 + \bar{\nu}\bar{b}\bar{m}}. \tag{16}\]

Islandia in numbers. With \(\bar{\nu} = 0.5\), \(\bar{b} = 1\) and \(\bar{m} = 0.5\) (Check 7 of the code), the factor is \(1 + \bar{\nu}\bar{b}\bar{m} = 1.25\). When the Bank of Islandia cuts its target from 6 to 2 percent, inflation falls to \(2 + 4/1.25 = 5.2\) percent in the first year.

By the Fisher equation (12), the rule sets the nominal rate at \(i_t = \bar{r} + \pi_t + \bar{m}(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})\), so each point of inflation raises the nominal rate by \(1 + \bar{m}\) points, more than one for one. Equation (16) is a first-order difference equation (Appendix C.10), and output follows from AD, \(\tilde{Y}_t = -\bar{b}\bar{m}(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})\). A central bank that reacts more strongly (a larger \(\bar{m}\)) closes the gap faster, with a deeper recession at the start.

The cost of a disinflation. Lowering the target \(\bar{\pi}\) leaves last year’s inflation above the new target, so equation (16) brings inflation down through a recession. Adding the AS curve up over all the years of the adjustment, the changes in inflation sum to the total fall, \(\pi_{\text{start}} - \bar{\pi} = -\bar{\nu}\sum_t \tilde{Y}_t\). So the output lost per point of disinflation, the sacrifice ratio, is \(1/\bar{\nu}\) under adaptive expectations, however fast the central bank moves. If instead price setters believe the new target, expected inflation drops straight to it, \(\pi_t^e = \bar{\pi}\), and inflation can fall with \(\tilde{Y}_t = 0\): the gain from credibility in Lesson 5.

Financial crises. Write the borrowing rate gap as \(R_t - \bar{r} = (R_t^{ff} - \bar{r}) + \bar{f}\) and let the central bank set the policy-rate gap by its rule. The AD curve becomes \(\tilde{Y}_t = (\bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{f}) - \bar{b}\bar{m}(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})\): a higher risk premium works like a negative demand shock of size \(\bar{b}\bar{f}\), which the central bank can offset only while it can still cut \(R_t^{ff}\). Leverage makes banks fragile: a fall in asset values of one over the leverage ratio wipes out all equity.

TipIn words: the Bank of Islandia brings inflation down

While inflation is above target, the Bank of Islandia keeps the real rate high, islanders spend less, and the slack pulls inflation a fixed share of the way back each year. If islanders believe a lower target, they cut their price plans at once, and the slump can be much milder.

A rule that leans against inflation brings it back to target, and credibility makes the trip cheaper in lost output.

21.1.7 Behind the curves: consumption and investment

Back to Chapter 16 and Chapter 17.

Building blocks

A household over two periods. An islander earns \(y_{\text{today}}\) and \(y_{\text{future}}\), can save or borrow \(f_{\text{future}}\) at the real rate \(R\), and starts with no assets. Today \(c_{\text{today}} + f_{\text{future}} = y_{\text{today}}\); in the future \(c_{\text{future}} = y_{\text{future}} + (1+R)f_{\text{future}}\). The household chooses consumption to maximize \(U = u(c_{\text{today}}) + \beta u(c_{\text{future}})\), where \(\beta\) discounts the future.

A firm deciding whether to buy capital. One unit of capital costs \(p_k\). Put in the bank, the money earns \(R\,p_k\). Used as capital, it earns its marginal product, loses \(\bar{d}p_k\) to wear and tear, and gains (or loses) \(\Delta p_k\) in price. The firm buys capital until the two returns are equal: \(R\,p_k = MPK - \bar{d}p_k + \Delta p_k\).

Key results

The lifetime budget constraint. From today’s budget, \(f_{\text{future}} = y_{\text{today}} - c_{\text{today}}\). Put it into the future budget, divide by \(1+R\) and move consumption to the left: \(c_{\text{today}} + c_{\text{future}}/(1+R) = y_{\text{today}} + y_{\text{future}}/(1+R) \equiv \bar{X}\). The present value of consumption equals the present value of income, lifetime wealth (Appendix C.12).

The Euler equation. Giving up one unit today costs \(u'(c_{\text{today}})\) in utility. Saved, it becomes \(1+R\) units in the future, each worth \(\beta u'(c_{\text{future}})\) today. At the best plan the two are equal, \(u'(c_{\text{today}}) = \beta(1+R)u'(c_{\text{future}})\). With log utility, \(u'(c) = 1/c\), which gives equation (17) below. Substitute \(c_{\text{future}} = \beta(1+R)c_{\text{today}}\) into the lifetime budget: the \((1+R)\) cancels and \(c_{\text{today}}(1+\beta) = \bar{X}\), so \(c_{\text{today}} = \bar{X}/(1+\beta)\). With \(\beta = 1\) the household consumes half of its lifetime wealth today.

The user cost of capital. Divide the firm’s condition by \(p_k\) and solve for the marginal product. This gives equation (18).

ImportantKey result: the Euler equation and the user cost of capital

\[\frac{c_{\text{future}}}{c_{\text{today}}} = \beta(1+R). \tag{17}\]

\[MPK = \underbrace{R + \bar{d} - \frac{\Delta p_k}{p_k}}_{\text{user cost } uc}. \tag{18}\]

Islandia in numbers. With \(\beta = 0.95\) and \(R = 0.04\) (Check 8 of the code), \(c_{\text{future}}/c_{\text{today}} = 0.95 \times 1.04 = 0.988\). With \(R = 0.02\), \(\bar{d} = 0.08\) and no price change, the user cost is 0.10 and the desired capital-output ratio is \(1/(3 \times 0.10) \approx 3.33\).

Implications. Consumption depends on lifetime wealth (the permanent-income hypothesis), so a temporary change in income moves it little. A tax cut repaid by later taxes leaves \(\bar{X}\) unchanged: Ricardian equivalence. A higher \(R\) makes consumption grow faster.

With \(MPK = \frac{1}{3}Y/K\) from the production model, the desired capital-output ratio is \(K/Y = 1/(3\,uc)\). And from \(\Delta K_{t+1}/K_t = (I_t/Y_t)(Y_t/K_t) - \bar{d}\), the investment rate that keeps capital growing at \(g_K\) is \(I/Y = (g_K + \bar{d})/(3\,uc)\). A higher interest rate raises the user cost and lowers investment: the microfoundation of the IS slope \(\bar{b}\). A corporate tax \(\tau\) divides the user cost by \(1 - \tau\), so taxes on profits discourage investment too.

TipIn words: islanders smooth, Harbor Boatworks compares

An islander with high income now and low income later saves to spread it over both periods. Harbor Boatworks builds another shed only while it earns at least the bank’s return plus its wear. A higher interest rate makes the shed harder to justify.

Households smooth consumption over their lifetimes, and firms invest until capital just covers its user cost; both choices hinge on the interest rate.

21.1.8 The government and the open economy

Back to Chapter 18, Chapter 19 and Chapter 20.

Building blocks

The government budget. Spending, transfers and interest on the debt are paid for with taxes, new debt or new money: \(G_t + Tr_t + iB_t = T_t + \Delta B_{t+1} + \Delta M_{t+1}\). Leaving out money and counting transfers in spending, the debt evolves as \(B_{t+1} = (1+i)B_t + G_t - T_t\).

National income. \(Y = C + I + G + NX\), where \(NX = EX - IM\) is net exports.

Exchange rates. The nominal exchange rate \(E\) is the number of units of foreign currency one dollar buys. If the same good costs \(P\) dollars at home and \(P^w\) abroad, trade makes it cost the same everywhere, \(EP = P^w\) (the law of one price). The real exchange rate is \(EP/P^w\), the price of home goods in terms of foreign goods.

Net exports in the short run. They fall when the home real rate rises above the world rate \(\bar{R}^w\): \(NX_t/\bar{Y}_t = \bar{a}_{nx} - \bar{b}_{nx}(R_t - \bar{R}^w)\).

Key results

The intertemporal budget constraint. Take two periods and require that all debt is repaid at the end, \(B_3 = 0\). Then \(B_2 = (1+i)B_1 + G_1 - T_1\) and \(0 = (1+i)B_2 + G_2 - T_2\). Substitute the first equation into the second, divide by \(1+i\), and move the taxes to the right; the result is equation (19) below.

Trade and saving. National saving is private saving plus government saving, \(S \equiv (Y - T - C) + (T - G) = Y - C - G\). By the national income identity \(Y - C - G = I + NX\), which gives equation (20).

ImportantKey result: the government’s budget and the trade balance

\[G_1 + \frac{G_2}{1+i} + (1+i)B_1 = T_1 + \frac{T_2}{1+i}. \tag{19}\]

\[NX = S - I. \tag{20}\]

Islandia in numbers. With \(Y = 100\), \(C = 65\), \(I = 20\), \(G = 18\) and \(T = 16\) (Check 9 of the code), national saving is \(100 - 65 - 18 = 17\), so \(NX = 17 - 20 = -3\): Islandia borrows 3 from Mainland and runs a trade deficit.

The present value of spending plus the debt already owed must equal the present value of taxes, so a tax cut today means higher taxes later. A country that saves more than it invests lends abroad and runs a trade surplus; one that saves less borrows and runs a deficit.

The debt-GDP ratio. Let nominal GDP grow at rate \(g_Y\) and write \(b_t \equiv B_t/Y_t\). Divide the debt equation by \(Y_{t+1} = (1+g_Y)Y_t\): \(b_{t+1} = \frac{1+i}{1+g_Y}\,b_t + \frac{G_t - T_t}{(1+g_Y)Y_t}\). When \(g_Y > i\) and the deficit is a constant share of GDP, the ratio settles where \(b_{t+1} = b_t\), at \(b^* = \frac{(G-T)/Y}{g_Y - i}\). Economic growth keeps the debt ratio in check; if \(i > g_Y\), a deficit makes the ratio grow without limit.

Trade by comparative advantage raises incomes in both countries even when one is more productive in every good: each specializes where its relative cost is lowest.

Purchasing power parity. Solving the law of one price for the exchange rate, \(\bar{E} = \bar{P}^w/\bar{P}\), and by the ratio rule for growth rates, \(g_E = \pi^w - \pi\). A country with faster inflation than the rest of the world sees its currency lose value at the difference of the two rates.

The open-economy IS curve. Add and subtract \(\bar{r}\) in the net exports equation, \(NX_t/\bar{Y}_t = \bar{a}_{nx} - \bar{b}_{nx}(R_t - \bar{r}) + \bar{b}_{nx}(\bar{R}^w - \bar{r})\), and repeat the steps of the IS curve. Net exports enter just like investment, so the IS curve keeps the form of equation (13), with slope \(\bar{b} = \bar{b}_i + \bar{b}_{nx}\) and with \(\bar{a}_{nx} + \bar{b}_{nx}(\bar{R}^w - \bar{r})\) inside \(\bar{a}\). A higher home interest rate lowers investment and, by drawing in foreign funds that raise the currency, net exports too. A change in world interest rates is a demand shock at home. And a country cannot have a fixed exchange rate, free capital flows and its own monetary policy all at once: the policy trilemma.

TipIn words: Islandia’s bills and its trade with Mainland

The Islandia Treasury can put taxes off but not escape them: every unit borrowed returns with interest. When Islandia invests more than it saves, the extra comes from Mainland, and Islandia buys more from Mainland than it sells.

Debt must be repaid with future taxes, and a country’s trade balance mirrors the gap between its saving and its investment.

# The text in triple quotes describes the function.
def production(A, K, L):
    """Output of the production model.

    Equation (1) for the whole economy:  Y = A K^(1/3) L^(2/3)

    Arguments
        A : total factor productivity, A-bar
        K : capital
        L : labor
    Returns
        output, Y
    """
    # `return` gives back the result; `**` means "to the power of"
    return A * K ** (1 / 3) * L ** (2 / 3)

# @title Drawing code: Fig. 1, a color for every economy
def group_colors(names, groups, fixed=None):
    """Give each country a color that no other country in its groups uses.

    As far as the palette allows: when every color is taken, the country gets the one
    that its groups use least.

    Arguments
        names  : the countries to color, one at a time
        groups : the lists of countries shown together (the website's menu views)
        fixed  : {country: color} for the book's countries, or None
    Returns
        a dictionary {country: color}
    """
    # start from a copy of the fixed colors (or an empty dictionary)
    colors = dict(fixed or {})
    for name in names:
        if name in colors:
            continue                    # `continue` skips to the next country
        # Step 1: the list `used` collects the colors of the countries shown with it
        used = []
        for group in groups:
            for other in group:
                if name in group and other in colors:
                    used.append(colors[other])
        # Step 2: the palette colors not in `used`; best of all, one no country has yet
        free, unused = [], []
        for c in PALETTE:
            if c not in used:
                free.append(c)
                if c not in colors.values():
                    unused.append(c)
        # Step 3: no free color: the one `used` counts least often (`.count`),
        # searching from the end of the palette (`reversed`)
        least = PALETTE[-1]
        for c in reversed(PALETTE):
            if used.count(c) < used.count(least):
                least = c
        # the first of: an unused color, a free one, the least used one
        colors[name] = (unused + free + [least])[0]
    return colors

21.5 Lesson 3: In the long run, money drives inflation and real forces drive output

Goal. Recall the classical dichotomy and test the quantity theory across 165 economies.

In the long run, the real side of the economy (output, employment, wages) is set by real forces: the inputs and ideas of Lesson 1. The nominal side (the price level and inflation) is set by money. With a stable velocity of money, the quantity theory says that inflation equals money growth minus real GDP growth (equation 11),

\[\pi = g_M - g_Y.\]

This split between the real and the nominal sides is the classical dichotomy. It means that, in the long run, there is no trade-off between inflation and real GDP. You tested it in Chapter 8, Fig. 3 (U.S. decades) and Chapter 8, Fig. 4 (countries). Behind fast money growth there is usually a government that cannot borrow and prints money to pay its bills. Long-run unemployment, by contrast, depends on labor-market institutions (through the job-losing and job-finding rates of equation 10) such as hiring and firing costs and unemployment insurance, as in Chapter 7, Fig. 7.

21.6 Lesson 4: In the short run, there is a trade-off between inflation and output

Goal. Recall the Phillips curve and estimate its slope from U.S. data.

In the short run, inflation is sticky: firms set prices partly by looking at past inflation, so inflation changes only gradually. What moves it is the state of the economy. The Phillips curve of Chapter 12 says (equation 14 in the Model overview above)

\[\Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\,\tilde{Y}_t + \bar{o},\]

where \(\tilde{Y}_t\) is short-run output, the percent gap between actual and potential GDP, and \(\bar{o}\) is a price shock. A boom raises inflation and a slump lowers it. Sticky inflation also breaks the classical dichotomy in the short run: when the central bank raises the nominal interest rate, the real interest rate rises too (equation 12, with inflation stuck), which lowers investment and output (equation 13). In an open economy the higher interest rate also raises the exchange rate, which lowers net exports, as in Chapter 20, Fig. 4. See also Chapter 9, Fig. 7, Chapter 12, Fig. 4, Chapter 12, Fig. 7 and the inflation-output loops of Chapter 13, Fig. 17.

We compute the change in inflation each year and fit a straight line through it against short-run output: its slope is \(\bar{\nu}\).

21.7 Lesson 5: A credible central bank stabilizes output as well as inflation

Goal. Measure what the Volcker disinflation cost in lost output, and see why credibility lowers that cost.

The Phillips curve gives a central bank one sure way to bring inflation down: a recession (equation 16). But if firms and workers believe that the bank will do whatever it takes, they lower their expected inflation on their own, and a smaller recession, or none, does the job. That is why credibility, transparency and explicit inflation targets matter so much. See the disinflation in Chapter 12, Fig. 12, the costless disinflation of Chapter 13, Fig. 20, and the low, stable inflation of the inflation-targeting era in Chapter 13, Fig. 19.

One way to measure the cost of a disinflation is the sacrifice ratio: the output lost, added up over the years of the recession (the sum of the negative values of \(\tilde{Y}\)), divided by the fall in inflation.

# The sacrifice ratio of the Volcker disinflation, from the same U.S. file.
# The year is the row label, so that `annual.loc[1980:1985]` picks years
annual = pd.read_csv(DATA_URL + "fig13-17.csv").set_index("year")


def sacrifice_ratio(start, end):
    """The sacrifice ratio of a disinflation between two years (Chapter 13).

    Output lost (the sum of the negative values of Y-tilde) per point of lower
    inflation.

    Arguments
        start : the first year
        end   : the last year
    Returns
        lost, fall, ratio : the output lost (percent of a year's potential GDP),
        the fall in inflation (points) and lost / fall
    """
    # Step 1: `.clip(upper=0)` replaces every positive number by 0, so that only
    # the years below potential count as losses
    lost = -annual.loc[start:end, "output_gap"].clip(upper=0).sum()
    # Step 2: the fall in inflation from the first to the last year
    fall = annual.loc[start, "inflation"] - annual.loc[end, "inflation"]
    # a function can `return` several values at once, separated by commas
    return lost, fall, lost / fall

21.8 Open questions at the frontier

Goal. Name the big questions macroeconomics has not yet answered, and measure one of them.

The textbook closes with problems that are still open. In short:

  • Why are investment and TFP so low in poor countries? Lesson 1 showed that TFP explains most of the income gap. Weak property rights and the rule of law are part of the answer, but why are they weak?
  • Which institutions work best? 21.9 Concluding remarks

  • Long run, levels: income per person depends on capital, skills, hours, ideas and TFP, and TFP explains most of the gap between rich and poor countries. Long run, growth: sustained growth comes from new ideas.
  • Transition dynamics: countries far below their steady state grow fast, as Japan did after 1950, and slow down as they catch up.
  • Classical dichotomy: in the long run, money growth drives inflation and real forces drive output, so there is no long-run trade-off between them.
  • Short run: sticky inflation creates a Phillips curve trade-off and lets monetary policy move real output; a credible central bank can lower inflation at a smaller cost.
  • The models in one place: the Model overview collected the book’s key results, from development accounting, equations (1) and (2), the Solow steady state (3) and transition dynamics (4), through growth from ideas (5) to (9), the natural rate of unemployment (10), the quantity theory (11) and the Fisher equation (12), to the short-run model, the IS curve (13), the Phillips curve (14), the AD curve (15) and the path of inflation (16), and the building blocks behind them: the Euler equation (17), the user cost of capital (18), the government’s budget constraint (19) and \(NX = S - I\), equation (20).
