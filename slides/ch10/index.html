A fall of 2.8% in the value of its assets was enough to wipe out Bear Stearns

2.8% The fall in asset values that erases the owners’ stake at a leverage of 35 to 1

Real U.S. house prices fell by more than a third after 2005, yet the mortgage losses were small next to the country’s wealth. So how did a housing bust bring the largest banks to the edge of collapse? And twelve years later, why did a far deeper slump fade so much faster?

Open with the number. It comes from the notebook’s leverage check: with a leverage ratio of 35, the fall in asset values that wipes out net worth is 1/(1 + 35) = 1/36, about 2.8 percent. Bear Stearns had about that leverage when it collapsed in March 2008.

Contrast it with a household that buys a house with 20 percent down: the same formula says a 20 percent fall in the house price wipes out its equity. An investment bank in 2007 had a cushion seven times thinner.

Two shocks, twelve years apart: the 2007–09 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic. Not since the 1930s and the 1918 flu had the world seen shocks this large. The chapter has no new model; it measures the two recessions and adds the finance needed to understand the first one.

Ask the room: where were you, or your family, in 2008 and in April 2020? What changed first: jobs, prices, or the news?

Today: three questions

  • What happened in 2007–09? A housing bubble, the Fed’s interest rate, fear between banks and a stock market crash.
  • Why did a fall in house prices break the banks? Balance sheets, leverage, fire sales, bank runs and interest rate spreads.
  • How did the two recessions compare, and what should policy do? Lives, output, jobs and inflation; then capital, rescues, jobs and lives.

These are the three acts of the deck and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide.

The headline result: leverage turned a fall in house prices into a banking crisis and a long, deep recession. The pandemic hit harder but more briefly, and inflation returned only a year later.

The chapter is a first look. Chapters 11 to 14 build the short-run model (the IS curve, monetary policy, aggregate demand and supply) that explains these booms, slumps and inflation. Here we collect the facts and the finance.

The housing bust

How unusual were the rise and fall of U.S. house prices in the 2000s?

Ask the room first. Most students know “house prices fell in 2008”. Ask: by how much? Was it the biggest fall since the 1950s, or one of several? Collect two or three guesses before moving on.

In real terms, U.S. house prices had their largest fall since 1953

The 2000s bust was more than twice the size of any earlier one

  • From the peak in December 2005 to February 2012, real prices fell 35.7%. The 1970s and 1980s busts were 12.2% and 14.4%.
  • The boom was large, but not as large as often quoted: in this real index, prices rose 1.72 times from 1996 to 2006, about 5.6% a year. The textbook’s “nearly 3 times” must come from another index.
  • On a ratio scale a fall by half and a doubling cover the same distance, so busts of different decades can be compared by eye.

Always ask which series a number comes from: nominal or real, national or the booming cities.

Numbers: the notebook’s Fig. 1 key numbers. Largest drawdown since 1953: −35.7 percent in February 2012; quarterly averages from 2006Q2 to 2012Q1 give −35.1 percent, the textbook’s “36 percent”. Annual averages: 1996 to 2006, prices times 1.72, or 5.6 percent a year by equation (2), g = 1.72^(1/10) − 1.

The textbook’s “factor of nearly 3” and “about 10 percent per year” do not match the real national index. The notebook guesses a nominal index, or a 20-city index with the booming coastal markets. This is a teaching moment: the same story told with two series gives very different numbers.

Beyond the U.S. (fig. 1b in the book chapter): in the BIS data, real house prices fell 56.7 percent in Ireland and 44.5 percent in Spain after 2007, more than in the U.S.; both had banking crises too.

The rule of 70: at 5.6 percent a year, prices double in about 12.5 years.

Cheap money and loose lending fed the boom

  • A saving glut. After their own crises of the 1990s, many emerging economies saved more and lent abroad, largely to the U.S. Cheap funds chased assets.
  • Low interest rates. The Fed kept its rate near 1% until mid-2004 (Fig. 2).
  • Weaker standards. More mortgages went to borrowers with poor credit or high debt, on the belief that prices could only rise.
  • Securitization. Mortgages were pooled and sliced into securities. Pooling spreads the risk of one house, but not the risk that all house prices fall together.

A nationwide fall in prices is an aggregate risk: no amount of pooling makes it go away.

This slide summarizes the causes the textbook lists, in our own words. They are not measured in the notebook, so the slide carries no numbers.

Saving glut: Bernanke’s 2005 Sandridge Lecture, which coined the phrase. Chapter 20 returns to the emerging-market crises of the 1990s.

Lending standards: Mian and Sufi (2009, Quarterly Journal of Economics) show that mortgage credit expanded most in ZIP codes with many subprime borrowers, where incomes were not rising, and that those areas later had the most defaults. Their reading is contested by other work that stresses middle-income borrowers and speculation; the point for us is that credit grew faster than the ability to repay.

Securitization: the originating bank sold the mortgage and no longer bore the loss if it went bad, which weakened the incentive to screen borrowers.

Ask: why would a sophisticated bank buy securities whose value depends on house prices always rising? (One answer: the bank’s managers are paid on profits during the boom, and the bank is highly leveraged; Act II.)

Ask the room first: did the Fed raise or lower rates in the years before 2007? And after the crash, how low did it go? Collect guesses.

The Fed raised its rate before the crash, then cut it to zero for seven years

How to read it: the effective Fed funds rate, the monthly average of what banks paid one another for overnight loans, in percent. Shaded bands are NBER recessions. The buttons zoom in on four periods; the title reports the lowest and highest rate in the window.

Do live:

  1. “2000–2026”: the whole story. The highest rate, 6.54 percent in July 2000; the lowest, 0.05 percent in April 2020.
  2. “2004–06 hikes”: step by step from about 1 percent to above 5 percent.
  3. “2007–08 cuts”: from August 2007 the Fed cut, and by December 2008 the rate was near zero.
  4. “2022–23 hikes”: the fastest rise in the chart, to fight the inflation of Figs. 10 and 11.

Rates rose just as house prices peaked, then hit a floor at zero

  • The effective rate was 1.00% in May 2004 and 5.24% in July 2006. Higher rates made mortgages dearer, above all adjustable ones whose low teaser rates were ending.
  • From January 2009 to November 2015 the rate never rose above 0.22%. It cannot go much below zero: the zero lower bound of Chapters 11 and 14.
  • In 2020 it went back to zero; in 2022–23 the Fed raised it to 5.33% by August 2023.

Once the rate was at zero, the Fed’s usual tool was used up for seven years.

Numbers: the notebook’s Fig. 2 key numbers. The textbook says the Fed raised its rate “from 1.25 to 5.25 percent” between May 2004 and May 2006. Those are the Fed’s targets. The effective rate follows the target with a short delay: 1.00 percent in May 2004, 4.94 percent in May 2006, and 1.26 and 5.24 percent two months later. The latest value, September 2026: 3.75 percent.

Beyond the U.S. (fig. 2b in the chapter): Switzerland and Japan had negative policy rates for years (Switzerland −0.75 percent from 2015), so zero is not quite a floor.

A debate to flag, not settle: some economists argue the Fed kept rates too low in 2002–04 and so fed the bubble; others reply that house prices rose in many countries with different monetary policies. We return to it with the IS-MP model.

Fear between banks

Where in the data can we see that banks stopped trusting one another?

Ask: if you lend to another bank for three months instead of to the U.S. government, what extra interest would you ask for? And what happens to that extra interest if you start to fear that the bank might fail? Let students propose a measure before showing it.

Banks charged one another a fear premium that exploded in 2008

How to read it: two spreads, in percentage points. The TED spread is the 3-month LIBOR rate (what banks charged one another) minus the 3-month Treasury bill rate (what the U.S. government pays). Point at three moments: August 2007, when banks first doubted one another; the autumn of 2008, after Lehman Brothers failed in September; and March 2020, a short spike that the Fed’s lending quickly calmed.

The spread moved through three phases of the crisis

  • Calm, 2002 to July 2007: on average 0.33 points, between 0.16 and 0.75.
  • Doubt, August 2007 to August 2008: on average 1.31 points.
  • Panic: a monthly peak of 3.35 points in October 2008. The textbook’s “more than 4.5” refers to single days; monthly averages smooth the spike.

A jump in a spread is the clearest sign of a financial crisis in the data. Act II explains what it measures.

Numbers: the notebook’s Fig. 3 key numbers. The two spreads correlate 0.97 over 2000–2022, so the commercial-paper spread is a good stand-in after 2022. Since February 2022 its highest value is 0.38 points (June 2022): no stress episode like 2007–08.

Flight to safety: lenders left interbank loans for T-bills, which pushed the T-bill rate down and the interbank rate up. The spread widened from both sides.

Beyond the U.S. (fig. 3b in the chapter): the euro area’s and the U.K.’s interbank spreads also jumped in August 2007 and peaked in October 2008; Canada’s hardly moved.

In two weeks of September 2008 the government took over Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, Lehman failed, Bank of America bought Merrill Lynch, and the Fed lent to AIG. That is the moment of the spike.

A crash on borrowed money is far more dangerous than a crash on savings

  • From October 2007 to March 2009 real stock prices fell 51.7%, below every month of 1997–99. They regained the 2007 peak only in November 2013.
  • The dot-com bust of 2000–03 was almost as deep, 46.8%, but caused no financial crisis.
  • Its losses fell on millions of shareholders who had not borrowed. In 2007–09 the losses fell on a few banks that held mortgage securities with high leverage.

Who bears the losses matters as much as how large they are.

Numbers: the notebook’s Fig. 4 key numbers. With the textbook’s dates, November 2007 to March 2009, the fall is 48.9 percent. The March 2009 level, 1,194, was below the lowest month of 1997–99, 1,600. In March 2009 the index was 58.6 percent below its record of August 2000.

Beyond the U.S. (fig. 4b): the crash was global. Germany −55.4, Japan −57.6, the U.K. −45.5 percent; Iceland, whose banks collapsed, −96 percent. After 2009 the paths split: real U.S. stock prices in 2026 are about 1.5 times their 2007 high, Greece’s about a third of theirs.

This slide is the bridge to Act II: leverage is what turns a loss into a bankruptcy.

Undoing a crash takes a larger rise than the fall

Stocks fell 50%, so a 50% rally brings them back to where they were.

A fall of a fraction \(f\) needs a rise of \(f/(1-f)\) to recover. After a fall of 51.7%, real stock prices had to rise 107%, which took until November 2013. A 10% fall needs 11.1%; a 35.7% fall needs 55.5%.

Equation (3) of the Model overview. The trough is (1 − f) times the peak, so getting back means multiplying by 1/(1 − f). The rise needed, in percent, is 100 × f/(1 − f).

The notebook’s checks: 100 → 90 → 100 needs +11.1 percent; 100 → 64.3 → 100 needs +55.5 percent (the house-price bust); 100 → 48.3 → 100 needs +107.0 percent (the stock crash).

The slope of f/(1 − f) is 1/(1 − f)², above one and rising with f: deep crashes take a long time to undo even with strong growth afterwards.

Log changes add up and percent changes do not: a fall of 50 percent then a rise of 50 percent leaves a series 25 percent below its start, because ln 0.5 + ln 1.5 = ln 0.75. This is why Figs. 1 and 4 use a ratio scale.

Net worth \(E\) 200

\[A = D + E \qquad \ell \equiv \frac{D}{E} = \frac{1{,}800}{200} = 9\]

  • Net worth (equity, capital) is what is left for the owners.
  • Capital ratio \(E/A = 10\%\); reserves are 10% of deposits.

Every 10 dollars of this bank’s assets rest on 1 dollar of its owners’ money.

The hypothetical bank of the textbook, loosely modeled on a large commercial bank. Notation from the Model overview: A for assets (not productivity in this chapter), D for liabilities (debt), E for net worth (equity), ℓ for the leverage ratio.

Net worth is defined as A − D, so the two sides always balance. The bank satisfies an illustrative capital requirement of 6 percent of assets and a reserve requirement of 3 percent of deposits.

Step 1 of the Model overview: dividing A = D + E by E gives A/E = 1 + ℓ, so E/A = 1/(1 + ℓ). Leverage and the capital ratio carry the same information. A capital requirement of 6 percent is a ceiling on leverage: 1 + ℓ ≤ 1/0.06, or ℓ ≤ 15.7.

The key assumption of everything that follows: liabilities are promises to repay fixed amounts; the value of assets moves with house prices, stock prices and defaults.

Balance sheets work the same way for households, firms and governments.

Leverage multiplies the owners’ gains and losses by \(1+\ell\)

\[\frac{\Delta E}{E} = \frac{A}{E}\cdot\frac{\Delta A}{A} = (1+\ell)\,\frac{\Delta A}{A}\]

Debt is fixed, so every dollar gained or lost on the assets lands on the owners: \(\Delta E = \Delta A\).

  • The bank. Securities rise from 900 to 1,000: a return of 11.1% on them, but net worth goes from 200 to 300, +50% for the owners.
  • A home buyer with 20% down has \(1+\ell = 5\). The house price rises 10%: equity goes from $100,000 to $150,000, +50%. It falls 10%: equity halves.

Leverage is a genie in good years and a trap in bad ones: the multiplier works both ways.

Equation (6). Change the value of the assets by ΔA; liabilities do not move; so ΔE = ΔA. Divide by E and multiply and divide by A.

For our bank 1 + ℓ = 10: a 1 percent gain on all its assets, 20 billion, raises its equity by 10 percent (the notebook’s check). When only one item changes, measure the change relative to total assets: 100 billion on the investments is 5 percent of all assets, so 50 percent for the owners.

The household: a 500,000 dollar house bought with 100,000 of the buyer’s money. Notebook: +10 percent → equity 150,000 (+50 percent); −10 percent → 50,000 (−50 percent); −20 percent → 0 (−100 percent).

Intuition: the owners hold a thin layer on top of a large pile of debt. A small change in the size of the pile is a large change in the thickness of the layer.

Ask: why do banks like leverage? (In good years the owners’ return is many times the return on the assets. Before 2007, banks that took leveraged bets that paid off nine years in ten showed long runs of spectacular profits.)

The thinner the equity, the smaller the fall that wipes it out

\[E' = (1-f)A - D = E - fA \le 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f \ge f^* = \frac{E}{A} = \frac{1}{1+\ell}\]

  • Our bank, \(\ell = 9\): \(f^* = 10\%\). Securities fall from 900 to 600: assets fall 15%, net worth goes from 200 to −100.
  • Bear Stearns, \(\ell \approx 35\): \(f^* \approx\) 2.8%.
Higher leverage \(\ell\) means … formula at \(\ell = 9\) at \(\ell = 35\)
a bigger multiplier on returns \(1+\ell\) 10 36
a smaller fall that wipes out equity \(1/(1+\ell)\) 10% 2.8%
more forced sales per $1 of losses \(\ell\) 9 35

A bank whose assets are worth less than its debts is insolvent: selling everything would not repay what it owes.

Equation (7). Let the value of the assets fall by the fraction f while D stays put. Net worth becomes E − fA. It reaches zero when f = E/A = 1/(1 + ℓ).

The textbook’s example: investments fall from 900 to 600 billion, so total assets fall from 2,000 to 1,700, 15 percent, and net worth goes from +200 to −100.

The table is the Model overview’s comparative statics. In logs, ln f* = −ln(1 + ℓ): doubling 1 + ℓ halves the cushion.

Households: with an 80 percent mortgage, ℓ = 4 and f* = 20 percent. A larger fall leaves the house worth less than the mortgage (“under water”). Lost housing wealth is one reason consumption fell in the Great Recession (Act III).

Bear Stearns: roughly 3 dollars of equity and 97 of borrowing per 100 of assets. The third row of the table is the next slide.

Losses force sales, and sales cause new losses

\[\underset{\text{assets left}}{(A-x)} \;-\; \underset{\text{target}}{(1+\ell)(E-x)} \;=\; \ell\,x\]

  • To bring leverage back to \(\ell\) without new equity, a bank that loses \(x\) must sell \(\ell x\) of assets and repay debt.
  • Our bank: a loss of 10 forces sales of 90. At Bear Stearns’ leverage, 35.

Asset prices fall → losses \(x\) → forced sales \(\ell x\) → many banks sell the same assets → asset prices fall again

Through this loop, mortgage losses that were small next to U.S. wealth could shake the whole financial system.

Equation (8). After a loss of x, assets are A − x and equity E − x, so leverage jumps to D/(E − x). To restore the old leverage the assets must shrink to (1 + ℓ)(E − x). Expand and use A = (1 + ℓ)E: the sales needed are ℓx. The notebook checks it: a loss of 10 forces sales of 90 and leaves leverage at 9.0.

This is deleveraging. One bank selling is harmless. Many leveraged banks selling the same kind of assets at the same time push those prices down, which causes new losses everywhere, which equation (8) turns into new sales.

Systemic risk: banks lend to and insure one another every day. Paul O’Neill, a former Treasury secretary, compared it to ten bottles of water with one poisoned: if no one knows which, no one drinks. Banks stopped lending to one another (the spread of Act I) because no one knew which balance sheet held the bad mortgages.

The full derivation is in the book chapter’s Model overview.

A run can sink a bank that was solvent

\[\text{loss} = \frac{\delta\,(W-R)}{1-\delta}\]

  • \(W\): withdrawals; \(R\): cash and reserves; \(\delta\): the fire-sale discount on loans and investments sold in a hurry.
  • Full run on our bank: it fails if \(\delta \ge E/(E+W-R)\) = 18.2%.
Withdrawals \(W\) loss at \(\delta = 25\%\) net worth left
100 0 200
400 100 100
700 200 0
1,000 (all) 300 −100

With a 25% discount, a run on 70% of the deposits is enough to make this bank insolvent.

Equation (9). If withdrawals exceed cash, the bank must sell illiquid assets of book value S with (1 − δ)S = W − R. It gives up S and gets (1 − δ)S, so it loses δS = δ(W − R)/(1 − δ), and that comes out of net worth.

Two ways to solve the failure condition, loss ≥ E. For δ in a full run (W = all deposits, 1,000): δ* = E/(E + W − R) = 200/1,100 = 18.2 percent. For W at a given discount: W* = R + E(1 − δ)/δ; at δ = 25 percent, W* = 100 + 200 × 3 = 700, 70 percent of deposits. The table is the notebook’s run check.

Comparative statics: a run is harder to trigger (W* larger) with more cash R, more equity E or a smaller discount δ. Each policy works on one of them: reserve requirements raise R, capital requirements raise E, a lender of last resort removes the fire sale, and deposit insurance removes the reason to run.

In 2007–08 the runs were not on insured deposits but on banks’ short-term debt, which lenders could simply refuse to renew.

Illiquid is not the same as insolvent

A bank that cannot pay its depositors today must have made bad loans.

At normal prices our bank is solvent: net worth 200. It fails only because a run forces it to sell at a discount. If each depositor expects the others to run, running first is the best move, and the run proves itself right.

This is the logic of Diamond and Dybvig (1983, Journal of Political Economy): a bank turns illiquid loans into liquid deposits, and that service makes it vulnerable to self-fulfilling runs. Without a run the fire-sale discount never matters; with one, it turns a liquidity problem into a solvency problem.

Real cases mix the two. In 2008 many institutions had both: real losses on mortgage securities (solvency) and lenders who stopped renewing short-term funding (liquidity). In a panic it is hard, even for regulators, to tell which bank is which. That difficulty is at the heart of the policy debate in Act III.

Ask: why does deposit insurance stop runs even if it is almost never paid out? (If every depositor knows their money is safe, no one gains by running first, so the bad equilibrium disappears.)

An interest rate spread is a thermometer of fear

Lend $1 to a bank that fails with probability \(p\), or buy a T-bill. A lender who cares about the average payoff is indifferent when

\[(1-p)(1+i) = 1+i^f\]

\[\Rightarrow\quad i - i^f = \frac{(1+i^f)\,p}{1-p} \approx p\]

  • Calm years: a spread of 0.3 points ↔︎ a perceived default risk of about 0.3% a year.
  • October 2008: 3.35 points ↔︎ about 3.2%.
  • Fear of default rose about tenfold.

The spread rises almost one for one with the risk of default as lenders see it.

Equation (10). The approximation holds when p and the T-bill rate are small. The notebook’s check with a T-bill rate of 1 percent: a default probability of 0.3 percent gives a loan rate of 1.30 percent and a spread of 0.30 points; 3.2 percent gives 4.34 and 3.34 points. In both cases the expected payoff equals the T-bill’s, 1.0100.

The slope of the spread with respect to p is (1 + i^f)/(1 − p)², close to 1.

Caveats: if lenders recover part of the loan when a borrower fails, the spread is smaller for the same p; if they dislike risk, it is larger. Banks that worry about their own funding also hoard cash rather than lend it, which adds a liquidity premium. Either way the spread moves with fear.

The research. Should governments insure jobs or insure workers?

In a sudden stop, should the state pay firms to keep workers on short hours, or pay workers who lose their jobs?

Insure jobs (short-time work)

  • Keeps the worker–firm match, so output can restart at once.
  • Unemployment hardly moves.
  • Cheapest when the shock is temporary.

Insure workers (unemployment insurance)

  • Lets workers move to firms and sectors that grow.
  • Short-time work can prop up jobs that would vanish anyway.
  • Its money can go to firms that would have kept the workers.

The data are the book’s: employment fell 1.7 to 2.4 percent in Germany, Japan and the U.K., 13.3 percent in the U.S.; U.S. payroll jobs were back at their peak after 28 months.

The research. Giupponi, Landais and Lapeyre (2022, Journal of Economic Perspectives, “Should We Insure Workers or Jobs during Recessions?”) compare the two tools and argue that short-time work is most useful for large, temporary shocks, while unemployment insurance is better when reallocation is needed. Giupponi and Landais (2023, Review of Economic Studies) find that Italy’s short-time work saved jobs in the Great Recession but slowed the move of workers to more productive firms. Cahuc, Kramarz and Nevoux (2018, IZA working paper) find that in France it saved jobs in hard-hit firms but paid windfalls to others. On the U.S. side, Ganong, Noel and Vavra (2020, Journal of Public Economics) show that the CARES Act’s flat supplement gave many unemployed workers more than their previous wage. The size of these effects is contested.

The pandemic was the ideal case for short-time work: a shock everyone expected to end. The Great Recession, which reshaped construction and finance, was less so.

Ask the policy students: which tool would you choose for a shock that permanently shrinks one industry, such as coal or retail?

Recessions are mostly investment slumps

Recessions happen when households stop spending.

In the average recession since 1953 consumption rose 0.4%, while investment fell 12.9%. In 2008–09 investment alone subtracted 4.79 points from GDP, three times as much as consumption. Only 2020, when shops and travel shut, was led by consumption.

Numbers: the notebook’s Table 10.1 and the contributions of equation (13).

Why investment swings so much: firms can postpone new machines and buildings, and households can postpone new houses (residential investment is part of investment). Postponing consumption is harder, and households smooth it on long-run income.

The exception proves the point: in 2020 consumption fell because the goods and services themselves were not available, and because people feared infection.

How much of their own money should banks put in?

Regulators set a floor on equity as a share of assets. How high should it be?

What the framework says

  • The capital ratio is the cushion: \(f^* = E/A\).
  • At 10% equity a bank survives a 10% fall in its assets; at Bear Stearns’ leverage, 2.8%.
  • More equity also makes runs harder to start.

What is debated

  • Banks: equity is costly, so lending shrinks.
  • Critics: most of that cost is private, not social.
  • Higher floors push risk to less-regulated lenders.

The left column is Act II: equation (7) says the fall that wipes out the owners equals the capital ratio, so a capital requirement is a ceiling on leverage (6 percent ↔︎ ℓ ≤ 15.7) and a floor on the cushion. The bank-run model adds that more equity E raises the run needed to sink a bank.

After 2008 the Basel III accords raised the minimum equity banks must hold and added a simple leverage-ratio floor alongside the risk-weighted rules.

The research. Admati and Hellwig (2013, The Bankers’ New Clothes, Princeton University Press) argue that equity is expensive for bankers mainly because debt is subsidized (tax deductions and implicit guarantees), and that much higher equity, a multiple of the pre-crisis levels, would cost society little. Miles, Yang and Marcheggiano (2013, Economic Journal) estimate the costs and benefits and find an optimal capital ratio far above the pre-crisis requirements. Others warn that higher requirements raise loan rates and move lending to “shadow banks” that the rules do not cover. The level is contested; the direction since 2008 has been up.

Ask the policy students: if you were the regulator, would you rather set one simple leverage limit, or many risk-weighted ones that banks can game?

Should the central bank and the government rescue banks in a panic?

In 2008 the Fed lent to banks and AIG, and Congress created the TARP rescue fund. Lehman Brothers was allowed to fail. Was rescuing right?

The case for rescues

  • A run can sink a solvent bank (Act II).
  • Lending against illiquid assets removes the fire sale.
  • The spread peaked the month after Lehman failed.

The case against

  • Rescues reward risk-taking.
  • In a panic, illiquid and insolvent look alike.
  • Uninsured lenders still run: SVB in 2023.

The left column is the bank-run model: with δ* = 18.2 percent a full run sinks our solvent bank, and a lender of last resort that lends against illiquid assets spares it the fire sale. Deposit insurance (FDIC, since 1933) removes the reason to run. Fig. 3: the TED spread peaked at 3.35 points in October 2008, the month after Lehman failed.

Bagehot’s rule (Lombard Street, 1873): in a panic, lend freely, at a penalty rate, against good collateral, to solvent institutions. The hard part is the last word.

The research. Diamond and Dybvig (1983, Journal of Political Economy) give the case for deposit insurance. Gorton and Metrick (2012, Journal of Financial Economics) show that the 2007–08 panic was a run on short-term “repo” funding of banks, outside deposit insurance. Bernanke (2018, Brookings Papers on Economic Activity) argues that the panic in funding markets, more than the housing losses themselves, explains why the recession was so deep. In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank lost uninsured deposits in a day; the authorities then guaranteed all its deposits (Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, 2023, review of the supervision of SVB). Each rescue reopens the moral-hazard debate on the next slide.

Ask the policy students: should deposit insurance cover all deposits, or only small ones?

“Bailouts reward the reckless”

A skeptic says: “Banks earned huge profits from leverage before 2007, and taxpayers covered their losses after. If every big bank knows it will be rescued, the next one will take even bigger bets.”

Reply: the worry is real, but letting a leveraged bank fail in a panic punishes more than its owners. Each dollar it loses forces \(\ell\) dollars of fire sales that hit healthy banks too. The better answer is to make owners bear losses before a crisis, with more equity, and to rescue the system, not the shareholders.

Take the objection seriously: it is the moral-hazard argument, and the evidence supports it. Kelly, Lustig and Van Nieuwerburgh (2016, American Economic Review) find that options on the financial sector in 2007–09 were priced as if the government would cap losses for the sector as a whole, an implicit guarantee investors could see. Large banks borrow more cheaply than small ones, which is consistent with a too-big-to-fail subsidy.

The reply uses Act II. Equation (8): every dollar of losses forces ℓ dollars of sales; with many banks selling the same assets, prices fall for everyone. The TED spread’s peak came after Lehman failed, not before. Veronesi and Zingales (2010, Journal of Financial Economics) estimate that the October 2008 capital injections raised the value of banks’ debt and equity by more than they cost taxpayers, because they stopped a run, while warning that the gains went largely to bondholders.

Rescues can be designed to hurt owners: in 2008, shareholders of Bear Stearns, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and AIG lost most of their stake. Capital requirements (previous policy slide) are the ex ante answer.

Ask: what is the difference between rescuing a bank and rescuing its shareholders?

What to remember from this chapter

  • A housing bust (−35.7% in real prices) started the crisis; spreads exploded and stocks lost half their value.
  • Leverage multiplies owners’ returns by \(1+\ell\); a fall of \(1/(1+\ell)\) wipes them out; each $1 lost forces \(\ell\) dollars of sales. Runs can sink a solvent bank.
  • The Great Recession was long (77 months to regain the jobs); the pandemic slump was deeper but short.
  • Inflation returned a year into the pandemic; in Europe it was mostly food and energy.
  • Measure carefully: percent vs points, log vs percent changes, which series, which vintage.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: how did a fall in house prices break the banks, and how was the pandemic slump different? A good answer: banks held mortgage securities with high leverage, so a modest fall in their value wiped out equity, forced fire sales and froze lending; the pandemic was a sudden stop with no broken balance sheets, so the economy came back fast.

For the policy students, three habits: ask who holds the losses and with how much leverage; separate a liquidity problem from a solvency problem; and judge a slump by its depth times its length.
