Learning goals

Consumption is more than two-thirds of GDP. Earlier chapters treated it simply, as a fixed share of income. This chapter asks how a household chooses to spend over its lifetime. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • write down the intertemporal budget constraint and split total wealth into financial wealth and human wealth;
  • explain diminishing marginal utility, and why it makes people want to smooth consumption;
  • solve the two-period neoclassical consumption model with log utility, and check the Euler equation;
  • compute the marginal propensity to consume with and without borrowing constraints, and see why Ricardian equivalence can fail;
  • read U.S. data on household debt, the personal saving rate and household wealth;
  • in Python: write functions with text options, find a maximum on a grid with np.argmax, turn monthly data into quarterly data with resample, join two tables with merge, and fit a line through a scatter.

The model in code

The neoclassical consumption model follows one consumer, called Irving after the economist Irving Fisher, over just two periods: today and the future. It has two building blocks.

1. The budget. Irving starts with financial wealth \(f_{\text{today}}\) (savings, stocks, bonds) and earns labor income \(y_{\text{today}}\) today and \(y_{\text{future}}\) in the future. What he saves today earns the real interest rate \(R\). Putting the budgets of the two periods together gives the intertemporal budget constraint:

\[c_{\text{today}}+\frac{c_{\text{future}}}{1+R} \;=\; \underbrace{f_{\text{today}}}_{\text{financial wealth}}+\underbrace{y_{\text{today}}+\frac{y_{\text{future}}}{1+R}}_{\text{human wealth}} \;\equiv\; \bar{X} \qquad (16.3)\]

The left side is the present value of consumption. The right side is total wealth \(\bar{X}\). Future amounts are divided by \(1+R\), because 1 dollar saved today turns into \(1+R\) dollars in the future.

2. Utility. Each period, consumption \(c\) gives a flow of utility \(u(c)\), and lifetime utility adds the two periods, with a weight \(\beta\) on the future (a measure of patience):

\[U = u(c_{\text{today}}) + \beta\,u(c_{\text{future}}). \qquad (16.4)\]

The textbook’s utility function is the logarithm, \(u(c)=\log c\). Its marginal utility, the extra utility from one more unit of consumption, is simply \(u'(c)=1/c\). We also allow a second shape, the square root \(u(c)=\sqrt{c}\), whose marginal utility is \(1/(2\sqrt{c})\).

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. Writing form="log" gives the argument a default value, used whenever we do not pass one. if ... : runs the indented line only when the condition is true; otherwise Python moves on. np.log and np.sqrt work on a single number and on a whole array of numbers.

def utility(c, form="log"):
    """Flow utility u(c) (Figure 16.1): log c, or the square root of c."""
    if form == "log":
        return np.log(c)
    return np.sqrt(c)


def marginal_utility(c, form="log"):
    """Marginal utility u'(c): 1/c for log utility, 1/(2 sqrt c) for the square root."""
    if form == "log":
        return 1 / c
    return 1 / (2 * np.sqrt(c))


def lifetime_utility(c_today, c_future, beta=1.0, form="log"):
    """Lifetime utility U = u(c_today) + beta u(c_future)   (equation 16.4)."""
    return utility(c_today, form) + beta * utility(c_future, form)

Irving’s choice. Irving picks \(c_{\text{today}}\) and \(c_{\text{future}}\) to make \(U\) as large as possible while respecting his budget (16.3). At the best choice he cannot gain by moving one unit of consumption between the periods. One unit consumed today gives \(u'(c_{\text{today}})\). Saved instead, it becomes \(1+R\) units in the future, each worth \(\beta u'(c_{\text{future}})\) today. The two must be equal, which is the Euler equation:

\[u'(c_{\text{today}})=\beta(1+R)\,u'(c_{\text{future}}) \quad (16.6) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{c_{\text{future}}}{c_{\text{today}}}=\beta(1+R) \;\;\text{with log utility.} \quad (16.8)\]

With \(\beta=1\), the Euler equation says \(c_{\text{future}}/(1+R) = c_{\text{today}}\). Put this into the budget and Irving spends exactly half of his total wealth today:

\[c_{\text{today}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bar{X} \quad (16.9) \qquad\qquad c_{\text{future}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,(1+R)\,\bar{X}. \quad (16.10)\]

A function can return two results at once, separated by a comma.

def total_wealth(f_today, y_today, y_future, R):
    """Total wealth X = f_today + y_today + y_future / (1 + R)   (equation 16.3)."""
    return f_today + y_today + y_future / (1 + R)


def consumption_plan(f_today, y_today, y_future, R):
    """Irving's choice with log utility and beta = 1: c_today = X/2, c_future = (1 + R) X/2   (16.9, 16.10)."""
    X = total_wealth(f_today, y_today, y_future, R)
    return X / 2, (1 + R) * X / 2

The shared baseline. Think of one unit as 10,000 dollars, so Irving looks like a student who expects to earn much more later.

Python note. An f-string such as f"X = {X:.2f}" inserts the value of a variable into a piece of text; :.2f means “show two decimals”. Writing c_today, c_future = ... unpacks the two results of the function.

Checking the solution without calculus

The formula \(c_{\text{today}}=\bar{X}/2\) comes from the Euler equation. We can also let the computer search for the best choice. Every \(c_{\text{today}}\) on a fine grid leaves \(c_{\text{future}}=(1+R)(\bar{X}-c_{\text{today}})\) for the future; we compute lifetime utility for each one and pick the largest. We use \(R=0.05\), so that the future is not a copy of today.

Python note. np.linspace(a, b, n) creates n evenly spaced numbers from a to b. Arithmetic on an array works element by element, so lifetime_utility returns one value per grid point. np.argmax gives the position of the largest value, and grid[best] picks the matching number.

R = 0.05
X = total_wealth(f_today, y_today, y_future, R)
grid = np.linspace(0.01, X - 0.01, 100001)          # candidate values of c_today
U = lifetime_utility(grid, (1 + R) * (X - grid))    # lifetime utility of each candidate
best = np.argmax(U)

c_today, c_future = consumption_plan(f_today, y_today, y_future, R)
print(f"Grid search: c_today = {grid[best]:.4f}    formula (16.9): X/2 = {c_today:.4f}")
print(f"Euler check: c_future / c_today = {c_future / c_today:.4f} = beta (1 + R) = {1.0 * (1 + R):.4f}")
print(f"Marginal utilities: u'(c_today) = {marginal_utility(c_today):.4f}, "
      f"beta (1 + R) u'(c_future) = {(1 + R) * marginal_utility(c_future):.4f}")
Grid search: c_today = 2.8810    formula (16.9): X/2 = 2.8810
Euler check: c_future / c_today = 1.0500 = beta (1 + R) = 1.0500
Marginal utilities: u'(c_today) = 0.3471, beta (1 + R) u'(c_future) = 0.3471

The search and the formula agree, and the two sides of the Euler equation are equal. With \(R=0.05\) consumption grows by 5 percent from today to the future, exactly \(\beta(1+R)=1.05\).

Figure 1: Flow utility

Goal. See diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit of consumption adds less utility than the one before.

The curve is the textbook’s \(u(c)=\log c\). Its slope at any point is the marginal utility \(u'(c)=1/c\). The figure marks one consumption level and draws the tangent line there, whose slope is \(u'(c)\). As \(c\) grows, the curve, and the tangent with it, becomes flatter and flatter.

Python note. go.Figure() starts an empty chart, and each add_trace adds one curve or set of points. fig.add_annotation writes a text label at a point; we use it to name the curve at its right end, as the textbook does. We fix the axis ranges so that you can see what changes when you move a slider.

def flow_utility(c=2.0, form="log"):
    """Figure 16.1: flow utility u(c), with the tangent at c, whose slope is the marginal utility."""
    grid = np.linspace(0.01, 10, 1000)
    u, mu = utility(c, form), marginal_utility(c, form)
    gain = utility(c + 1, form) - u                         # utility added by one more unit
    near = np.linspace(max(c - 2, 0.01), c + 2, 50)         # a short stretch around c for the tangent
    fig = go.Figure()
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=grid, y=utility(grid, form), name="u(c)", line=dict(color=BLUE, width=4)))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=near, y=u + mu * (near - c), name="tangent", mode="lines",
                             line=dict(color=GREEN, width=2, dash="dash")))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[c], y=[u], name="chosen c", mode="markers", marker=dict(color="black", size=11)))
    name = {"log": "u(c) = log c", "square root": "u(c) = √c"}[form]
    fig.add_annotation(x=10, y=utility(10, form), text=name, showarrow=False, xanchor="right", yshift=16)
    fig.add_annotation(x=c, y=u, text=f"slope u′(c) = {mu:.2f}", showarrow=False, xanchor="left",
                       xshift=10, yshift=-22, font=dict(size=13, color=GREEN))
    fig.update_layout(title=f"c = {c:.1f}:  u(c) = {u:.2f},  marginal utility u′(c) = {mu:.2f},  "
                            f"one more unit adds {gain:.2f}",
                      showlegend=False,
                      xaxis=dict(title="Consumption, c", range=[0, 10.5]),
                      yaxis=dict(title="Utility", range=[-2.5, 3.5]))
    return fig

What to notice.

  • Utility always rises with consumption, but by less and less. At \(c=1\) the slope is \(1/1=1\); at \(c=2\) it is \(0.50\); at \(c=8\) it is only \(0.125\).
  • The title compares the slope with the utility of one whole extra unit. Going from 2 to 3 adds \(\log 3-\log 2 = 0.41\), a little less than the slope at 2, because the curve keeps flattening along the way.
  • The log curve has no floor: as \(c\) approaches zero, \(\log c\) falls without limit. Very low consumption is very painful, which is a strong reason to avoid it in any period.
  • The square-root curve has the same basic shape, rising and flattening. Any such concave curve shows diminishing marginal utility.

Figure 2: The desire to smooth consumption

Goal. See why diminishing marginal utility makes Irving prefer the same consumption in both periods to an uneven path with the same total.

Suppose Irving simply spent his income each period: \(c_1=y_{\text{today}}=1\) and \(c_2=y_{\text{future}}=5\). Or he could consume the average \(\bar{c}=(c_1+c_2)/2=3\) in both periods. With \(\beta=1\) and \(R=0\) both paths cost the same, and \(\bar{c}=3\) is exactly the solution \(\bar{X}/2\) of equation (16.9). Which path gives more utility?

  • The uneven path gives the average utility \(\tfrac{1}{2}[u(c_1)+u(c_2)]\), the midpoint of the dashed line between the two points on the curve (the open circle).
  • The smooth path gives \(u(\bar{c})\) in each period, the point on the curve above \(\bar{c}\) (the filled dot in the middle).

Because the curve bends, the point on the curve lies above the dashed line, so smoothing wins. Lifetime utility, twice these numbers, is higher with the smooth path.

The diagram draws dotted guides from each point down to the consumption axis. A small function adds one guide with its label, so that the figure function stays short.

def add_guide(fig, x, top, label):
    """A dotted line from the consumption axis up to the point (x, top), with a label at the axis."""
    fig.add_shape(type="line", x0=x, x1=x, y0=-2.5, y1=top, line=dict(color=GRAY, width=1, dash="dot"))
    if label:
        fig.add_annotation(x=x, y=-2.5, text=label, showarrow=False, yanchor="bottom", xshift=10,
                           font=dict(size=15))
    return fig
def smoothing_diagram(c1=1.0, c2=5.0, form="log"):
    """Figure 16.2: utility from smoothing, u(c-bar), against the average of u(c1) and u(c2)."""
    grid = np.linspace(0.01, 10, 1000)
    c_bar = (c1 + c2) / 2                                    # the same consumption in both periods
    u1, u2, u_bar = utility(c1, form), utility(c2, form), utility(c_bar, form)
    average = (u1 + u2) / 2                                  # average utility of the uneven path
    fig = go.Figure()
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=grid, y=utility(grid, form), line=dict(color=BLUE, width=4)))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[c1, c2], y=[u1, u2], mode="lines", line=dict(color=GREEN, width=3, dash="dash")))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[c1, c_bar, c2], y=[u1, u_bar, u2], mode="markers+text", text=["", "u(c̄)", ""],
                             textposition="top left", marker=dict(color="black", size=11)))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[c_bar], y=[average], mode="markers+text", text=["average utility"],
                             textposition="bottom right",
                             marker=dict(color="white", size=11, line=dict(color="black", width=1.5))))
    for x, top, label in [(c1, u1, "c₁"), (c_bar, u_bar, "c̄"), (c2, u2, "c₂")]:

What to notice.

  • In the baseline, \(u(\bar{c}) = \log 3 = 1.10\) while the average utility is \(\tfrac{1}{2}(\log 1 + \log 5) = 0.80\). Smoothing raises utility by 0.29 in each period.
  • The more uneven the path, the larger the gain. Move \(c_2\) toward \(c_1\): the dashed line shrinks toward the curve and the gain falls to zero when \(c_1=c_2\).
  • With square-root utility the gain is smaller (0.11 in the baseline), because that curve bends less. The desire to smooth comes from the curvature of \(u(c)\).
  • This is the idea behind the permanent-income hypothesis: consumption follows total wealth, an average of lifetime resources, rather than this period’s income.

Key numbers: the marginal propensity to consume

How much does consumption today rise when Irving gets a little more? The ratio of the rise in consumption to the rise in resources is the marginal propensity to consume (MPC). The textbook finds that 100 extra dollars today raise consumption by 50 dollars, an MPC of 1/2.

A borrowing constraint changes the answer. If Irving has no financial wealth and cannot borrow, his consumption today can be no more than what he has today (equation 16.11 with \(f_{\text{today}}=0\)):

\[c_{\text{today}} \le f_{\text{today}} + y_{\text{today}}.\]

Our baseline Irving wants to consume 3 but has only 1, so the constraint binds: he consumes his whole income, and saves nothing for the future. The function below picks the smaller of the two numbers with Python’s min.

def constrained_today(f_today, y_today, y_future, R):
    """c_today when Irving cannot borrow: the plan of (16.9), but no more than f_today + y_today (16.11)."""
    wanted, later = consumption_plan(f_today, y_today, y_future, R)
    return min(wanted, f_today + y_today)

We now give both versions of Irving 0.01 more (100 dollars, if one unit is 10,000 dollars) in three ways, and divide the change in consumption today by 0.01. The last row is a tax cut today of 0.01, paid for by a tax increase of \(0.01(1+R)\) in the future, which leaves total wealth unchanged.

Python note. shocks is a dictionary: each entry pairs a name with three changes (to \(f_{\text{today}}\), \(y_{\text{today}}\) and \(y_{\text{future}}\)). .items() hands the entries to the for loop one at a time. consumption_plan(...)[0] keeps only the first of its two results, \(c_{\text{today}}\). rows.append(...) adds one row to a list, and pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=[...]) turns the list of rows into a table with named columns.

R, extra = 0.05, 0.01
shocks = {"income today +0.01": (0, extra, 0),
          "income in the future +0.01": (0, 0, extra),
          "financial wealth +0.01": (extra, 0, 0),
          "tax cut today, paid by a tax rise later": (0, extra, -extra * (1 + R))}
rows = []
for shock, (d_f, d_today, d_future) in shocks.items():
    free_before = consumption_plan(f_today, y_today, y_future, R)[0]
    free_after = consumption_plan(f_today + d_f, y_today + d_today, y_future + d_future, R)[0]
    tied_before = constrained_today(f_today, y_today, y_future, R)
    tied_after = constrained_today(f_today + d_f, y_today + d_today, y_future + d_future, R)
    free = round((free_after - free_before) / extra, 3) + 0.0     # + 0.0 turns a rounded -0.0 into 0.0
    tied = round((tied_after - tied_before) / extra, 3) + 0.0
    rows.append([shock, free, tied])
mpc = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["Irving receives ...", "MPC, can borrow", "MPC, cannot borrow"])

  • When Irving can borrow, an extra 100 dollars today raises consumption by 50 dollars (MPC 0.5), the textbook’s number. Future income counts for a little less, \(0.5/1.05 = 0.476\), because it is discounted.
  • The tax cut paid by later taxes leaves total wealth, and so consumption, unchanged: this is Ricardian equivalence.
  • The constrained Irving spends every extra dollar he gets today (MPC 1), including the tax cut. Ricardian equivalence fails for him. Extra future income does not help him today at all.

With more periods of life the MPC shrinks. With \(R=0\) and \(\beta=1\), a consumer who will live \(T\) more periods spreads an extra dollar evenly over them, so the MPC is \(1/T\):

The last two model rows use the Euler equation (16.8) the other way round. If long-run growth fixes consumption growth at \(g = 2\) percent a year, then \(1+g = \beta(1+R)\), so the interest rate must be \(R = (1+g)/\beta - 1\).

def euler_interest_rate(g, beta=1.0):
    """The real interest rate that makes consumption grow at rate g: R = (1 + g) / beta - 1   (from 16.8)."""
    return (1 + g) / beta - 1

Summary

  • In the neoclassical consumption model, a consumer chooses consumption today and in the future to maximize lifetime utility, subject to the intertemporal budget constraint: the present value of consumption equals total wealth, financial plus human wealth.
  • Diminishing marginal utility makes people want to smooth consumption. With log utility and \(\beta=1\), Irving consumes half of his total wealth today, and the Euler equation links consumption growth to \(\beta(1+R)\), which is why interest rates and growth rates are similar numbers.
  • So consumption follows permanent income: the marginal propensity to consume out of a temporary windfall is small, and the timing of taxes does not matter (Ricardian equivalence). Borrowing constraints and precautionary saving raise the MPC toward 1 for many low-wealth households.
  • U.S. data show household debt doubling relative to GDP before 2008 and the saving rate falling to about 2.5 percent, while household wealth rose relative to income.
  • The next chapter turns to the other big private spending decision, investment by firms.
