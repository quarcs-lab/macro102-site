Learning goals

Chapter 11 built the IS curve, which links the real interest rate to short-run output. This chapter adds the two missing pieces of the short-run model: the MP curve (how the central bank sets the real interest rate) and the Phillips curve (how output moves inflation over time). By the end of it you will be able to:

  • explain why a central bank that sets a nominal interest rate also sets the real interest rate in the short run, using the Fisher equation and the assumption of sticky inflation;
  • use the IS/MP diagram to analyze monetary policy and demand shocks, such as the end of a housing bubble;
  • explain the Phillips curve \(\Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\tilde{Y}_t + \bar{o}\), with adaptive expectations and price shocks;
  • simulate the Volcker disinflation year by year and explain how mistakes contributed to the Great Inflation;
  • read U.S. data on the federal funds rate, the yield curve, inflation and the Covid-19 recession;
  • in Python: draw several panels with make_subplots, simulate a model with a loop, reshape tables with melt and pivot, animate a chart with animation_frame, and fit a trend with np.polyfit.

The model in code

The short-run model has three building blocks. Each one links two variables, and together they form a chain from the central bank’s interest rate to inflation.

1. The MP curve. The central bank sets a short-term nominal interest rate \(i_t\). The Fisher equation links it to the real interest rate \(R_t\) and the inflation rate \(\pi_t\):

\[i_t = R_t + \pi_t \qquad (12.1) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad R_t = i_t - \pi_t. \qquad (12.2)\]

The key assumption is sticky inflation: in the very short run (say six months), inflation does not react to monetary policy. So when the central bank moves \(i_t\), \(R_t\) moves one for one. In effect the central bank chooses the real interest rate, and the MP curve is a horizontal line at that rate.

2. The IS curve of Chapter 11 turns the real interest rate into short-run output:

\[\tilde{Y}_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R_t - \bar{r}). \qquad (11.12)\]

3. The Phillips curve. Firms raise their prices by the inflation they expect, plus an adjustment for demand. With adaptive expectations, \(\pi^e_t = \pi_{t-1}\), and with a price shock \(\bar{o}\) (an oil shock, for example):

\[\pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + \bar{\nu}\tilde{Y}_t + \bar{o} \qquad (12.7) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\tilde{Y}_t + \bar{o}. \qquad (12.8)\]

One baseline for every diagram. The textbook draws its diagrams without numbers, apart from a few examples. All the figures of this notebook start from one set of values:

  • the IS curve of Chapter 11’s numerical example: no demand shock \(\bar{a} = 0\), \(\bar{b} = 2\) and a marginal product of capital \(\bar{r} = 5\) percent. Then the textbook’s “\(-2\) percent” in Figures 12.4, 12.5 and 12.10 comes from \(R' = 6\) percent or from \(\bar{a}' = -2\);
  • the slope of the Phillips curve \(\bar{\nu} = 0.5\) (illustrative; Chapter 9 estimated about 1/3 for the United States) and no price shock, \(\bar{o} = 0\);
  • sticky inflation of 2 percent in normal times and 10 percent at the start of the Volcker disinflation (the textbook’s number);
  • a money market with money demand \(M^d = 10 - i\) and money supply \(\bar{M}^s = 5\) (illustrative units), so that the nominal interest rate is 5 percent;
  • for the productivity slowdown of Figure 13, potential output growth of 3.5 percent a year before 1973 and 2.5 percent after (illustrative).

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. Writing b=B_BAR gives the argument a default value, used whenever we do not pass one; here the default is a constant we defined just before. The text in triple quotes is a short description of what the function does.

A_BAR, B_BAR, R_BAR = 0.0, 2.0, 5.0     # IS curve: no demand shock, b = 2, r_bar = 5 percent
NU, O_BAR = 0.5, 0.0                     # Phillips curve: slope nu = 0.5, no price shock
M0, M_BAR = 10.0, 5.0                    # money market: demand M = 10 - i, supply 5 (units)


def real_rate(i, pi):
    """Equation 12.2, the Fisher equation: the real interest rate R = i - pi, in percent."""
    return i - pi


def is_curve(R, a=A_BAR, b=B_BAR, r_bar=R_BAR):
    """Equation 11.12, the IS curve: short-run output Y~ = a - b (R - r_bar), in percent."""
    return a - b * (R - r_bar)


def rate_for_output(Y, a=A_BAR, b=B_BAR, r_bar=R_BAR):
    """The IS curve solved for R: the real interest rate at which short-run output equals Y."""
    return r_bar + (a - Y) / b


def phillips(Y, nu=NU, o=O_BAR):
    """Equation 12.8, the Phillips curve: the change in inflation = nu * Y~ + o, in percentage points."""
    return nu * Y + o

The money market of Section 12.7 has two more functions. People hold less money when the nominal interest rate, the opportunity cost of holding money, is higher.

def money_demand(i, m0=M0):
    """Figure 12.18: money demanded falls as the nominal interest rate rises, M = m0 - i."""
    return m0 - i


def market_rate(M_s, m0=M0):
    """The nominal rate that clears the money market: m0 - i = M_s, so i = m0 - M_s."""
    return m0 - M_s

The full model runs year by year: the central bank sets \(R_t\), the IS curve gives \(\tilde{Y}_t\), and the Phillips curve turns last year’s inflation into this year’s. simulate repeats these three steps for a whole path of interest rates.

Python note. A for loop repeats the indented lines once for each number in R_path. Y_path = [] starts an empty list, and .append(...) adds one number at its end. np.array(...) turns a list into an array, so that we can do arithmetic with it.

def simulate(R_path, pi_start, nu=NU, a=A_BAR):
    """The short-run model year by year: MP sets R, IS gives Y~, the Phillips curve gives pi (eq. 12.7)."""
    Y_path, pi_path = [], []
    pi = pi_start                          # inflation of the year before the first year
    for R in R_path:
        Y = is_curve(R, a)
        pi = pi + phillips(Y, nu)          # last year's inflation, adjusted for demand
        Y_path.append(Y)
        pi_path.append(pi)
    return np.array(Y_path), np.array(pi_path)

Drawing tools

All the diagrams of this chapter are built from the same few pieces, so we write them once:

  • add_line draws a curve through some points and writes its label at the right end, as the textbook does. When an older curve of the same panel ends within 0.6 axis units, its label is removed, so that two labels never overlap;
  • add_is, add_pc and add_demand draw an IS curve, a Phillips curve and a money demand curve inside the fixed axes;
  • add_point marks a point with dashed guides to the axes and writes its label at the top right, or wherever where says, and add_arrow draws an arrow;
  • set_axes sets fixed axes, so that you can see the curves move when you change a slider.

Every function takes row and col arguments, so it can draw in one panel of a figure with several panels.

Python note. go.Figure() starts an empty chart and add_trace adds a line or a set of points. [""] * 3 is the list ["", "", ""], and + [label] adds one more element, so only the last point of a curve gets the label. fig.data[-1] is the trace just added and fig.data[:-1] all the earlier ones. max(a, b) and min(a, b) give the larger and the smaller of two numbers, and abs(x) the size of x without its sign. != means “is not equal to”.

def add_line(fig, x, y, label, color, dash="solid", row=None, col=None):
    """Draw a curve through the points (x, y) and write its label at the right end."""
    text = [""] * (len(x) - 1) + [label]
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=x, y=y, mode="lines+text", text=text, textposition="middle right",
                             textfont=dict(size=15, color=color), cliponaxis=False, showlegend=False,
                             line=dict(color=color, width=4, dash=dash)), row=row, col=col)
    new = fig.data[-1]
    for old in fig.data[:-1]:                  # an older curve of the same panel ending close by loses its label
        close = abs(old.x[-1] - new.x[-1]) < 0.6 and abs(old.y[-1] - new.y[-1]) < 0.6
        if old.mode == "lines+text" and old.xaxis == new.xaxis and close:
            old.text = None
def add_point(fig, x, y, label, x0, y0, row=None, col=None, where="top right"):
    """Mark the point (x, y) with a dot, a label (at `where`) and dashed guides to the axes, which start at (x0, y0)."""
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[x0, x, x], y=[y, y, y0], mode="lines", showlegend=False, hoverinfo="skip",
                             line=dict(color="black", width=1, dash="dash")), row=row, col=col)
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[x], y=[y], mode="markers+text", text=[label], textposition=where,
                             textfont=dict(size=16), showlegend=False, marker=dict(color="black", size=12)),
                  row=row, col=col)


def add_arrow(fig, x0, y0, x1, y1, row=None, col=None):
    """Draw an arrow from (x0, y0) to (x1, y1); nothing if the two points are the same."""
    if (x0, y0) != (x1, y1):
        fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[x0, x1], y=[y0, y1], mode="lines+markers", showlegend=False, hoverinfo="skip",
                                 line=dict(color="black", width=2),
                                 marker=dict(symbol="arrow", size=[0, 14], angleref="previous", color="black")),
                      row=row, col=col)
def add_is(fig, label, color, a=A_BAR, b=B_BAR, dash="solid", row=None, col=None):
    """Draw the IS curve inside the axes (R between 1 and 9.5 percent, Y~ between -7 and 6)."""
    Y = np.array([max(-7.0, is_curve(9.5, a, b)), min(6.0, is_curve(1.0, a, b))])
    add_line(fig, Y, rate_for_output(Y, a, b), label, color, dash, row, col)


def add_pc(fig, label, color, nu=NU, o=O_BAR, dash="solid", row=None, col=None):
    """Draw the Phillips curve inside the axes (change in inflation between -3.6 and 3.4 points)."""
    Y = np.array([max(-7.0, (-3.6 - o) / nu), min(6.0, (3.4 - o) / nu)])
    add_line(fig, Y, phillips(Y, nu, o), label, color, dash, row, col)


def add_demand(fig, label, color, m0=M0, dash="solid"):
    """Draw the money demand curve M = m0 - i for nominal rates from 9 down to 1 percent."""
    i = np.array([9.0, 1.0])
    add_line(fig, money_demand(i, m0), i, label, color, dash)


def set_axes(fig, xaxis, yaxis, row=None, col=None):
    """Fixed axes (title, range, ticks), so that you can see the curves move."""
    fig.update_xaxes(xaxis, row=row, col=col)
    fig.update_yaxes(yaxis, row=row, col=col)

Figure 1: The structure of the short-run model

Goal. See the whole short-run model at once: how the central bank’s nominal interest rate ends up changing inflation.

The textbook’s Figure 12.1 is a chain of four circles. Each arrow is one building block:

\[i_t \;\xrightarrow{\text{MP curve}}\; R_t \;\xrightarrow{\text{IS curve}}\; \tilde{Y}_t \;\xrightarrow{\text{Phillips curve}}\; \Delta\pi_t\]

Here the chain is live. You choose the inputs: the nominal interest rate \(i\) that the central bank sets, the current (sticky) inflation rate \(\pi\), and the demand shock \(\bar{a}\). The circles show the outcomes that the model computes: the real interest rate, short-run output and the change in inflation. The baseline is normal times: inflation of 2 percent and a nominal rate of 7 percent, so that \(R = 5\% = \bar{r}\) and output is at potential.

Python note. fig.add_shape(type="circle", ...) draws a circle inside the box from (x0, y0) to (x1, y1), and add_annotation(..., showarrow=False) writes text at a point. for k in range(4): repeats the indented lines for k = 0, 1, 2, 3, so names[k] picks one name at a time. scaleanchor="x" gives both axes the same scale, so the circles stay round, and visible=False hides the axes.

def short_run_model(i=7.0, pi=2.0, a=0.0):
    """Figure 12.1: the nominal rate sets R (MP), R sets Y~ (IS), and Y~ sets the change in inflation (PC)."""
    R = real_rate(i, pi)
    Y = is_curve(R, a)
    dpi = phillips(Y)
    names = ["Nominal<br>interest rate, i", "Real interest<br>rate, R", "Short-run<br>output, Ỹ", "Change in<br>inflation, Δπ"]
    values = [f"{i:.1f}%", f"{R:.1f}%", f"{Y:+.1f}%", f"{dpi:+.2f}"]
    links = ["MP<br>curve", "IS<br>curve", "Phillips<br>curve"]
    fig = go.Figure()
    for k in range(4):
        fig.add_shape(type="circle", x0=3.4 * k - 1.15, x1=3.4 * k + 1.15, y0=-1.15, y1=1.15,
                      line=dict(color=TEAL, width=3), fillcolor="#e6f5f1")
        fig.add_annotation(x=3.4 * k, y=0.35, text=names[k], showarrow=False, font=dict(size=12))
        fig.add_annotation(x=3.4 * k, y=-0.45, text=f"<b>{values[k]}</b>", showarrow=False, font=dict(size=20, color=BLUE))
    for k in range(3):
        add_arrow(fig, 3.4 * k + 1.2, 0.0, 3.4 * k + 2.2, 0.0)
        fig.add_annotation(x=3.4 * k + 1.7, y=0.5, text=links[k], showarrow=False, font=dict(size=12))
    fig.add_annotation(x=5.1, y=-1.7, showarrow=False, font=dict(size=14),

Figure 3: The MP curve in the IS/MP diagram

Goal. Put the MP curve and the IS curve in one diagram and read off short-run output.

With sticky inflation, the central bank picks the real interest rate. The MP curve is simply a horizontal line at that rate. The IS curve then tells us the output that goes with it: the economy is where the two curves cross. This is the IS/MP diagram. When the central bank sets \(R = \bar{r}\) and there is no demand shock, the curves cross at \(\tilde{Y} = 0\), point \(A\): output is at potential.

The textbook uses this diagram for three figures: the starting point (Figure 12.3), a higher interest rate (Figure 12.4), and the Volcker tightening (Figure 12.10). So we write one function, ismp_experiment, for all three. Its arguments are the new values: the real interest rate R_new that the central bank sets, the demand shock a_new and the slope b. With the defaults nothing changes. When a curve moves, the old one stays behind as a dashed gray line.

Python note. if ... else runs one block of lines or the other. (a_new, b) != (A_BAR, B_BAR) is true as soon as one of the two numbers differs from the baseline.

Figure 5: Stabilizing the economy after a housing bubble

Goal. See how monetary policy can offset an aggregate demand shock.

When a housing bubble bursts, households lose wealth and confidence, and spend less: the demand shock \(\bar{a}\) falls to \(\bar{a}' < 0\). The IS curve shifts left, and at an unchanged interest rate the economy moves from \(A\) to \(B\), a recession (panel a; the textbook uses \(-2\) percent as its example). The central bank can respond by cutting the real interest rate to

\[R' = \bar{r} + \frac{\bar{a}'}{\bar{b}},\]

the rate at which the new IS curve gives \(\tilde{Y} = 0\) (our rate_for_output). Cheaper borrowing raises investment and makes up for the fall in \(\bar{a}\): the economy moves to \(C\), back at potential (panel b).

Figure 7: The Phillips curve

Goal. Draw the Phillips curve and see how the state of the economy changes inflation.

Imagine you run a firm and inflation has been 5 percent a year. You expect it to stay there, so normally you raise your prices by 5 percent. If demand for your products is weak, you raise them by less, to win back customers; if demand is strong, by more. When all firms do this, inflation equals last year’s inflation plus an adjustment for demand:

\[\Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\,\tilde{Y}_t + \bar{o}.\]

A booming economy (\(\tilde{Y} > 0\)) raises inflation; a slumping one lowers it. The slope \(\bar{\nu}\) measures how strongly prices react to demand. As with the IS diagram, one function draws three textbook figures: the Phillips curve itself (12.7), an oil shock (12.8) and a recession (12.11). Its arguments are short-run output Y_new, the price shock o_new and the slope nu.

Python note. for x, y, anchor, text in REGIONS: takes the list of (x, y, anchor, text) groups one at a time and unpacks each into four names. dict(X_OUTPUT, title=...) copies the settings of X_OUTPUT and replaces the title.

A brief history. A. W. Phillips first related the level of (wage) inflation to economic activity. In the 1960s many economists concluded that a country could keep output high for ever by accepting, say, 5 instead of 2 percent inflation. Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps argued that firms would soon build the higher inflation into their prices: holding output above potential would make inflation rise year after year, which is the version of the curve used here. The rising inflation of the 1970s proved them right.

When oil prices jump, gasoline, airline tickets and heating cost more at once, and firms that use oil as an input pass the higher cost on to their prices. Figure 13: Mistaking a slowdown in potential for a recession

Goal. See how a slowdown in potential output, mistaken for a recession, can feed inflation.

From the early 1970s, U.S. productivity growth slowed down, so potential output grew more slowly. At the time, policymakers thought potential output was still growing at its old rate. They saw actual output fall behind that believed path, read it as a recession (\(\tilde{Y}_{\text{perceived}} < 0\)), and lowered interest rates. In truth output was then above its true potential (\(\tilde{Y}_{\text{true}} > 0\)), and through the Phillips curve inflation rose.

The textbook’s figure is stylized, so we describe it with three numbers: potential growth before 1973 (g_old), after 1973 (g_new), and how far the stimulus eventually pushes output above true potential (gap_max, reached gradually). Output is an index with 1973 = 100. This small model is used only here, so we define it in this section.

Python note. np.exp is the exponential function: an index growing at g percent a year is \(100\,e^{g\,(t - 1973)/100}\). np.minimum(x, 0) keeps the negative values of x and sets the others to 0, and np.maximum(x, 0) does the opposite. continue skips the rest of the loop for the current item.

def output_paths(g_old=3.5, g_new=2.5, gap_max=4.0):
    """Figure 12.13: believed potential, true potential and actual output (1973 = 100)."""
    years = np.arange(1965, 1986)
    before, after = np.minimum(years - 1973, 0), np.maximum(years - 1973, 0)
    believed = 100 * np.exp(g_old * (years - 1973) / 100)
    true = 100 * np.exp((g_old * before + g_new * after) / 100)
    gap_true = gap_max * (1 - np.exp(-after / 4))      # the stimulus pushes output above true potential
    actual = true * (1 + gap_true / 100)
    return years, believed, true, actual

Figure 18: How the central bank sets the nominal interest rate

Goal. See where “money” comes in: the central bank sets the interest rate through the money market.

The nominal interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money: cash and checking accounts pay nothing, while a savings or money-market account pays \(i\). At 1 percent you might as well hold cash; at 20 percent you would keep as little as possible. So money demand \(M^d\) falls as \(i\) rises. The central bank decides how much money to supply, \(M^s\): a vertical line. The interest rate \(i^*\) is where people want to hold exactly the money supplied. If \(i\) were higher, people would want less money than there is, and the rate would fall; if lower, the opposite.

As before, one function draws two textbook figures: this one (12.18) and a change in the money supply (12.19). Its arguments are the new money supply M_new and the new level of money demand m0_new (demand is \(M^d = m_0 - i\)).

Python note. "Mᵈ′" if m0_new != M0 else "Mᵈ" is a one-line if: it gives the first text when the condition is true and the second otherwise.

Goal. Answer Exercise 12 of the textbook: how mistaking the productivity slowdown for a demand shock could have caused the Great Inflation.

The four panels tell the story of Figure 13 with the model’s diagrams:

    1. Policymakers believe a negative demand shock \(\bar{a}' < 0\) caused the slowdown: they think the IS curve shifted to IS′, and the economy is at \(B\), below potential.
    1. So they lower the real interest rate to \(R' = \bar{r} + \bar{a}'/\bar{b}\), which, they believe, takes the economy back to potential at \(C\).

What to notice.

  • Believing in a shock of \(-2\), the Fed cuts \(R\) from 5 to 4 percent. On the true IS curve this takes output to \(+2\) percent, not 0, and inflation rises by 1 point a year for as long as the mistake lasts.
  • The bigger the shock policymakers believe in, the bigger the cut, the bigger the boom, and the faster inflation rises.
  • How can policymakers find out? Rising inflation with no other shock is the sign that output is above potential. In the 1970s the oil shocks also raised inflation, so the mistake took years to see.

Summary

  • The short-run model has three building blocks. Through the MP curve, the central bank’s nominal interest rate sets the real interest rate, because inflation is sticky. Through the IS curve, the real rate sets short-run output. Through the Phillips curve, \(\Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\tilde{Y}_t + \bar{o}\), output changes inflation over time.
  • In the IS/MP diagram, monetary policy can in principle offset demand shocks such as a housing bust, but long and variable lags make this hard in practice.
  • Lowering inflation requires a temporary recession: the Volcker disinflation brought inflation from 13.5 percent in 1980 to 3.2 percent in 1983, at the cost of the deepest recession in decades. Oil shocks, the belief in a permanent trade-off, and a productivity slowdown mistaken for a recession all contributed to the Great Inflation of the 1970s.
  • Central banks set the interest rate by supplying whatever money is demanded at their target. Through the term structure, the short rate also moves longer-term rates.
  • The next chapter adds the central bank’s policy rule and expectations, leading to the AS/AD framework.
