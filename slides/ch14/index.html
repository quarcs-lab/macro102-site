In 2008 the Fed cut its rate toward zero, and companies paid more to borrow

7.45% The average yield on solid (BAA-rated) U.S. corporate bonds in 2008, up from 6.48% in 2006

Over the same two years the 10-year Treasury yield fell from 4.79% to 3.67%. The central bank pushed one interest rate down, and the rate that firms actually pay went up. How can that happen, and what does it do to output and inflation?

Open with the puzzle. The numbers are the notebook’s Fig. 1 key numbers: annual averages of Moody’s BAA yield and the 10-year Treasury yield, 2006 and 2008.

Chapters 11 to 13 assumed that the Fed sets the real interest rate at which firms and households borrow. This chapter drops that assumption. Between the Fed’s rate and the rate on a loan sits a premium for the risk of not being repaid, and in a financial crisis it jumps.

Chapter 10 told the story of the crisis with the finance toolkit: balance sheets, leverage, runs. This chapter puts the crisis inside the short-run model and asks what policy can do when the interest rate reaches zero.

Ask the room: if you ran a firm in late 2008 and wanted to build a new plant, which rate would you care about, the Fed’s overnight rate or the rate on your own bonds?

Today: three questions

  • What does the crisis look like in the data? A risk premium that tripled, a policy rate stuck at zero, and bubbles before the great crashes.
  • What does a financial friction do to the short-run model? It acts like a fall in demand; at the zero lower bound, deflation can turn a slump into a spiral.
  • How did policy respond, and what came after? Money growth, quantitative easing, forward guidance; a lower path for potential output and productivity.

These are the three acts and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide.

The headline: a jump in the risk premium works like a collapse in demand, and the zero lower bound stops the Fed from offsetting it. Deflation can then turn a recession into a spiral, so central banks and governments need tools beyond the interest rate.

The model is the one of Chapters 11 to 13 (the IS curve, the MP curve, the Phillips curve and the AS/AD diagram) with one new parameter, the financial friction f̄.

The crisis in the data

Act I

When the Fed cuts, does borrowing get cheaper?

From 2007 to the end of 2008 the Fed cut its rate to almost zero. What happened to the rate on 10-year Treasury bonds, and to the rate on corporate bonds?

Predict the direction of each yield, and whether the gap between them grew or shrank.

Ask the room first. Most students expect both yields to fall, because “the Fed lowers interest rates”. Ask for a reason for each answer before showing the chart.

Hint if needed (only out loud): who is the safest borrower in the country, and where do scared investors put their money?

The government borrowed more cheaply, companies more dearly

Data: Moody’s BAA corporate bond yield (BAA); Federal Reserve Board, 10-year Treasury constant maturity (GS10), via FRED; the spread is our calculation. Chart inspired by Fig. 14.1 in Jones (2024), Macroeconomics, 6th ed.

How to read it: monthly yields in percent. The top line is the yield on BAA-rated corporate bonds (investment grade, but not risk-free), the middle line the 10-year Treasury yield, and the bottom line their difference, the spread, in percentage points. The hover box shows all three for one month. Click a line in the legend to hide it.

Do live:

  1. Hover over 2006, then over December 2008: the spread jumps to about 6 points.
  2. Hide the spread and look at the two yields in 2007–2008: they move in opposite directions.
  3. Point at the smaller spikes: 2002 after the dot-com crash, early 2016 with the oil-price collapse, spring 2020 with Covid-19.

The euro-area version of this chart is in the book chapter: government bond spreads over Germany, which exploded in 2010–2012 (Greece 27.4 points in February 2012). Chapter 20 returns to that crisis.

The spread is the financial friction, and in 2008 it tripled

  • From 1996 to 2007 the spread averaged 2.16 points. In December 2008 it reached 6.01, about three times its usual size.
  • From 2006 to 2008 the Treasury yield fell from 4.79% to 3.67%, while the BAA yield rose from 6.48% to 7.45%.
  • Investors fled to safety: they bought Treasuries and sold the bonds of firms that might not repay.

The Fed’s cuts reached the government, not the companies that invest.

Numbers: the notebook’s Fig. 1 key numbers (annual averages for 2006 and 2008; monthly peak of the spread).

The spread is our measure of the risk premium f̄ in the next act: the extra interest a lender asks a risky borrower to pay. In calm times it is small and stable; in a crisis it jumps because lenders fear default and because they want safe, liquid assets.

Contrast with Chapter 10, which used the TED spread, a 3-month rate between banks. This one is a 10-year rate for non-financial companies, closer to the rate that matters for building a factory.

Ask: why does the Treasury yield fall in a panic? (Demand for the safest asset rises, so its price rises and its yield falls.)

A zero policy rate does not mean cheap credit

“The Fed’s rate was zero, so money was cheap for everyone.”

Firms pay the policy rate plus a risk premium. In 2008 the premium rose by more than the Treasury yield fell, so the rate on corporate bonds went up, from 6.48% to 7.45%.

A common mistake in the news and among students: equating the central bank’s rate with “the” interest rate.

The policy rate is an overnight rate between banks. Households and firms borrow for years, from lenders who can lose money. Their rate is the policy rate, plus a term premium for lending long, plus a risk premium for default. In a financial crisis the last term can swamp the first.

For the policy students: when a minister says “rates are at record lows”, ask “which rates, and for whom?”

Loose or tight?

After 2008 the Fed’s rate sat at zero for seven years. By the standard of a Taylor rule, was that policy too loose, too tight, or about right?

Remind the room of the Taylor rule from Chapter 13: the rate rises with inflation above target and with output above potential. Ask students to guess what the rule recommended in 2009, with output far below potential and inflation low.

Before 2008 the Fed sat below its rule; after 2008 it was stuck at zero

Data: Federal Reserve Board (FEDFUNDS); BEA (PCEPILFE, GDPC1); CBO (GDPPOT); NBER (USREC), via FRED; the rule is our calculation. Chart inspired by Fig. 14.4 in Jones (2024), Macroeconomics, 6th ed.

How to read it: quarterly, in percent. One line is the actual fed funds rate, the other the rate a Taylor rule recommends: i = r̄ + π + 0.5(π − 2) + 0.5Ỹ, with core PCE inflation over four quarters and short-run output measured against the CBO’s potential GDP. With r̄ = 2 this is i = 1% + 1.5π + 0.5Ỹ. Shaded bands are NBER recessions. The slider sets r̄, the long-run real rate in the rule; the title reports the average gap between the rule and the actual rate in 2002–2006.

Do live:

  1. Start at r̄ = 2 (the traditional value): from 2002 to 2006 the rule sits above the actual rate by 1.4 points on average.
  2. Move r̄ to 1, the value many economists now prefer: the rule drops by one point in every quarter, and the 2002–2006 gap falls to 0.4 points.
  3. Point at 2021–2022: with core inflation near or above 5 percent, even the lower rule called for rates far above the actual ones.

The inputs of the rule (a deep slump after 2008, low inflation until 2021) are drawn in the book chapter; data revisions have raised short-run output by 1 to 2 points since 2013.

By the rule, the Fed looked loose both before 2006 and after 2011

  • In 2009 the rule itself was near or below zero (between −0.48% and 0.95% across the four quarters). From 2011 to 2015 it called for at least 1.05%, while the actual rate never rose above 0.19%.
  • But firms did not borrow at the fed funds rate: with the spread of the last chart, a zero rate was not generous.
  • From 2002 to 2006 the actual rate was 1.4 points below the rule, a possible fuel for the housing boom.

A rule is a benchmark, not a verdict: it ignores the risk premium, and it moves one for one with \(\bar{r}\).

Numbers: the notebook’s Taylor-rule key numbers and the Fig. 4 slider titles. The highest rule rate in the sample, 9.84 percent in January 2022, came when the fed funds rate was 0.12 percent: the inflation surge of Chapter 10 caught the Fed far behind its rule.

Beyond the U.S. (Fig. 4c in the book chapter): in 2022 all 14 central banks were far below their rules, by 6.8 points in the U.S., 6.5 in the euro area and 13.8 in the U.K.; in 2002–2006 the euro area was also below its rule, by 1.4 points a year on average. “Too low for too long” was not only an American story.

The debate on 2002–2006 returns in Act III (should central banks lean against bubbles?).

Why inflation did not keep falling after 2008, although output was far below potential, is the Devil’s advocate at the end.

Bubbles in the stock market

Stock prices are high today relative to company profits. How can we tell when prices have run too far ahead of what firms earn?

Ask: what is a share of a company worth? Let students arrive at “the profits it will pay its owners, now and in the future”. Then ask how we would compare prices across a century in which the economy grew many times over.

Prices ran far ahead of profits before the crashes of 1929 and 2000

Data: Robert J. Shiller, cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio (CAPE), shillerdata.com. Chart inspired by Fig. 14.7 in Jones (2024), Macroeconomics, 6th ed.

How to read it: Shiller’s price-earnings ratio, the real S&P stock price index divided by the average real earnings of the previous ten years (smoothing out the swings of profits over the cycle). Monthly since 1881. The dashed line is the average from the year on the slider to today.

Do live:

  1. Point at the two labelled peaks: September 1929 (32.6) and December 1999 (44.2). Both were followed by long falls.
  2. Move the slider from 1881 to 2001: the average rises from 17.8 to 27.6. Today’s ratio, 40.7, looks extreme against the long average and less so against the recent one.

Why only the U.S.: Shiller’s long series is free only for the U.S. market.

A high ratio has always come back down, but “normal” is hard to pin down

  • From September 1929 to June 1932 the ratio fell from 32.6 to 5.6: −83%.
  • From December 1999 to March 2009 it fell from 44.2 to 13.3: −70%.
  • In October 2026 it stands at 40.7. Against the average since 1881 (17.8) that is a bubble; against the average since 2001 (27.6), less clearly.

A bubble is easy to see afterwards and hard to prove in real time.

Numbers: the notebook’s “two stock market crashes” key numbers and the Fig. 7 slider titles.

Shiller used this ratio to warn of a bubble in the late 1990s, before the dot-com crash. But the ratio has stayed above its long-run average for most of the time since 1990. Either markets have been overvalued for thirty years, or something changed: lower real interest rates, a different mix of firms, different accounting of earnings. The next slide shows why a small change in the discount rate moves the “right” ratio a lot.

Home prices tell a similar story; Chapter 10 drew them (real prices fell 35.7 percent from 2005 to 2012). The book chapter adds 58 economies: Ireland −56.7 percent, Spain −44.5 percent.

Stock prices are very sensitive to the interest rate

\[\frac{P}{E} = \frac{1}{R-g}\]

  • Price \(P\) = present value of profits \(E\) growing at \(g\), discounted at \(R\).
  • With \(R = 7\%\) and \(g = 2\%\): \(P/E = 20\). One point more on \(R\): \(P/E = 16.7\), a fall of 17%.
  • When prices collapse, household wealth and spending fall: a lower \(\bar{a}\).

Asset prices link the financial system to demand: a premium that jumps also cuts wealth.

The derivation (in the book chapter’s Model overview): the price is a geometric series E/(1+R) + E(1+g)/(1+R)² + …, with ratio (1+g)/(1+R) < 1, which sums to E/(R − g). The notebook’s Check 6 adds 2,000 years of discounted profits and gets 20.00 both ways; with R = 8 percent, 16.67, a change of −17 percent.

The log derivative is −1/(R − g) = −20: one point of R lowers ln P by about 0.2 at the baseline. The exact fall for a full point is 17 percent because the slope flattens as R − g grows.

A ratio far above what plausible values of R − g can explain is the textbook’s sign of a bubble. The policy question, whether the central bank should try to deflate one, is in Act III.

Friction, deflation and the zero lower bound

Act II

Firms borrow at the Fed’s rate plus a risk premium

\[R_t = R^{ff}_t + \bar{f}\]

\[\widetilde{Y}_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R_t - \bar{r}) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \widetilde{Y}_t = \underset{\text{demand shock}}{(\bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{f})} - \bar{b}\,(R^{ff}_t - \bar{r})\]

Measured against the rate the Fed controls, a higher premium looks exactly like a fall in \(\bar{a}\).

The new assumption: the Fed sets the real fed funds rate R^ff, and lenders add a premium f̄ ≥ 0 for the risk of not being repaid. In calm times f̄ is close to zero (the textbook’s normalization); Fig. 1 measured it as the BAA spread.

Substitute the friction into the IS curve of Chapter 11 and split the bracket: Ỹ = ā − b̄(R^ff + f̄ − r̄) = (ā − b̄f̄) − b̄(R^ff − r̄).

Intuition: a firm does not care why its loan is expensive. A higher premium cuts investment just as a loss of confidence would. One point of premium costs b̄ points of output, exactly like a fall of b̄ in ā.

Baseline values used in every diagram (the book chapter’s illustrative set): r̄ = 2 percent, b̄ = 1, π̄ = 2 percent, m̄ = 1, ν̄ = 0.5. The 2008 shocks: the housing bust lowers demand by ā′ = −2, and the crisis adds f̄ = 4 points.

Experiment: a housing bust, a Fed cut, and then a financial crisis

The housing bust lowers demand by \(\bar{a}' = -2\). The Fed cuts \(R^{ff}\) from 2% to 0%, just enough to offset it. Then the crisis adds a premium \(\bar{f} = 4\) while the Fed stays at 0%. Where does output end: at potential, at \(-2\%\) (the Fed doing nothing), or worse?

Ask for a number. Students who use the equation of the last slide should get Ỹ = −2 − 1 × (0 + 4 − 2) = −4 percent.

A frequent wrong answer: “the Fed’s cut offsets the bust, so output is back at potential”. Ask: which rate do firms pay after the crisis?

A risk premium can undo the Fed’s cut, and more

Model figure: the IS/MP model with a financial friction, \(R = R^{ff} + \bar{f}\); baseline \(\bar{r} = 2\), \(\bar{b} = 1\), \(\bar{a}' = -2\). Adapted from Fig. 14.2 in Jones (2024), Macroeconomics, 6th ed.

How to read it: two IS/MP diagrams, output Ỹ across and the real interest rate R up. Panel (a): the housing bust shifts IS back to IS′; with no Fed action the economy moves from A to B; the Fed cuts to MP′ and reaches potential at C. Panel (b) adds the crisis: firms pay R = R^ff + f̄ (MP″), and the economy lands at D. The slider sets the friction f̄; the title computes B, the rate firms pay, and D.

Do live:

  1. Start at f̄ = 4: R = 4 percent, D at −4 percent.
  2. Move to 0: D and C coincide, the cut fully offsets the bust.
  3. Move to 2: D sits exactly at B, as if the Fed had done nothing.
  4. Move to 6: D at −6 percent.

Ask: does the size of the housing shock matter for D, once the Fed has cut to 0? (No: the cut removed it; D = −b̄f̄.)

The answer: output falls to −4%, twice the recession with no Fed at all

  • With \(\bar{f} = 0\) the cut to 0% brings the economy back to potential at \(C\).
  • With \(\bar{f} = 2\), the premium cancels the cut: \(D\) lands on \(B\), at −2%.
  • With \(\bar{f} = 4\), firms pay 4%, two points above \(\bar{r}\), and output falls to −4%.

Two shocks conspired: households spent less, and credit cost more.

Numbers: the notebook’s “quick check of the 2008 story”: B −2.0 percent, C 0.0 percent, D −4.0 percent.

After the cut that offsets the bust, what is left is −b̄f̄: every point of premium costs one point of output, whatever the size of the housing shock.

The textbook’s panel (b) only says “a deep recession at D”; the baseline numbers make it concrete.

To close the gap the Fed needed a real rate of −4%

\[R^{ff,*} = \bar{r} + \frac{\bar{a}}{\bar{b}} - \bar{f} = 2 + \frac{-2}{1} - 4 = -4\%\]

  • Set \(\widetilde{Y}_t = 0\) in the IS curve with the friction and solve for \(R^{ff}\).
  • The Fed must offset the demand shock and cut one extra point for every point of premium.
  • With 2% inflation, a real rate of −4% means a nominal rate of −2%: below zero.

Here the short-run model meets a floor. The Fed cannot set a negative nominal rate.

Derivation: 0 = ā − b̄f̄ − b̄(R^ff − r̄), so R^ff − r̄ = ā/b̄ − f̄. The notebook’s zero-lower-bound key numbers: the real fed funds rate needed is −4.0 percent, a nominal rate of −2.0 percent; the best the Fed can do is R^ff = −2 percent, so firms pay R = 2 percent and output is −2 percent, point B.

This is why the Fed turned to other tools (Act III). The floor itself is three slides ahead.

The friction enters the AD curve as one more demand shock

\[R_t - \bar{r} = \bar{f} + \bar{m}\,(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})\]

\[\widetilde{Y}_t = \underset{\text{AD shock}}{(\bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{f})} - \bar{b}\bar{m}\,(\pi_t - \bar{\pi}) \qquad\qquad \pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + \bar{\nu}\,\widetilde{Y}_t\]

The AD curve of Chapter 13 with \(\bar{a}\) replaced by \(\bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{f}\); the AS curve does not move at first.

Add the monetary policy rule of Chapter 13, R^ff − r̄ = m̄(π − π̄), to the friction equation: firms pay the premium on top of whatever the rule sets. Substitute into the IS curve: the new AD curve. It slopes down because higher inflation makes the Fed raise rates.

The AS curve is the Phillips curve with adaptive expectations, π^e = π_{t−1}. The economy sits where AD and AS cross; solving the two gives this year’s output: Ỹ_t = [ā − b̄f̄ − b̄m̄(π_{t−1} − π̄)] / (1 + b̄m̄ν̄).

With b̄ = m̄ = 1 and ν̄ = 0.5 the denominator is 1.5.

Experiment: the 2008 shocks in the AS/AD diagram

The housing bust and the premium together give an AD shock of \(\bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{f} = -6\). Inflation starts at the 2% target, and the Fed follows its rule. Predict output and inflation in the first year, and where inflation ends in the long run.

Students who use the solution on the last slide’s notes get Ỹ₁ = (−6 − 0)/1.5 = −4 percent and π₁ = 2 + 0.5 × (−4) = 0 percent.

For the long run, ask: in the long run inflation stops changing. What must output be then? (Zero.) Then read inflation off the AD curve at Ỹ = 0.

A deep recession, then inflation slides below zero

Model figure: the AS/AD model with a financial friction; \(\bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{f} = -6\), \(\bar{b} = \bar{m} = 1\), \(\bar{\nu} = 0.5\). Adapted from Fig. 14.3 in Jones (2024), Macroeconomics, 6th ed.

How to read it: output Ỹ across, inflation π up. AD is the old aggregate demand curve, AD′ the new one after the shocks. AS is this period’s aggregate supply curve, which shifts down each period because firms expect last period’s inflation. A is the start, B the first period, C the long run. D is where the economy would jump if the shocks suddenly ended: today’s AS curve meets the old AD curve. The slider sets the period t; the title reports output and inflation in that period.

Do live:

  1. Period 1: Ỹ = −4 percent, π = 0 percent, at B.
  2. Period 2: −2.7 percent and −1.3 percent. Period 4: −1.2 and −2.8.
  3. Period 12: output back at potential, inflation −4 percent, at C.

Point at how far D drifts from A as AS moves down: ending the crisis late would bring an inflationary boom on the way back.

The recession fades; the deflation stays

  • Year 1: output −4%, inflation 0%. Without the rule, the AD shock would cost 6% of output: falling inflation makes the Fed cut.
  • Then the AS curve shifts down each year, and the economy slides along AD′.
  • In the long run output is back at potential, but inflation is −4%: deflation.

Starting from only 2% inflation, a large enough shock ends in falling prices.

Numbers: the notebook’s Check 2: year 1 −4.00 percent and 0.000 percent; year 2 −2.67 and −1.333; year 3 −1.78 and −2.222; year 8 −0.23 and −3.766. Long-run inflation, equation (6): −4.00 percent.

The textbook’s figure ends at −2 percent inflation; with our baseline (m̄ = 1) it is −4. The book chapter’s “Your turn” asks which m̄ gives −2 (answer: m̄ = 1.5).

Deflation ends when the rule has cut the borrowing rate enough to offset both shocks. That only works if the rule can deliver the negative real rate it asks for, the hidden assumption of the AS/AD diagram.

Each year the economy closes one third of the remaining gap

\[\pi_C = \bar{\pi} + \frac{\bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{f}}{\bar{b}\bar{m}} = 2 + \frac{-6}{1} = -4\%\]

\[\pi_t - \pi_C = \left(\frac{1}{1 + \bar{b}\bar{m}\bar{\nu}}\right)^{t} (\pi_0 - \pi_C) = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{t} \times 6\]

A more aggressive rule (a larger \(\bar{m}\)) makes the first recession milder and the convergence faster.

Long run: inflation stops changing only when Ỹ = 0 (the AS curve). Put Ỹ = 0 into the AD curve and solve for π.

Speed: write x_t = π_t − π_C. Using the long-run equation, the solution for output becomes Ỹ_t = −b̄m̄x_{t−1}/(1 + b̄m̄ν̄), and the AS curve then gives x_t = x_{t−1}/(1 + b̄m̄ν̄). With 1 + b̄m̄ν̄ = 1.5, two thirds of the gap remain each year: π₁ = −4 + 6 × 2/3 = 0, π₂ = −4 + 6 × 4/9 ≈ −1.3. The notebook’s Check 2 confirms the closed form year by year to three decimals.

This is a stable difference equation: the factor is below one. Keep it in mind for the spiral, where the factor is above one.

The table is the notebook’s comparative statics, from the derivatives of the period-1 solution and of π_C: first-year output ∓1/1.5 = ∓0.67, first-year inflation ∓ν̄/1.5 = ∓0.33, long-run inflation ±1/(b̄m̄) or −1/m̄. The notebook’s Check 3 raises the premium by one point and gets −0.667, −0.333 and −1.000.

The friction moves only the AD curve. A recession that comes from a premium is temporary; the disinflation it causes is lasting unless policy or the target changes.

Ask: with inflation starting at 2 percent and m̄ = 1, how large must the AD shock be for long-run inflation to turn negative? (Worse than −2.)

At a zero nominal rate, the lowest real rate is minus inflation

\[R_t = i_t - \pi_t, \qquad i_t \geq 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_t \geq \bar{f} - \pi_t\]

\[{\widetilde{Y}}^{\max}_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\bar{f} + \bar{b}\,(\bar{r} + \pi_t)\]

The zero lower bound caps the output the Fed can reach, and the cap falls with inflation.

The Fisher equation of Chapter 12, rearranged for the real rate: R = i − π. (The textbook’s display of the rearranged equation prints a plus sign; it is a typo, and the text around it uses the minus.)

The zero lower bound: a nominal rate cannot go much below zero, because savers can always hold cash, which pays exactly zero. Several central banks set slightly negative rates in the late 2010s, by fractions of a point, so the floor is a little below zero, but it exists.

At the bound the real fed funds rate is −π, and firms pay f̄ − π. Put that into the IS curve: the best output the Fed can reach. The bound binds when the rate the Fed needs is below the floor, which is the same as Ỹmax < 0.

A liquidity trap: at a zero rate, money and short-term government bonds both pay zero, so swapping one for the other (an ordinary open-market operation) changes nothing.

In 2008, with 2% inflation, even a zero rate left output at \(-2\%\); with 2% deflation it would leave \(-6\%\).

Numbers: the notebook’s Check 4, which computes the best output both from the formula and step by step (zero nominal rate → Fisher equation → friction → IS curve): −2.0, −4.0 and −6.0 percent.

The same check shows the policy rule of our AS/AD baseline, i = 2π, asking for i = +4, 0 and −4 percent at these inflation rates: as soon as inflation turns negative, the rule asks for a negative nominal rate. In the path of the last experiment that happens from period 2 on, so the AS/AD figure quietly assumed something the Fed could not do. That is why the textbook switches back to the IS/MP diagram for deflation.

Each point of deflation costs b̄ = 1 point of reachable output.

Falling prices are not good news when rates are stuck

“Deflation is good for consumers: everything gets cheaper.”

With the nominal rate stuck at zero, each point of deflation adds a point to the real interest rate. Debts must be repaid in dollars that are worth more, and investment falls: at 2% deflation, the best the Fed can reach is −6% output, not −2%.

Deflation that comes from faster productivity growth (cheaper computers) is not the problem. The danger is a general fall in prices in a slump, when the nominal rate cannot fall with it.

The textbook’s example: repaying a 100-dollar loan in a year with dollars that buy more than when you borrowed. A borrower’s real burden rises; borrowers spend less, and lenders do not spend the gain one for one.

Ask the policy students: why do central banks aim for 2 percent inflation rather than zero? (A buffer above the floor, so the real rate can go negative in a slump; and measured inflation overstates true inflation a little.)

A frozen nominal rate turns a stable model into a spiral

\[\widetilde{Y}_t = (1 + \bar{b}\bar{\nu})\,\widetilde{Y}_{t-1}\]

When inflation falls … the real rate each year the gap is multiplied by
the Fed follows its rule (\(\bar{m} = 1\)) falls \(1/(1 + \bar{b}\bar{m}\bar{\nu}) = 0.67\)
the nominal rate is frozen rises \(1 + \bar{b}\bar{\nu} =\) 1.5

A slump of −6% becomes −9%, then −13.5%: each year half as deep again.

Derivation: with the nominal rate stuck at ī and adaptive expectations, firms pay R_t = ī − π_{t−1} + f̄. The IS curve becomes Ỹ_t = ā − b̄(ī + f̄ − r̄) + b̄π_{t−1}. Write it one year earlier and subtract: every barred term cancels, and Ỹ_t − Ỹ_{t−1} = b̄(π_{t−1} − π_{t−2}) = b̄ν̄Ỹ_{t−1} by the Phillips curve.

Numbers: the notebook’s Check 5 freezes the nominal rate at the 1929 level (5 percent) and starts from the crash: −6.00, −9.00, −13.50, −20.25 percent, a ratio of 1.50 each year.

There is no resting point except Ỹ = 0, and the economy moves away from it. A frozen nominal rate behaves like a policy rule with a negative m̄. This is the deflationary spiral; the next figure applies it to the 1930s.

Experiment: the Great Depression, one shock at a time

Late 1920s: output at potential, inflation about zero. First the Fed raises the interest rate by 3 points. Then the stock market crash lowers demand by \(\bar{a}' = -3\). Then prices start to fall, and the Fed keeps the nominal rate where it is. Predict output after each of the three steps.

The textbook’s worked exercise. With b̄ = 1, each of the first two steps costs 3 points of output: students should get −3 and −6. The third step needs the Phillips curve: at −6 percent, inflation falls by 0.5 × 6 = 3 points, so the real rate rises by 3 points if the nominal rate is fixed.

Ask: why would a central bank raise rates in 1928? (It worried about a stock market bubble; Act I showed the 1929 peak of the price-earnings ratio.)

Each step of the 1930s cost another 3 points of output

Model figure: the IS/MP model with the Fisher equation, \(R = i - \pi\); a 3-point rate hike, \(\bar{a}' = -3\), \(\bar{\nu} = 0.5\). Adapted from Fig. 14.12 in Jones (2024), Macroeconomics, 6th ed.

How to read it: output Ỹ across, the real interest rate R up. IS slopes down; each MP line is the real rate the economy faces. The slider walks through the steps: 0 = A, 1 = B, 2 = C, 3 = D. The title reports output, the real rate and inflation at each point.

Do live:

  1. Step 0, A: the late 1920s, Ỹ = 0, R = 2 percent.
  2. Step 1, B: the Fed raises rates, MP → MP′, R = 5 percent, Ỹ = −3 percent.
  3. Step 2, C: the crash shifts IS back to IS′, Ỹ = −6 percent at the same R.
  4. Step 3, D: deflation of 3 percent with a fixed nominal rate raises R to 8 percent (MP″), Ỹ = −9 percent.

The answer: −3%, −6% and −9%, and the last step feeds itself

  • \(B\): the rate hike raises \(R\) from 2% to 5%: output −3%.
  • \(C\): the crash shifts the IS curve back: output −6%, at the same real rate.
  • \(D\): prices fall 3%; with \(i\) fixed, \(R = i - \pi\) rises to 8%: output −9%.

At \(D\) the slump is deeper, so prices fall faster, so the real rate rises again: the spiral.

Numbers: the notebook’s depression steps (and the slider titles): A (0, 2, 0), B (−3, 5, 0), C (−6, 5, 0), D (−9, 8, −3) for (Ỹ, R, π).

Nothing in the model stops the spiral unless policy changes. In the book chapter’s notebook, setting ν̄ = 0 (prices that never react) puts D on top of C: the spiral needs prices that fall in a slump.

History: the economy turned around after 1933, when the Fed eased and the U.S. left the gold standard. Chapter 20 returns to the gold standard.

Deflation in the 1930s

Every point \(B\), \(C\) and \(D\) lies below potential. With \(\bar{\nu} = 0.5\), by how much does inflation fall each year at each point?

Students apply Δπ = ν̄Ỹ: −1.5, −3 and −4.5 points. Ask: inflation was about zero in the late 1920s; what does a fall of 3 points mean for the price level? (Prices fall: deflation.)

The deeper the slump, the faster prices fall

Model figure: the Phillips curve \(\Delta\pi = \bar{\nu}\widetilde{Y}\) with \(\bar{\nu} = 0.5\), at the points of the Great Depression exercise. Adapted from Fig. 14.13 in Jones (2024), Macroeconomics, 6th ed.

How to read it: output Ỹ across, the change in inflation Δπ up. The Phillips curve goes through the origin. The dots are the points A to D of the last figure; the slider walks through them. Below the dotted horizontal line, next year’s inflation is lower than this year’s.

Do live: step through 0 to 3 and read Δπ in the title: 0, −1.5, −3 and −4.5 points.

Recession lowers inflation, deflation deepens the recession

  • The fall in inflation is −1.5 points a year at \(B\), −3 at \(C\) and −4.5 at \(D\).
  • The fall at \(C\) is what raises the real rate from \(C\) to \(D\); the larger fall at \(D\) would raise it again.
  • In the 1930s prices fell by about a quarter between 1929 and 1933.

A steeper Phillips curve makes deflation, and so the spiral, faster.

Numbers: the notebook’s d_pi column of the depression steps; “about a quarter” is the book chapter’s figure for the fall in prices from 1929 to 1933.

The two diagrams tell one story: the IS/MP diagram turns a real rate into output, and the Phillips curve turns output into the next change in inflation, which, through the Fisher equation with a fixed nominal rate, becomes the next real rate.

Friedman and Schwartz (1963) blamed the Depression on a Fed that let the money supply collapse. Act III checks whether the Fed repeated that mistake after 2008.

Did the Fed repeat the 1930s?

In the Great Depression the U.S. money supply shrank. After 2008, did money shrink, stall, or grow fast?

Remind the room: currency is notes and coins; M1 adds checking accounts; M2 adds savings accounts and small time deposits (Chapter 8). Ask for a direction and a rough size, and a reason.

Bernanke, then a Fed governor, told Friedman and Schwartz at a 2002 celebration that the Fed had caused the Depression and would not do it again. This chart is the test.

Money did not collapse: it grew faster in the crisis

  • Between October and December 2008, M1 growth rose from about 7% to about 17%; all three measures stayed high for most of 2009.
  • In 2020–21 M2 grew by up to 27%; from late 2022 to early 2024 it fell, for the first time since 1959.
  • Abroad, broad money stopped growing in 2009 in the euro area (−0.3%) and the U.K. (−0.1%).

The Fed passed Friedman and Schwartz’s test. Money growth alone did not end the slump.

Numbers: the notebook’s Fig. 6 “What to notice” and the world broad-money chart (Fig. 6b in the book chapter): of the 160 economies with data for 2009, 13 saw broad money shrink. China’s grew 28 percent with its stimulus.

A single year of fast money growth does not mean inflation is coming. At a zero interest rate, people and banks are happy to hold money that pays nothing, so velocity falls (Chapter 8). The quantity theory’s link from money to prices loosens.

Data hygiene from the world chart: Sierra Leone shows +131,119 percent in 2001; that is a break in how the stock was recorded, not money creation. Always check a surprising growth rate against inflation.

With its rate at zero, what else could the Fed do?

The fed funds rate cannot go below zero. Which other assets could the Fed buy, and how would that lower the rates firms pay?

Let students name candidates: long-term government bonds, mortgage-backed securities, loans to banks, commercial paper. Ask how buying a bond changes its yield (a higher price is a lower yield).

The identity: Treasuries + MBS + other assets = currency + reserves + other liabilities. When the Fed buys a bond from a dealer, it credits the dealer’s bank with reserves: both sides grow by the same amount, and no currency is printed. Unusually, banks kept those reserves at the Fed (which since 2008 pays interest on them) instead of lending them out.

The book chapter’s “Your turn” asks for May 2022, near the peak.

Quantitative easing is not the same as printing money

“QE means the Fed printed trillions of dollars, so high inflation had to follow.”

The Fed paid mostly by crediting banks’ reserve accounts: currency paid for only 15.3% of the 2007–2015 expansion. And inflation stayed low until 2021, more than a decade after QE began.

The mistake mixes up two liabilities of the Fed. Currency circulates and is spent; reserves are deposits of banks at the Fed. At a zero interest rate and with interest paid on reserves, banks were willing to hold them.

Inflation did rise in 2021–2022 (Chapter 10), but economists still debate how much of it came from the 2020 QE, from fiscal transfers, from supply bottlenecks and from energy prices. The 2009–2015 rounds came with low inflation.

Ask: if QE is not printing money, how is it supposed to work? (Lower long-term and mortgage rates: the next slides.)

Forward guidance: promises about future rates move today’s long rates

\[i^{L}_t \approx \frac{i_t + i^e_{t+1} + \dots + i^e_{t+n-1}}{n}\]

  • A 4-year bond when markets expect short rates of 0, 1, 2 and 3%: a long rate of 1.5%.
  • A credible promise of zero for three years (0, 0, 0, 2): a long rate of 0.5%.

Even at a zero short rate, the Fed can lower long rates by shaping expectations.

The term structure of Chapter 12: a long rate is roughly the average of the short rates expected over its life (plus a term premium, ignored here). Firms borrow for years, so long rates matter for investment.

Numbers: the notebook’s Check 6 computes 1.50 percent and 0.50 percent.

In January 2012 the Fed said its rate would likely stay exceptionally low at least through late 2014. Whether markets believe such a promise depends on the central bank’s credibility: a promise to keep rates low after the economy recovers is a promise to allow some inflation later.

  • After 2008 the old estimate kept its trend; the new one bent down to meet actual GDP.

Two implications. For policy: the Taylor rule of Act I uses the latest estimate; with the 2007 estimate, the gap would have been much larger and the rule lower. For theory: a financial crisis may lower the long-run path, through lower investment and slower productivity, a link between the short run and the long run that research is still working out.

Beyond the U.S. (Fig. 5b in the book chapter, OECD data against the 1997–2007 trend extended): in 2019 U.S. GDP was 14.2 percent below that trend, the euro area’s 16.9, Spain’s 30.9 and Greece’s 52.4. The U.S. did not lose more than others. An extrapolated trend is a counterfactual, not a fact: for boom economies such as Latvia or Ireland it gives absurd paths.

Did quantitative easing and forward guidance work?

The Fed bought trillions of dollars of bonds and promised low rates for years. Did it lower borrowing costs and raise output, and by how much?

What the model says

  • QE and guidance lower long and mortgage rates.
  • That raises demand even at a zero short rate.
  • The emergency lending of 2008 lowered \(\bar{f}\).

What the evidence says

  • Announcements lowered long yields: clear.
  • Effects on output and jobs: smaller, debated.
  • Later rounds did less than the first.

The left column is Act II: QE works on the long-term and mortgage rates (the present value and term-structure equations), guidance on expected future short rates; both lower the rates firms pay beyond R^ff.

The evidence. Gagnon, Raskin, Remache and Sack (2011, International Journal of Central Banking) find that the first round of purchases lowered the 10-year term premium substantially, by tens of basis points, mostly on announcement days. Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen (2011, Brookings Papers on Economic Activity) find large effects on the yields of the assets bought (mortgage-backed securities in QE1), smaller ones elsewhere. Swanson (2021, Journal of Monetary Economics) separates forward guidance from asset purchases in announcement data and finds that both moved yields, guidance mostly at short maturities. Engen, Laubach and Reifschneider (2015, Federal Reserve FEDS working paper) estimate, in a macro model, that the programs together lowered unemployment by about 1.25 points at their peak in 2016, the “larger estimate” the textbook mentions. Greenlaw, Hamilton, Harris and West (2018, U.S. Monetary Policy Forum) are skeptical: many announcement effects reversed, and the measured effects are small. The size of the effects on output is contested; that QE1 in a panic mattered is widely accepted.

Ask the policy students: QE leaves the central bank holding trillions of long-term bonds. What happens to its profits, and to the Treasury, when rates rise? (Since 2022 the Fed pays more interest on reserves than it earns on its bonds.)

Should the government spend more when rates are stuck at zero?

In February 2009 Congress passed a large stimulus (the Recovery Act). At the zero lower bound, how much output does each dollar of government spending buy?

What the model says

  • Spending raises \(\bar{a}\); at the bound rates do not rise to offset it.
  • To close the gap needs \(\Delta\bar{a} = -{\widetilde{Y}}^{\max}\): 2% of output in 2008.
  • With 2% deflation it needs 6%: waiting is costly.

What the evidence says

  • Some models: multipliers well above 1 at the bound.
  • Historical data: near 1 or below, even at zero rates.
  • Future taxes may offset part of it.

The left column is the zero-lower-bound block of Act II: government purchases are part of ā, and at the bound the Fed does not raise the rate when ā rises, so reachable output rises one for one. In the 2008 example, closing the gap needs Δā = 2 percent of potential output with 2 percent inflation, but 6 percent once deflation of 2 percent has set in.

The evidence. Christiano, Eichenbaum and Rebelo (2011, Journal of Political Economy) show that in a New Keynesian model the spending multiplier can be well above 1 when the zero bound binds, because spending also stops the fall in expected inflation. Ramey and Zubairy (2018, Journal of Political Economy), with U.S. data back to 1889, find multipliers mostly below 1, with no robust evidence that they are higher near the zero bound. Chodorow-Reich (2019, American Economic Journal: Economic Policy) surveys cross-state evidence, including the Recovery Act, and finds local multipliers around 1.8, which do not translate one for one into a national multiplier. Ramey (2019, Journal of Economic Perspectives) reviews the debate. The size of the multiplier at the bound is contested.

Ricardian equivalence (Chapter 11): spending financed by future taxes may lead households to save more today. Chapter 18 returns to fiscal policy and debt.

Ask: which would you choose at the bound, tax cuts or spending, and why?

Should central banks raise rates to prick asset bubbles?

The Fed raised rates in 1928 to cool the stock market, and kept rates below its rule in 2002–06 as home prices soared. Should monetary policy lean against bubbles?

The case for leaning

  • Bursting bubbles cut wealth and \(\bar{a}\) (Act I).
  • Low rates in 2002–06 may have fed the boom.
  • Credit-fuelled bubbles end in the worst slumps.

The case against

  • Bubbles are hard to spot in real time.
  • A rate hike hits the whole economy: 1928.
  • Capital rules and lending limits aim better.

The left column. Act I: the price-earnings ratio peaked before the 1929 and 2000 crashes; a small change in R − g moves prices a lot, and a collapse lowers ā. Fig. 4: from 2002 to 2006 the fed funds rate was 1.4 points below the Taylor rule (r̄ = 2). Taylor (2007, Jackson Hole symposium of the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City) argues that these low rates fuelled the housing boom. Jordà, Schularick and Taylor (2015, Journal of International Economics) show with 140 years of data that bubbles financed by credit are followed by much deeper recessions than equity bubbles without leverage.

The right column. Bernanke and Gertler (2001, American Economic Review Papers and Proceedings) argue that central banks should respond to asset prices only through their effect on the inflation and output forecast. Bernanke (2010, speech to the American Economic Association) argues that the Fed’s rates in 2002–2006 explain little of the boom, which happened in countries with different policies. Svensson (2017, Journal of Monetary Economics) estimates that the costs of leaning (a weaker economy now) exceed the benefits (a smaller chance of a crisis later). The 1920s are the cautionary tale of the model: the 1928 hike was step 1 of the Great Depression exercise.

The alternative is “macroprudential” policy: capital requirements and limits on loan-to-value ratios aimed at the credit that feeds the bubble (Chapter 10’s policy slide on capital). The question is contested; most central banks today lean on macroprudential tools first.

Ask the policy students: who should decide that a market is in a bubble, the central bank, a financial-stability council, or nobody?

“The model’s spiral never came”

A skeptic says: “After 2008 output stayed far below potential for years, yet U.S. inflation barely fell and never became deflation. The model’s deflationary spiral did not happen, so the model is wrong.”

Reply: the spiral assumes that people expect last year’s inflation. With a credible 2% target, expectations stayed near 2%, so the real rate did not climb. That is the model’s own way out: anchored expectations keep \(\pi\) up in \({\widetilde{Y}}^{\max}\). Why inflation fell so little is a genuine puzzle, but it is an argument for credibility, not against the model.

Take the objection seriously: it is the “missing disinflation” after 2008. Fig. 4 showed core inflation low after 2008 but not falling much, though short-run output was far below potential; a Phillips curve with adaptive expectations (Chapter 12) predicts much lower inflation.

Explanations, all debated. Hall (2011, American Economic Review, “The Long Slump”) argues that inflation behaves as if it were nearly unresponsive to slack, a near-exogenous force. Ball and Mazumder (2011, Brookings Papers on Economic Activity) find that the Phillips curve became flatter and that anchored expectations held inflation up. Coibion and Gorodnichenko (2015, American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics) argue that households’ and firms’ inflation expectations rose with oil prices in 2009–2011, which explains the missing disinflation. The textbook’s own reading: the Fed’s credibility in managing expectations.

The reply also shows why the model is useful: it tells policymakers what to protect. Japan in the 1990s and 2000s is the counterexample where expectations did slip and mild deflation lasted for years.

Ask: what could a central bank do to keep expected inflation from falling? (Announce and defend a target; forward guidance; some economists propose a higher inflation target, to stay farther from the floor.)

What to remember from this chapter

  • Firms pay \(R = R^{ff} + \bar{f}\). In 2008 the spread tripled, so the Fed’s cuts did not lower borrowing costs.
  • A higher premium works like a fall in demand: a recession first, and lower inflation for good.
  • At the zero lower bound \(R = i - \pi\) cannot fall far enough; deflation raises it and can start a spiral.
  • The Fed used money, QE and forward guidance; the gap closed partly because potential was revised down.
  • Policy debates: how much QE and stimulus buy, and whether to lean against bubbles.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: why did the 2008 crisis cause such a deep slump although the Fed cut its rate to zero? A good answer: the crisis raised the risk premium, so firms’ borrowing rates rose; to offset it the Fed needed a negative real rate below what a zero nominal rate allows, and falling inflation made the gap worse.

For the policy students, three habits: ask which interest rate a policy moves, and for whom; check whether the zero lower bound binds; and judge a recovery against more than one estimate of potential.
