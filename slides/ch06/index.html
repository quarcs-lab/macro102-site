A vocabulary of 20,000 words holds an almost endless supply of new texts

10430 The number of different 100-word paragraphs that can be written with 20,000 words

Machines run into diminishing returns, and raw materials are finite. Recipes are neither. Can new recipes keep income growing?

Open with the number. It comes from the notebook’s “Model in code” section: with 20,000 words, there are 20,000^100 possible strings of 100 words, which is 10 to the power 100 × log10(20,000), about 10^430. Most of them are nonsense, of course, but even a tiny fraction of useful ones is a vast store.

The same logic holds for chemicals, genes, machine parts or lines of code: combining a finite set of objects in new ways gives an enormous number of possible recipes.

Link back to Chapter 5: the Solow model showed that piling up machines cannot keep income per person growing, because each machine adds less than the last. Today’s question is whether ideas, the recipes for using objects, can do what machines could not.

Today: three questions

  • Why ideas are different. What makes a recipe unlike a machine, and why can’t perfect competition pay for it?
  • The Romer model. How do ideas produce sustained growth, and what changes its level rather than its rate?
  • Ideas in the data. Have raw materials run out? Is growth slowing down, and why?

These are the three acts of the deck and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide. Act I builds the economics of ideas from one property, nonrivalry. Act II puts that property into a growth model and adds back the capital of Chapter 5. Act III takes the theory to a century of commodity prices and to the growth of the United States and other countries since 1960.

The headline result: growth in income per person comes from growth in the stock of ideas, and that comes from the number of people looking for them.

Why ideas are different

Act I

Objects are rival; ideas are nonrival

Objects

  • Land, oil, machines, buildings, workers.
  • Rival: if one factory uses a machine, another cannot use it at the same time.
  • Scarcity comes from rivalry.

Ideas

  • Designs, formulas, recipes, computer code.
  • Nonrival: a second factory can follow a design while the first still has all of it.
  • Ideas are instructions for using objects.

Nonrival is not the same as free: a patent can make an idea excludable, but it does not make it rival.

Romer (1990) sorted all goods into these two groups. Ask for examples of each: a phone is an object; its design is an idea. A dose of a drug is an object; its chemical formula is an idea. Management methods count as ideas too, such as just-in-time inventories or a checklist that cuts hospital infections.

Excludability is the legal or technical ability to stop others from using a good. Patents, copyrights and trade secrets give it to some ideas for a while. Rivalry is a physical property; excludability is an institution. Keep the two apart: much of the policy debate in this chapter is about excludability.

The production function of Chapters 4 and 5 now has a time subscript on productivity: Y = A_t K^(1/3) L^(2/3), where A_t is the stock of ideas, which keeps growing.

Nonrivalry alone implies increasing returns

\[Y_t = A_tK_t^{1/3}L_t^{2/3} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad (\lambda A)(\lambda K)^{1/3}(\lambda L)^{2/3} = \lambda^{2}\,Y\]

Multiply … by Output is multiplied by
capital and labor only 2 2
the stock of ideas only 2 2
objects and ideas 2 4
objects and ideas 3 9

To double output, copy the factory and its workers; the blueprint does not need copying. Doubling the blueprints too must more than double output.

This is the replication argument. A second identical factory needs its own machines and its own workers, but not its own design: both factories follow the same one. So there are constant returns to objects (the exponents 1/3 and 2/3 add to one).

Once ideas count as an input, scaling every input by λ multiplies output by λ²: doubling everything quadruples output, tripling it gives nine times as much. The notebook checks the table with numbers (2.00, 4.00, 9.00).

Increasing returns are not an extra assumption here. They follow from nonrivalry. The next slides show the same thing through a fixed cost, which is how firms experience it.

A new antibiotic: research first, then cheap doses

Discovering a new antibiotic costs $2.5 billion. After that, each dose costs $10 to make. If a firm doubles its total spending from $3.6 billion to $7.2 billion, how many times more doses does it make?

Let students compute before the reveal. A common first answer is “twice as many”, which would be right without the research cost.

The calculation: with spending X and research cost F̄, the firm makes (X − F̄)/10 doses. At 3.6 billion: 1.1 billion dollars of production, 110 million doses. At 7.2 billion: 4.7 billion dollars, 470 million doses. That is more than four times as many.

The increasing returns come entirely from the fixed cost of the idea.

The numbers are the slider titles and the annotation of Fig. 1.

The formula: total spending X buys (X − F̄)/c doses. Divide by X to get the average product. Without the fixed cost it is 1/c, a constant. With it, the term F̄/X shrinks as the firm grows, so each extra billion buys more doses than the last.

Equivalently, average cost per dose, c + F̄/Y, falls with scale. This is why bigger markets make more ideas worth inventing, a point that comes back in the software example and when we ask why the Romer model is about the world’s ideas.

If the price equals marginal cost, nobody pays for the research

\[\pi = (p - c)\,Y - \bar{F} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p = c:\ \ \pi = -\bar{F}\]

\[\text{break even:}\quad p = c + \frac{\bar{F}}{Y}\]

  • Spending $5.0 billion makes 250 million doses. Sold at $10, they bring in $2.5 billion: a loss of exactly the $2.5 billion research cost.
  • At 470 million doses, the firm needs at least \(10 + 2{,}500/470 \approx\) $15.32 a dose.

Perfect competition drives prices to marginal cost, so it cannot reward invention. Some wedge between price and cost is needed.

The numbers are the notebook’s key numbers and Check 1.

Walk through the timing. After the drug exists, selling at marginal cost looks fine: each dose covers its own cost. But before the research is done, a firm that expects to sell at marginal cost will never spend the 2.5 billion. So the price has to exceed marginal cost at some point, or the research must be paid for some other way.

This is where Adam Smith’s invisible hand runs into trouble. With increasing returns, the competitive price cannot cover all costs.

The wedge has a cost too: a price above marginal cost keeps out buyers who would pay more than the cost of making one more dose, but less than the price. One price cannot both reward invention and spread an existing idea as widely as possible.

Software: a huge fixed cost, almost no cost per copy

A word processor with perfect speech recognition costs $100 million to write, and each copy costs $1 to distribute. How many copies must the firm sell to break even at $20 a copy? At $2?

This is a worked exercise in the textbook. Ask students to set up the break-even condition first: revenue p·Y must cover the fixed cost plus the cost of the copies, 100 + Y (in millions). So Y = 100/(p − 1).

Most students get the $20 case quickly. The $2 case is the point: at a low price, the firm needs a huge market.

Ask: open-source software such as Linux is sold at a price of zero. Who pays for it? (Volunteers, companies that use it, people building a reputation. Profit is not the only motive for creating ideas.)

Paying for ideas: patents, public money or prizes?

Research must be paid for somehow, and a price at marginal cost cannot do it. Which institutions should?

Patents and copyrights

  • A price above cost rewards the inventor.
  • It also prices out buyers who would pay the cost of a copy.
  • Their effect on follow-on research is disputed.

Public money and prizes

  • Public research funding leads to private patents.
  • Prizes and purchase commitments pay for results.
  • But someone must pick the targets, and taxes pay.

Left column. Patents give a temporary monopoly in exchange for publishing the idea. Williams (2013, Journal of Political Economy) finds that Celera’s intellectual property on human genes was followed by less later research and fewer products on those genes. Sampat and Williams (2019, American Economic Review) find that gene patents themselves had no clear effect on follow-on innovation. Budish, Roin and Williams (2015, American Economic Review) show that fixed patent lengths tilt cancer research toward drugs that can be tested quickly. Moser (2005, American Economic Review) finds that countries without patent laws in the nineteenth century invented as much, but in industries where secrecy worked.

Right column. Azoulay, Graff Zivin, Li and Sampat (2019, Review of Economic Studies) use quirks in how the U.S. National Institutes of Health allocates grants and find that public biomedical funding produces substantial private-sector patenting. Kremer (1998, Quarterly Journal of Economics) proposes that governments buy out patents and put the ideas in the public domain. Kremer, Levin and Snyder (2020, AEA Papers and Proceedings) review advance market commitments, such as the one for pneumococcal vaccines in poor countries.

The trade-off from the model: any wedge between price and marginal cost rewards invention but limits use; public funding avoids the wedge but needs taxes and someone to choose the research. Ask the policy students: which mix would you choose for vaccines, for software, for basic science?

The Romer model

Act II

The Romer model: goods, ideas and people

What the model assumes Equation Parameter
Workers make goods with all the ideas \(Y_t = A_t^{\gamma}L_{yt}\) \(\gamma\)
Researchers make new ideas \(\Delta A_{t+1} = \bar{z}L_{at}\) \(\bar{z}\)
Everyone makes goods or ideas \(L_{yt} + L_{at} = N_t\)
Population grows \(\Delta N_{t+1}/N_t = \bar{n}\) \(\bar{n}\)
A fixed share does research \(L_{at} = \bar{\ell}N_t\) \(\bar{\ell}\)

No capital at all: the model isolates ideas. Capital comes back at the end of the act.

Five equations, five unknowns in every year: output Y, the stock of ideas A, goods workers L_y, researchers L_a and population N.

The parameters: γ is the degree of increasing returns, how much ideas matter in production (scaling ideas and workers by λ multiplies output by λ^(1+γ)). z̄ is research productivity, new ideas per researcher per year. n̄ is population growth, and ℓ̄ is the research share.

The stock of ideas is like a library that is never emptied: each year researchers add z̄ L_a new blueprints, and nothing is lost.

The research share is a rule of thumb, like the investment rate s̄ in the Solow model. In Romer’s original paper, patents and monopoly profits decide how many people do research, and markets tend to choose too few.

Income depends on the total stock of ideas, not ideas per person

\[y_t = \frac{Y_t}{N_t} = A_t^{\gamma}\,(1-\bar{\ell}) \qquad\qquad A_t^* = \frac{\bar{z}\bar{\ell}N_t}{\bar{n}}\]

  • One new idea raises the productivity of every worker at once: that is nonrivalry at work.
  • Compare Solow: \(y = \bar{A}k^{1/3}\) depends on capital per person; a new machine helps only the worker who uses it.
  • On the balanced growth path, the stock of ideas is proportional to the number of researchers, \(\bar{\ell}N_t\).

How to get there. Step 1: L_a = ℓ̄N and L_y = (1 − ℓ̄)N. Step 2: divide output by N. Step 3: the growth rate of ideas is z̄ℓ̄N/A. On a balanced growth path every growth rate is constant, so A must be a constant times N, and ideas grow at n̄. Rearranging gives A* = z̄ℓ̄N/n̄.

The key contrast with Chapter 5: in the Solow model, adding people dilutes capital per person. In the Romer model, adding people adds researchers, and their ideas are shared by everyone.

Long-run growth equals the degree of increasing returns times population growth

\[y_t^* = (1-\bar{\ell})\left(\frac{\bar{z}\bar{\ell}N_t}{\bar{n}}\right)^{\gamma} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad g_y^* = \gamma\bar{n}\]

  • More people → more researchers → more ideas → higher income for everyone.
  • The only term that changes over time is \(N_t^{\gamma}\), which grows at \(\gamma\bar{n}\).
  • With \(\bar{n} = 0\) there is no long-run growth at all.

This is the engine that the Solow model was missing.

Step 5 substitutes A* into y = A^γ(1 − ℓ̄). Step 6 uses the growth rule for powers: g_y = γ g_A, and g_A = n̄ on the balanced growth path.

Two textbook cases, from the notebook: γ = 2 and n̄ = 1 percent give 2 percent growth; γ = 1/2 and n̄ = 4 percent also give 2 percent.

Why ideas grow with researchers rather than faster: Romer’s original version let the growth rate of ideas rise with the number of researchers. Then growth itself would keep speeding up as population grows. U.S. growth has stayed close to 2 percent a year for more than a century, with no such acceleration, so the version here fixes that.

More people do not make us poorer in the Romer model

A growing population means more mouths to feed and less of everything to go around, so it must lower income per person.

For the world’s ideas the opposite holds: more people means more researchers, and each new idea serves everyone at once. Income per person grows at \(\gamma\bar{n}\), so a faster-growing population means faster growth.

The myth is Malthusian: with fixed resources, each extra person leaves less for the others. That is true of rival goods, of objects. Ideas are nonrival, so an extra inventor’s idea does not have to be shared out.

Be fair to the myth: in the Solow model of Chapter 5, faster population growth lowered steady-state income, because capital had to be spread over more workers. Both forces are real. The Romer force works through the world’s stock of ideas, so it is about the number of people who can create ideas anywhere, not the population of one country.

Kremer (1993, Quarterly Journal of Economics) finds that over very long periods of history, regions and eras with larger populations had faster technological progress.

Romer’s Nobel lecture puts it as a choice between “us versus them” and “us and them”.

Some changes move the growth path up; only population growth tilts it

A permanent rise in … Long-run growth \(g_y^*\) Level of the path \(y_t^*\) Output on impact
research productivity \(\bar{z}\) unchanged rises unchanged
research share \(\bar{\ell}\) unchanged rises, if \(\bar{\ell}\) is not too large falls
population growth \(\bar{n}\) rises by \(\gamma\Delta\bar{n}\) unchanged

A level effect shifts the path up or down; a growth effect changes its slope. Only \(\gamma\) and \(\bar{n}\) have growth effects.

Take logs of the BGP level: ln y* = ln(1 − ℓ̄) + γ ln ℓ̄ + γ ln z̄ + γ ln N − γ ln n̄. Each coefficient is an elasticity. A 1 percent rise in z̄ raises the BGP by γ percent (the notebook checks it: +0.599 percent with γ = 0.6).

The research share appears twice, once with a positive sign (more researchers, more ideas) and once with a negative one (fewer people making goods), so its elasticity γ − ℓ̄/(1 − ℓ̄) is positive only when ℓ̄ is small.

On impact the stock of ideas is what past research left, so output per person jumps only when the number of goods workers changes, that is, when ℓ̄ changes.

The next two experiments are the first two rows.

Why only a level effect? Long-run growth of ideas is pinned down by growth in the number of researchers. More productive researchers produce more ideas each year, a bigger stock, but the stock still grows at the rate of the research workforce in the long run.

Experiment: more people go into research

In 2050, the share of people doing research rises from 3% to 35% and stays there. What happens to output per person in 2050 itself? And in the long run?

Ask students to think about the two terms in y = A^γ(1 − ℓ̄). In 2050 the stock of ideas has not changed yet, but the number of people making goods has. Ask for a number: how much does output per person fall on impact? (0.65/0.97, a fall of about a third.)

The calculus: maximize ln(1 − ℓ̄) + γ ln ℓ̄. The derivative −1/(1 − ℓ̄) + γ/ℓ̄ is zero where ℓ̄/(1 − ℓ̄) = γ, so ℓ̄ = γ/(1 + γ) = 0.6/1.6 = 0.375. The notebook finds the same maximum numerically.

The impact effect is simply 1 − (1 − ℓ̄′)/(1 − ℓ̄): it depends only on how many people leave goods production.

For the policy students: a policy that moves people into research is an investment. It lowers output now and raises it later; whether it is worth it depends on how much we value the future.

More research is not always better

If ideas drive growth, a country should put as many people as possible into research.

In the model, the path peaks at a research share of 0.375. Raising the share from 0.03 to 0.80 leaves a path only 1.48 times the old one, far below the 2.93 reached at 0.35, because too few people are left to use the ideas.

The myth takes a true statement (ideas drive growth) one step too far. Ideas are only valuable if someone uses them to make goods.

Two cautions for the policy debate. First, real economies are far below the peak of the hump: researchers are a small share of the workforce, and in Romer’s original model markets choose too little research, because inventors cannot capture all the benefits of their ideas. So the practical question is usually how to raise research, not how to cap it. Second, even a share below the peak lowers output for years, as Fig. 4 showed.

The number 1.48 is from the notebook’s table of level effects.

Ideas cross borders: the Romer model describes the world

  • Read literally, \(y_t^*\) rises with a country’s own population, so large countries should be richer. They are not.
  • Ideas travel through trade, multinational firms, licenses and migration. A country can use ideas invented anywhere.
  • So \(N_t\) counts the people anywhere who create ideas, and \(\gamma\bar{n}\) is the growth of the world frontier.

Why countries grow at different rates is a question for the combined model, next.

This slide heads off the most common misreading of equation (4). China and India have the largest populations but are not the richest countries per person.

The resolution is that ideas are nonrival across borders as well as across firms, as long as countries can access them. Oral rehydration therapy and mobile phones are ideas that spread to poor countries quickly; patents granted in the United States go in large part to foreign inventors.

So the Romer model is best read as a model of the world’s stock of ideas. Each country then sits somewhere relative to that frontier, depending on its investment, its institutions and its access to ideas.

Combining Solow and Romer: capital amplifies the growth of ideas

\[Y_t = A_t^{\gamma}K_t^{1/3}L_{yt}^{2/3}, \qquad \Delta K_{t+1} = \bar{s}Y_t - \bar{d}K_t\]

\[g_y^* = \gamma\bar{n} + \tfrac{1}{3}g_y^* \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_y^* = \tfrac{3}{2}\gamma\bar{n}\]

  • Ideas raise output; part of it is invested; the extra capital raises output again: \(1 + \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{9} + \dots = \tfrac{3}{2}\).
  • With \(\gamma = 2/3\) and \(\bar{n} = 3\%\): growth of 3% a year, against 2% in the Romer model alone.
  • On the path, capital per person grows as fast as output per person, and \(K^*/Y^* = \bar{s}/(g_Y^* + \bar{d}) \approx 0.62\).

The combined model has six equations: the five of the Romer model, with capital added to production, and the capital-accumulation equation of Chapter 5.

The derivation: output per person is y = A^γ k^(1/3)(1 − ℓ̄)^(2/3), so g_y = γ g_A + (1/3) g_k. Ideas still grow at n̄. On a balanced growth path, K/Y is constant (from the capital equation), so g_k = g_y. Substituting gives g_y = γn̄ + (1/3)g_y, and solving gives (3/2)γn̄.

The capital-output ratio: the Solow ratio s̄/d̄, with depreciation replaced by g_Y + d̄, because investment now must also keep capital growing with output. With s̄ = 0.05, d̄ = 0.02 and g_Y = 6.09 percent, it is 0.62. The notebook’s Check 5 simulates 300 years from very little capital and lands on 0.618.

Capital cannot drive growth on its own, but it multiplies whatever ideas do.

Saving sets the level of the path; ideas set its slope

\[y_t^* = \left(\frac{\bar{s}}{g_Y^*+\bar{d}}\right)^{1/2}(A_t^*)^{3\gamma/2}\,(1-\bar{\ell})\]

A permanent rise in … Long-run growth Elasticity of the level
investment rate \(\bar{s}\) unchanged +0.5
research productivity \(\bar{z}\) unchanged +1
research share \(\bar{\ell}\) unchanged +0.89
population growth \(\bar{n}\) rises

The level comes from substituting K* = [s̄/(g_Y + d̄)] Y* into the production function and solving for Y. The textbook prints the exponent on A as 3/2, which is right only when γ = 1; for any γ it is 3γ/2. The notebook flags this.

The elasticities use the values of Fig. 8 (γ = 2/3, n̄ = 3%, d̄ = 2%, ℓ̄ = 0.1). Every elasticity linked to ideas is 3/2 times its Romer value, for the same reason as the growth rate: capital amplifies ideas. Depreciation has an elasticity of −0.12.

The headline: every parameter except γ and n̄ has only a level effect. Saving behaves as it did in Solow; ideas set the long-run growth rate.

Experiment: the investment rate jumps

In the combined model, the investment rate jumps from 5% to 60% of output in 2050. Does output per person jump? Does long-run growth rise above 3% a year?

This mirrors the Solow experiment of Chapter 5, now on top of growing ideas. Ask students to sketch the path on a ratio scale. Two things to predict: whether there is a jump on impact (no: capital takes time to build), and whether the long-run slope changes (no).

Why growth rates differ across countries for decades

  • In the long run, every country that can use the world’s ideas grows at the same rate: \(\tfrac{3}{2}\gamma\bar{n}\).
  • Over any decade, countries differ in how far they sit below their own growth paths.
  • A reform that raises \(\bar{s}\), \(\bar{\ell}\) or access to ideas opens a gap, and the transition brings decades of fast growth: Japan and South Korea.

Think of each country as a Solow economy riding on the world’s growing stock of ideas.

This is the full theory of long-run growth that the textbook’s three growth chapters build: the Romer model explains the trend of the frontier; Solow’s transition dynamics explain why some countries grow much faster or slower than the frontier for decades.

South Korea, from Chapter 5: its investment rate rose steeply, and its access to technology improved, so its growth path moved up and it grew fast during the transition. Countries whose investment or access to ideas deteriorated fall behind instead.

What the model does not say is why some countries change their parameters and others do not. That is the question of institutions from Chapter 4.

The Simon–Ehrlich bet

In 1980 the economist Julian Simon bet the biologist Paul Ehrlich that metal prices would fall over the next ten years. Four of the five metals are in the data, and all fell from 1980 to 1990:

chromium −12%, copper −23%, nickel −14%, tin −77%.

The numbers are the notebook’s key numbers for the 40-commodity chart (real prices in the latest year, 1900 = 100, and the changes from 1980 to 1990).

The exceptions are mostly energy and precious metals, plus beef, lamb, tin, chromium, manganese and coffee. Ask: why might energy be different? (Demand for energy grew even faster than for materials, extraction faces depletion, and prices are affected by cartels and wars.) Why gold? (It is held as an asset, not used up.)

The bet: Simon let Ehrlich pick five metals and a ten-year window. Ehrlich, who had predicted mass shortages, paid up in 1990. The policy slide comes back to whether Simon was skillful or lucky.

Finite resources do not have to mean rising prices

The planet’s supply of raw materials is fixed, and demand keeps growing, so their prices must rise over time.

Over the twentieth century, the real price of the six industrial commodities fell by a factor of about 3, and 28 of 40 commodities are cheaper than in 1900. New ideas kept expanding the usable supply faster than demand grew.

The myth applies a correct static argument (fixed supply plus rising demand) to a world where supply is not fixed. What is fixed is the number of atoms; what is not fixed is our knowledge of where to find them, how to extract them and how to use fewer of them.

Be careful not to overclaim. Prices rose steeply after 2002, and a few commodities, especially energy, are more expensive than in 1900. The point is that rising prices are not inevitable: whether ideas keep up with demand is an empirical question.

Ask: what would rising prices do to the incentive to find new ideas? (Raise it, which is part of why the long-run trend has been down.)

Limits to growth: can ideas keep beating scarcity?

Can new ideas keep growth going on a finite planet, or will resources and pollution set the limit?

Why ideas can win

  • Most raw materials got cheaper as use soared.
  • Higher energy prices brought more energy-saving inventions.
  • Simon won his bet against scarcity.

Why it is contested

  • In many other decades, Simon would have lost.
  • Market prices leave out pollution and carbon.
  • Ideas must be steered toward clean technology.

The debate goes back to Meadows and others, The Limits to Growth (1972), which projected that resource depletion and pollution would end growth within a century.

Left column: the commodity figures above. Popp (2002, American Economic Review) shows that energy-saving patents in the United States rose with energy prices: scarcity directs research toward saving the scarce input. The Simon–Ehrlich bet, from the notebook.

Right column: Kiel, Matheson and Golden (2010, Ecological Economics) repeat the bet for every ten-year window of the twentieth century and find that Ehrlich would often have won: the result depends on the window. Prices measure scarcity of the commodity to buyers, not the damage from burning it: carbon emissions are an externality. Acemoglu, Aghion, Bursztyn and Hémous (2012, American Economic Review) show that innovation follows profits, so without policy it keeps improving dirty technologies; a carbon price plus research subsidies can redirect it toward clean ones.

The 2018 Nobel Prize went jointly to Romer, for ideas and growth, and William Nordhaus, for climate economics: the two questions belong together. Ask the policy students: which policy would you use to steer ideas, a carbon tax, research subsidies, or both?

Growth accounting: TFP is what is left over

\[\underset{\text{output per hour}}{g_Y - g_L} \;=\; \underset{\text{capital per hour}}{\tfrac{1}{3}(g_K - g_L)} \;+\; \underset{\text{labor composition}}{\tfrac{2}{3}(g_{L_y} - g_L)} \;+\; \underset{\text{TFP}}{g_A}\]

  • Output, capital, hours and the skills of workers can be measured; TFP cannot. So TFP growth is the residual: the Solow residual.
  • Example: output per hour +2.0%, capital per hour +2.4%, composition +0.2: TFP \(= 2.0 - 0.8 - 0.2 =\) 1.0 point, half of all growth.

The derivation: take logs of Y = A K^(1/3) L_y^(2/3) and the change from one year to the next: g_Y = g_A + (1/3)g_K + (2/3)g_Ly. Subtract the growth of hours, g_L, from both sides, splitting it as (1/3)g_L + (2/3)g_L.

L is total hours worked; L_y is the effective labor input, which also rises when the workforce becomes more educated or experienced. That is the “labor composition” term.

The worked example is the notebook’s Check 7. Capital per hour contributes a third of its growth, 0.8 points; labor composition 0.2; the rest, 1.0 point, is TFP.

This is a decomposition, not a causal model. Keep that in mind for the myth slide.

Growth accounting does not tell us what caused growth

Capital per hour accounts for about a third of U.S. growth, so about a third of growth was caused by investment in machines.

In the combined model with \(\gamma = 1\), output per hour grows 1.5% if TFP grows 1.0%. Accounting credits 0.5 points to capital, yet all of the growth is caused by ideas: the capital is there only because new ideas made it worth building.

The numbers are the notebook’s Check 7: on the balanced growth path with γ = 1, g_y = (3/2)g_A, and capital per hour grows as fast as output per hour, so equation (10) credits (1/3)(1.5) = 0.5 points to capital and the remaining 1.0 to TFP.

The lesson: accounting splits growth into proximate sources, not ultimate causes. Without TFP growth, capital would stop growing too, as in the Solow model. So the true contribution of ideas is larger than the TFP row of the table.

A second test from the model: on a balanced growth path, output per hour and capital per hour grow at the same rate. An economy whose capital per hour grows much faster is still in transition.

Is growth slowing down for good?

Is slow productivity growth a passing phase, or the new normal? And could artificial intelligence change the answer?

The pessimists

  • The great inventions are behind us.
  • Ideas are getting harder to find.
  • Mismeasured free apps cannot explain it.

The optimists

  • AI can transform how goods are made.
  • AI may also speed up research itself.
  • In the model, a higher \(\bar{z}\) means decades of fast growth.

Left column. Gordon (2016, The Rise and Fall of American Growth) argues that electricity, sanitation, antibiotics and the internal combustion engine transformed life in a way that email and smartphones cannot repeat. Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb (2020, American Economic Review) show that the number of researchers has risen steeply while productivity growth has not, so each researcher produces less growth over time: research productivity is falling, in the economy and in specific fields such as computer chips and crop yields. Syverson (2017, Journal of Economic Perspectives) finds that the gains from free digital goods are too small to explain the missing growth.

Right column. Brynjolfsson and McAfee (2014, The Second Machine Age) argue that machine learning and robots will raise productivity sharply. Agrawal, McHale and Oettl (2019, in The Economics of Artificial Intelligence) model AI as a tool that helps researchers find useful combinations of existing ideas, which is a rise in z̄. Fig. 3 showed what that does: a level effect and decades of fast growth, though not a permanent rise in the growth rate.

For the policy students: what would you do to raise z̄ or ℓ̄? Research funding, education in science and engineering, immigration of researchers, competition policy. Each has costs and uncertain effects.

“If growth needs a growing population, does growth end when population stops growing?”

A skeptic says: “In the Romer model, \(g_y^* = \gamma\bar{n}\). World population growth is slowing and may turn negative this century. So the model predicts that growth will stop, or that a shrinking world will get poorer.”

Reply: the skeptic reads the model correctly, and economists take the warning seriously. What matters is the number of people creating ideas, not population itself, and that number can keep growing for a long time through education, a larger research share and billions of people in poorer countries joining the frontier.

Take the objection seriously: in the model, ideas grow with researchers, and researchers are a share of the population. If the population stops growing and the share is fixed, the stock of ideas grows ever more slowly in percentage terms, and so does income.

Jones (2022, American Economic Review, “The End of Economic Growth? Unintended Consequences of a Declining Population”) makes exactly this point: if population declines, an economy can end up with living standards that stagnate, as the stock of ideas stops growing. He calls it the “empty planet” result.

The reply has three parts. First, the research share can rise: in the past century the number of researchers in rich countries grew far faster than population, because of mass education. Second, most of the world’s people live in countries far from the frontier; as they become richer and better educated, many more become idea creators. Third, research productivity z̄ can rise too, for example with AI, though in the model that gives a level effect, not lasting growth.

Ask: which policies would you consider if the number of researchers stopped growing? (Education, immigration of researchers, research subsidies, family policy.) Each one is a choice about the parameters ℓ̄ and n̄.

What to remember from this chapter

  • Ideas are nonrival, so returns to objects and ideas together are increasing, and prices at marginal cost cannot pay for research.
  • In the Romer model, income depends on the total stock of ideas, and long-run growth is \(g_y^* = \gamma\bar{n}\).
  • Research productivity, the research share and saving have level effects; only \(\gamma\) and \(\bar{n}\) change long-run growth.
  • With capital added, growth is \(\tfrac{3}{2}\gamma\bar{n}\), and transition dynamics explain decades of catch-up.
  • In the data, raw materials got cheaper, and the U.S. slowdowns were mostly TFP slowdowns.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: can new ideas keep income growing forever? A good answer: yes, as long as the number of people creating ideas keeps growing; saving and research policies change the level of the path, not its long-run slope.

This closes the textbook’s study of long-run growth (Chapters 3 to 6). The open question is why some countries have the parameters, investment, research and access to ideas, that put them on high paths, and others do not.
