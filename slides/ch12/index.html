In June 1981 the Fed’s interest rate reached 19.1%

19.1% The federal funds rate, June 1981

An overnight rate that banks charge one another. The Fed pushed it that high on purpose, and a deep recession followed. Why would a central bank do that, and how can one overnight rate move the whole economy?

Open with the number. It is the peak of the effective federal funds rate in the notebook’s Fig. 2: 19.10 percent in June 1981. In 2009–2015 the same rate sat between 0.07 and 0.24 percent, and in September 2026 it was 3.75 percent.

The puzzle the textbook opens with, in our words: the Fed’s main tool is a very short-term nominal rate, used only between banks. Yet a change in it moves stock prices, mortgage rates, investment plans and, in the end, GDP and inflation.

The 1981 answer: inflation had passed 13 percent, and the Fed under Paul Volcker decided to bring it down even at the cost of a recession. Act III of this deck shows why the model says a recession was unavoidable.

Ask the room: who has a loan or a credit card whose rate follows the Fed? (Most variable-rate loans do.)

Today: three questions

  • How does the central bank set its interest rate, and why does it matter? The fed funds rate, the money market, the real rate and the yield curve.
  • How does that rate move output and inflation? The IS/MP diagram, the Phillips curve, demand shocks and price shocks.
  • What does it take to bring inflation down? The Volcker disinflation, the Great Inflation of the 1970s, the pandemic surge, and a policy rule.

These are the three acts of the deck and the three branches of the mind map on the title slide.

The headline result: because inflation is sticky, the central bank’s nominal rate sets the real rate, the real rate sets short-run output through the IS curve, and output moves inflation through the Phillips curve. So lowering inflation takes a period of output below potential, and its total cost depends only on the slope of the Phillips curve.

This chapter completes the short-run model: Chapter 9 gave the Phillips curve in a simple form, Chapter 11 the IS curve. Chapter 13 adds a policy rule and expectations (the AS/AD framework).

The policy rate is the central bank’s one lever, and it moves a lot

  • Range since 1960: about 0.05% (2020–21) to 19.1% (June 1981).
  • Volcker’s tight money: 17.6% in April 1980, 18.9% in December 1980, above 19% in 1981.
  • Near zero in 2009–15 and 2020–21; fast hikes in 2022–23 against the return of inflation; cuts from September 2024.
  • In and after nearly every shaded recession, the rate falls: the Fed leans against the slump.

A rate on overnight loans between banks is the instrument. The rest of the deck explains how it reaches output and prices.

All numbers are the notebook’s Fig. 2 key numbers. The latest value in the data is 3.75 percent (September 2026).

How the Fed makes the rate stick: it stands ready to lend to banks, or to pay them, at its chosen rate. No bank will pay a higher rate to borrow overnight, since it could borrow from the Fed. And no bank can keep lending below the rate, since others would borrow from it cheaply and park the money at the Fed for a riskless profit, an arbitrage.

The interest rate is the price of holding money

\[M^d = m_0 - i \qquad M^s = \bar{M}^s\]

\[i^* = m_0 - \bar{M}^s\]

  • Cash and checking accounts pay nothing; a savings account pays \(i\). So \(i\) is the opportunity cost of holding money, and money demand slopes down.
  • The central bank chooses the supply: a vertical line.
  • Baseline: \(m_0 = 10\), \(\bar{M}^s = 5\), so \(i^* = 5\%\).

In the short run prices are sticky, so the money market clears through the interest rate, not through the price level.

The money demand line is illustrative (straight, in made-up units); the book chapter’s Microfoundations section has the derivation. Higher prices or output shift m0 to the right; new ways of paying (cards, phones) shift it to the left.

Above i, people want to hold less money than there is: they buy bonds or move money to savings accounts, and the rate falls. Below i, they want more money than there is, and the rate rises.

Contrast with Chapter 8: in the quantity theory, the price level adjusts to make money demand equal supply. Here inflation is sticky over a short horizon, so the interest rate does the adjusting (and with it the velocity of money).

Tight money is a higher rate, carried out by selling bonds

  • One unit less of money raises \(i^*\) by one point: 5% → 6%.
  • The Fed changes the supply by open-market operations: it sells bonds to take money in, buys bonds to put money out.
  • A bond’s price and its interest rate move in opposite directions.

Targeting the interest rate

Money demand rises from \(10 - i\) to \(11 - i\): people want more money at every rate. The central bank wants to keep the rate at exactly 5%. What must it do with the money supply?

A good answer: supply one more unit, 6 instead of 5. Ask what would happen if the bank did nothing: the rate would rise to 6 percent, although nothing real changed in the economy.

Central banks set a rate, not a quantity of money

  • With the rate pegged, demand up by 1 unit is met by 1 more unit: 6 instead of 5, and \(i\) stays at 5%.
  • With the supply fixed, the same shift would raise \(i\) to 6%: with sticky inflation, a higher real rate and lower output, for no real reason.
  • Money demand shifts all the time: prices, output, new ways to pay.
  • IS/LM: hold the supply fixed and the rate rises with output (an upward “LM” curve). Pegging the rate flattens it into our MP line.

Targeting the rate shields output from noise in money demand. That is why the MP curve is a flat line.

Numbers: the notebook’s money-market check: demand 11 − i with the supply fixed gives 6 percent; with the rate held at 5 percent the supply needed is 6.

The IS/LM model is the older textbook version of the short-run model; it assumes a fixed money supply. Because real central banks adjust the supply to hit a rate, the two approaches give the same answers once the LM curve is replaced by the MP curve.

Ask: why did some central banks target the money supply in the past? (Money growth and inflation go together in the long run, Chapter 8. The Fed under Volcker described its policy as targeting money growth, which meant very high interest rates.)

Because inflation is sticky, setting \(i\) means setting \(R\)

\[i_t = R_t + \pi_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_t = i_t - \pi_t \qquad\text{and, with } \Delta\pi_t = 0,\quad \Delta R_t = \Delta i_t\]

  • Sticky inflation: over about six months, inflation does not react to a policy move. Firms reprice only now and then, information and calculation are costly, and many wages and rents are fixed in nominal contracts.
  • So each point on \(i\) is a point on \(R\). To hit a real rate \(R^T\), set \(i = R^T + \pi\).
  • 2% inflation: \(i = 7\%\) gives \(R = 5\% = \bar{r}\). 10% inflation, as in 1979: it takes 15%, and 16% gives \(R = 6\%\).

The MP curve: a horizontal line at the real rate the central bank picks.

The Fisher equation (Chapter 8) says a nominal rate pays for the real return plus the loss of purchasing power. Subtract inflation from both sides and compare the moments just before and after a policy move: if inflation does not move, the real rate moves one for one with the nominal rate. The notebook’s Check 1 confirms: i from 7 to 8 with inflation stuck at 2 raises R by 1.0 point; if inflation also rises to 3, R does not move.

If inflation were not sticky (the classical dichotomy holding even in the short run), a higher nominal rate could simply come with lower inflation and the real rate, and output, would be untouched. Stickiness is what gives monetary policy real effects.

Ex ante and ex post: a borrower decides with expected inflation, i − π^e; the realized rate is i − π. Inflation above what was expected lowers the real rate paid: with i = 7, expected inflation 2 and actual 4, the ex ante rate is 5 percent and the ex post rate 3. The model ignores the difference.

The reasons for stickiness come from the textbook’s microfoundations (a pizza parlor that cannot re-price every day), in our words.

A long rate is roughly the average of the short rates expected over its life

\[(1 + i^{(n)})^n = (1 + i_1)(1 + i^e_2)\cdots(1 + i^e_n) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad i^{(n)} \approx \frac{i_1 + i^e_2 + \dots + i^e_n}{n}\]

  • One 5-year bond, or five 1-year bonds in a row: if one paid more, everyone would switch.
  • Expected 1-year rates of 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6% give a 5-year rate of about 4% (3.990% exactly).
  • A short-rate hike lifts long rates more when markets expect it to last.

The yield curve plots rates against maturity. When it is inverted, markets expect short rates to fall.

Take logs of both sides: the log of a power is the exponent times the log, the log of a product is the sum of the logs, and ln(1 + x) ≈ x for small rates. Divide by n. This is the term structure of interest rates (expectations version).

The notebook’s Check 2 computes the exact five-year rate that matches five one-year bonds in a row: 3.990 percent, against a simple average of 4.000 percent.

This is how an overnight rate reaches the rates firms and homebuyers pay: by changing what markets expect the overnight rate to be over the next years. In practice long rates also include a risk premium, which shows up in the country data two slides ahead.

A spread mixes expected policy with risk

  • An inversion says markets expect short rates to fall, often because they expect a slump that will make the central bank cut.
  • That forecast was right in 2000, 2006–07 and 2019. In 2022–25 it was not, at least so far.
  • A long yield also carries a risk premium: Greece’s +28 points in 2012 was fear of default, not a forecast of Greek monetary policy.

Read the yield curve as expectations plus risk, not as a recession alarm.

The predictive record of the term spread is a well-known empirical result: Estrella and Hardouvelis (1991, Journal of Finance) and Estrella and Mishkin (1998, Review of Economics and Statistics) find that the spread forecasts U.S. recessions a year or more ahead better than most indicators. The 2022–25 inversion without a recession is a warning against reading it mechanically: the inversion may have reflected an expected fall in inflation, and with it in nominal rates, rather than a slump.

The IS/MP diagram: the central bank picks the height, the IS curve picks the output

\[\text{IS: } \widetilde{Y}\!_t = \bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R_t - \bar{r}) \qquad\qquad \text{MP: } R_t \text{ set by the central bank}\]

\[\widetilde{Y}\!_t = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R_t = \bar{r} + \frac{\bar{a}}{\bar{b}}\]

  • \(\bar{a}\) is the demand shock (zero in normal times); \(\bar{b}\) is how strongly spending reacts to the real rate.
  • When borrowing costs more than capital earns (\(R > \bar{r}\)), output falls; a fall in demand is offset by a cut of \(1/\bar{b}\) points per point of \(\bar{a}\).

The IS curve comes from Chapter 11. When borrowing costs more than new capital earns, firms and households postpone investment. The baseline of every diagram: ā = 0, b̄ = 2, r̄ = 5 percent.

The second equation solves the IS curve for the rate that delivers output at potential (equation 3 of the book chapter). The notebook’s Check 1 confirms it: ā = −2 gives R = 4.00 percent, ā = −1 gives 4.50, ā = +1.5 gives 5.75, and at each the IS curve returns Ỹ = 0.

In terms of the nominal rate: i = r̄ + ā/b̄ + π.

In the model, policy can undo any demand shock; in practice it is late

  • Bust of \(\bar{a}' = -2\): output −2% at \(R = 5\%\); cut to \(R' = 4\%\) and output is back at potential.
  • The larger the shock, the larger the cut. The flatter the IS curve, the smaller the cut needed.
  • But the size of a shock is seen only with a delay, and rates take roughly 6 to 18 months to move spending: Friedman’s “long and variable lags”.

The Phillips curve: output moves the change in inflation

\[\pi_t = \pi^e_t + \bar{\nu}\widetilde{Y}\!_t \qquad \pi^e_t = \pi_{t-1} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta\pi_t = \bar{\nu}\,\widetilde{Y}\!_t + \bar{o}\]

  • Price setting: each firm raises prices by the inflation it expects elsewhere, plus more when demand is strong and less when it is weak.
  • Adaptive expectations: firms expect this year’s inflation to equal last year’s.
  • Price shocks \(\bar{o}\): oil, steel, a big union settlement (\(\bar{o} > 0\)); a wave of immigration that holds wages down (\(\bar{o} < 0\)).

Output sets the change in inflation. At potential and with no shock, inflation stays wherever it was.

Inflation here is the rise of prices over the coming year, π_t = (P_{t+1} − P_t)/P_t, set by firms during year t when they choose next year’s prices. That timing is why a recession this year already lowers this year’s inflation.

The slope ν̄ is illustrative: 0.5 in all the figures. Chapter 9 estimated about 1/3 for the United States.

Two names for the two terms: ν̄Ỹ is demand-pull inflation (strong demand pulls prices up); ō is cost-push inflation (higher costs push them up).

Experiment: a boom

Inflation has been 2% for years, and \(\bar{\nu} = 0.5\). This year output is 2% above potential. What happens to inflation this year? And next year, if output is still 2% above potential?

Above potential, inflation rises; below it, inflation falls

A boom raises inflation every year it lasts

  • At \(\widetilde{Y} = +2\%\): inflation rises by \(0.5 \times 2 =\) 1 point, from 2% to 3%.
  • Next year firms expect 3%, so a boom that continues takes it to 4%, then 5%…
  • To keep output above potential for ever, inflation must rise without limit: there is no lasting trade-off.
  • History: the 1960s curve linked the level of inflation to activity. Friedman and Phelps argued that firms would build higher inflation into their prices; the 1970s proved them right.

A steeper curve means prices react more to demand; a flat curve means a deep slump is needed to lower inflation.

Numbers: the live figure and the Fig. 7 “What to notice”: output 2 percent above potential raises inflation by 0.5 × 2 = 1 point a year.

The algebra: with Ỹ held at x every year, inflation after t + 1 years is the starting inflation plus ν̄x(t + 1). Inflation is a running total of all past gaps.

Friedman (1968, American Economic Review) and Phelps (1967, Economica) are the classic statements of the natural-rate hypothesis.

Quantity theory or Phillips curve? In Chapter 8, faster growth of real GDP lowers inflation; here a boom raises it. The quantity theory is about the long run, where more output is more supply; here a positive Ỹ is demand above what the economy normally supplies.

The whole model is one equation for inflation

\[\pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + \bar{\nu}\left[\bar{a} - \bar{b}\,(R_t - \bar{r})\right] + \bar{o}\]

  • Given last year’s inflation, this year’s policy \(R_t\) and the shocks, it gives this year’s inflation: a difference equation.
  • Within a year the chain runs one way: \(R_t \Rightarrow \widetilde{Y}\!_t \Rightarrow \pi_t\).
  • Once policy returns to \(R = \bar{r} + \bar{a}/\bar{b}\), output is at potential and inflation stays where it has got to. Any inflation rate can be a resting point.

Nothing inside the model pulls inflation back to 2%. Only policy can move it.

Substitute the IS curve for Ỹ in the Phillips curve in levels. The book chapter’s simulate function applies this equation year after year; the notebook checks it against the closed form for inflation as a running total of past gaps and shocks.

A high interest rate is not the same as tight money

Interest rates are very high, so monetary policy must be very tight.

Tightness is the real rate. In 2022 Türkiye’s policy rate averaged 12.6% while inflation reached 72%: a real rate of about −60%. Inflation began to fall only after the rate was raised to about 49% in 2024.

Experiment: the disinflation year by year

  1. t* = 5: inflation 9, 8, 7, 6, 5 percent, then flat at 5. Output lost: 10 percent-years.

  3. t* = 8: inflation falls to 2 percent; the cost grows to 16 percent-years.

The notebook’s interactive version also lets you choose the depth of the recession and the slope.

The cost of a disinflation: \(1/\bar{\nu}\) percent-years per point

\[R = \bar{r} + \frac{\pi_{-1} - \pi^T}{\bar{\nu}\,\bar{b}\,T}\]

\[\text{lost output} = \frac{\pi_{-1} - \pi^T}{\bar{\nu}}\]

  • 10% to 5% in 5 years: \(R = 6\%\), \(\widetilde{Y} = -2\%\) a year, path 9, 8, 7, 6, 5.
  • Cost: \(5/0.5 =\) 10 percent-years of output.
  • Once \(R = \bar{r}\), output returns to potential and inflation stays at 5%.

Disinflation is a temporary recession, not a permanent loss. Its total size is fixed by the slope.

The first equation: hold the rate constant for T years and solve the running-total formula for the output gap, then the IS curve for the rate. The second: add up the gaps; the T cancels.

The notebook’s checks: holding R at 6.00 percent for 5 years gives 9, 8, 7, 6, 5 and an output loss of 10.0 percent-years, equal to equation (9). Another design: from 10 to 4 percent in 4 years needs R = 6.50 percent, with inflation 8.5, 7, 5.5, 4.

In reality U.S. inflation fell from 13.5 percent in 1980 to 3.2 percent in 1983, faster than in this example, and the recession of 1981–82 was the deepest in decades. Chapter 13 explains part of the speed with expectations.

A faster disinflation is deeper, not cheaper

Going slowly spreads the pain thin, so a gradual disinflation costs less output.

In the model the total is the same: 10 percent-years to go from 10% to 5%. Output at −4% gets there in 2.5 years; at −2% in 5 years. Only a steeper Phillips curve makes it cheaper.

Numbers: the Fig. 12 “What to notice” (a deeper recession, Ỹ = −4, takes 2.5 years at about the same total cost). The general result: any path of output whose gaps add up to (π^T − π_{−1})/ν̄ does the job, whatever its shape.

Real-world caveats, which the simple model leaves out: if the central bank is credible, expectations may fall faster than last year’s inflation and the cost may be lower (Chapter 13). Ball (1994) found smaller sacrifice ratios in faster disinflations across OECD countries, a contested finding discussed in the Chapter 9 deck.

Ask: which costs more in human terms, two years at −5 percent or five years at −2 percent? (Same output in total; the answer depends on who loses jobs and for how long.)

Should a central bank follow a rule instead of its judgment?

Instead of choosing each year’s rate by judgment, the central bank could commit to a rule: raise the real rate by \(\bar{m}\) points for each point of inflation above its target, \(R_t - \bar{r} = \bar{m}(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})\).

What the model says

  • Each year the inflation gap shrinks to \(\lambda\) of itself: 2/3 at \(\bar{m} = 1/2\).
  • From 10%: 7.3, 5.6, 4.4%… toward the 2% target.
  • Any \(\bar{m}\) costs the same 16 percent-years.

What is debated

  • How strongly to respond.
  • Whether to respond to output as well.
  • A rule is predictable; judgment can react to surprises.

The model numbers are the book chapter’s preview of Chapter 13 (equations 10 and 11) and the notebook’s Check 5: λ = 1/(1 + ν̄b̄m̄), so with ν̄ = 0.5, b̄ = 2 and m̄ = 1/2, λ = 2/3. Starting from 10 percent with a 2 percent target, inflation runs 7.33, 5.56, 4.37, 3.58 percent and output −5.33, −3.56, −2.37, −1.58 percent. For m̄ = 0.25, 0.5 and 1, output in year 0 is −3.20, −5.33 and −8.00 percent, but the total loss is 16.00 percent-years in every case: a tougher rule front-loads the recession without making it cheaper.

The nominal rate rises by 1 + m̄ points for each point of inflation, more than one for one, so that the real rate rises: the Taylor principle.

The research. Taylor (1993, Carnegie-Rochester Conference Series on Public Policy) proposed the rule that bears his name, responding to inflation and the output gap, and showed that it tracked Fed policy in 1987–1992. Clarida, Galí and Gertler (2000, Quarterly Journal of Economics) estimate that before 1979 the Fed raised nominal rates by less than inflation, violating the Taylor principle, and that it raised them by more after 1979. Orphanides (2003, Journal of Monetary Economics) argues that with the output-gap estimates available in real time, 1970s policy looked closer to a rule than it does with today’s data. How mechanically central banks should follow rules is debated.

Ask the policy students: what would you lose by tying the central bank to a formula? (Room to respond to a financial crisis or a pandemic; a rule also needs an estimate of r̄ and of the output gap.)

A productivity slowdown

After 1973 potential output grew more slowly, but policymakers still believed in the old trend. Actual output fell behind that believed trend. What did they think they saw, what did they do, and what happened to inflation?

This is the textbook’s explanation of part of the Great Inflation. Let students reason: if you think potential is higher than it is, an economy at its true potential looks like a recession. You stimulate. Then?

A misread slowdown turned stimulus into inflation

  • 1980: perceived \(\widetilde{Y} =\) −3.7%, true \(\widetilde{Y} =\) +3.3%.
  • The true gap was positive every year, so inflation rose every year: about 8 points by 1980.
  • Actual U.S. inflation: 6.3% in 1973, 13.5% in 1980, helped by the oil shocks of 1974 and 1979.
  • The warning sign is rising inflation, but oil shocks also raise inflation, so the lesson came slowly.

Potential output is not observed. Mistake a slower trend for a slump, and policy feeds inflation.

The research. Orphanides (2002, American Economic Review Papers and Proceedings; 2003, Journal of Monetary Economics) uses the Fed’s real-time data to show that output gaps believed in the 1970s were far more negative than today’s estimates, so policy that followed a sensible rule on those numbers was too loose. Romer and Romer (2002) stress instead policymakers’ beliefs, such as an optimistic natural rate of unemployment. The weight of each is debated.

Believing in a shock that never happened creates a boom of the same size

  • The cut to \(R' = \bar{r} + \bar{a}'/\bar{b}\) gives true output \(\widetilde{Y} = -\bar{b}(R' - \bar{r}) = -\bar{a}'\): +2% for a believed −2.
  • Inflation rises by 1 point a year for as long as the mistake lasts: \(\downarrow R \Rightarrow \uparrow\widetilde{Y} \Rightarrow \uparrow\pi\).
  • Add oil shocks and the belief in a lasting trade-off, and you have the Great Inflation.

The same tool that cures a demand shock causes inflation when the shock is imaginary.

Numbers: the notebook’s Check 4 (believed −2: R cut to 4.00 percent, true Ỹ = +2.0 percent; believed −1: 4.50 percent, +1.0 percent).

The book chapter’s practice question: once the Fed discovers the mistake, to what rate should it return? (To r̄ = 5 percent, which stops the rise in inflation; to bring inflation back down by 1 point a year it must go higher, to 6 percent.)

Should a central bank fight an oil shock or let it pass?

Oil prices jump and inflation rises at once, with output at potential. Should the central bank raise rates and cause a recession, or accommodate the shock and accept higher inflation?

What the model says

  • Accommodate: inflation up by \(\bar{o}\), and it stays up.
  • Fight: output at \(-\bar{o}/\bar{\nu}\); undoing a 1-point shock costs 2 percent-years.
  • No option avoids both costs.

What it leaves out

  • Expectations: if people trust the target, a one-off shock may not stick.
  • Oil can lower potential output too.
  • Part of the 1970s damage came from the policy response.

Model numbers: the notebook’s Check 4 (a one-year shock of 1 point raises inflation from 2 to 3 percent for good; undoing it costs 2.0 percent-years) and the Fig. 8 key numbers.

The research. Bernanke, Gertler and Watson (1997, Brookings Papers on Economic Activity) argue that much of the output loss after the oil shocks of the 1970s came from the Fed’s tightening response rather than from oil itself, a conclusion others dispute. Blanchard and Galí (2007, NBER Working Paper 13368; published 2010 in Galí and Gertler, eds., International Dimensions of Monetary Policy) find that the oil shocks of the 2000s did far less damage than those of the 1970s, and attribute it partly to more credible monetary policy, more flexible labor markets and a smaller oil share. In the model of this chapter, with adaptive expectations, credibility has no role; Chapter 13 adds it.

Today’s version: in 2022 energy prices jumped after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, and central banks had to decide how much of the inflation to fight (Fig. 15, next).

Ask the policy students: how would you explain to the public that you are causing a recession to fight an inflation that came from abroad?

Transfers kept income up, and the savings fed the recovery

  • Income rose while unemployment hit 14.8%; consumption fell 16.8% in two months.
  • Households saved much of the money: in the model, transfers raised \(\bar{a}_c\) and so \(\bar{a}\).
  • Per person, U.S. real income rose 17.6% from 2019Q4 to 2021Q1; consumption only 1.1%.
  • Germany and the United Kingdom paid firms to keep workers instead: their income barely moved.

Fiscal support turned a collapse in demand into a fast recovery, and later into demand pressure on prices.

Numbers: the Fig. 17 “What to notice” and its cross-country key numbers (OECD household dashboard, real income and consumption per person). Canada and Japan also show income jumps in 2020Q2: emergency benefits in Canada and 100,000 yen to every resident in Japan.

The Covid-19 shock in the model, from the textbook: partly a fall in demand (people avoided shops and restaurants), which lowered inflation at first; partly a fall in potential output (people stopped going to work), which by itself leaves Ỹ unchanged and does not push inflation down. That mix helps explain why inflation fell so little in 2020.

Real GDP, Fig. 14 of the book chapter: down 9.1 percent from 2019Q4 to 2020Q2, back within about 1 percent of its pre-Covid trend from 2021Q2. Chapter 10 told that story in detail.

What caused the 2021–22 inflation, and should the Fed have moved sooner?

Was the surge a temporary supply shock that a central bank should look through, or excess demand from stimulus that it should have fought earlier?

Supply shocks

  • Energy, food and supply chains: a rise in \(\bar{o}\).
  • Headline peaked before core, and core stayed lower.
  • Fighting it means a recession.

Demand

  • Transfers, savings and near-zero rates.
  • Potential output was reduced, so demand ran above it.
  • A late response let inflation spread.

The two columns are the textbook’s forces, sorted. Numbers on the slides: Fig. 15 (headline peak 9.0 percent in June 2022, core 6.6 percent in September).

The research. Bernanke and Blanchard (2023, Brookings Hutchins Center Working Paper 86, “What Caused the U.S. Pandemic-Era Inflation?”) estimate that most of the surge came from price shocks in energy, food and supply chains, with tight labor markets playing a growing and more persistent role later. Shapiro (2022, Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco Working Paper 2022-18) splits PCE inflation into supply- and demand-driven parts and finds that supply factors explained about half of the rise, demand about a third. The share each side deserves, and whether the Fed waited too long to raise rates, is still argued.

Ask the policy students: if most of the surge was supply, was the Fed’s fast hiking in 2022 a mistake? (The model’s answer: it depends on whether the shock would have stuck in expectations. That is Chapter 13’s subject.)

Inflation does not return to target on its own

Once the recession is over and output is back at potential, inflation drifts back to 2% by itself.

In the model inflation stays wherever it has got to: after a 1-point oil shock it stays at 3%; after five years of tight policy it stays at 5%, not 2%. Only policy moves it.

Numbers: the notebook’s Check 4 (one-year price shock: inflation 3 percent in every later year) and Fig. 12 (inflation stays at 5 percent once R returns to r̄).

Why: with adaptive expectations, firms expect last year’s inflation; at potential they have no reason to raise prices faster or slower than that. Any inflation rate can be a resting point.

What changes this: a policy rule that responds to inflation (the policy slide on rules), and expectations anchored by a credible target (Chapter 13). Both are ways to put a “pull” toward 2 percent into the model.

“Inflation fell without a recession, so the model is wrong”

A skeptic says: “U.S. inflation fell from 9.0% in June 2022 to between 2.3% and 3.0% from mid-2024 through 2025, and there was no recession. The model says each point of disinflation costs \(1/\bar{\nu}\) percent-years of lost output. It cannot be right.”

Reply: the cost formula is for inflation that has become expected. A price shock that reverses (\(\bar{o} < 0\)) lowers inflation with output at potential, at no cost. Much of 2022–24 was shocks unwinding, with expectations that stayed close to target, which the next chapter adds to the model.

Take the objection seriously: it is the main empirical challenge to this chapter’s model, and economists argued about it in real time. Blanchard, Domash and Summers (2022, Peterson Institute Policy Brief 22-7) argued from the Beveridge curve that bringing inflation down would likely require a sizable rise in unemployment; it did not happen.

The reply in the model’s own terms: in Δπ_t = ν̄Ỹ_t + ō, inflation can fall because Ỹ < 0 (costly) or because ō < 0 (free). When energy prices and supply chains normalized, ō turned negative. The key caveat of the model is adaptive expectations: if people expected inflation to return to 2 percent, expected inflation did not follow last year’s 8 percent, and the Phillips curve did not have to grind it down.

The research. Benigno and Eggertsson (2023, NBER Working Paper 31197) argue that the Phillips curve is steeper when labor markets are very tight, so that inflation rose fast in 2021–22 and could fall back quickly as the labor market cooled, without large job losses. Bernanke and Blanchard (2023) find that the price shocks that caused much of the surge faded on their own. Neither view is settled.

The book’s numbers: Fig. 15 (9.0 percent in June 2022; 2.3 to 3.0 percent from mid-2024 through 2025).

Ask: what would the 2022–24 episode have looked like if expectations had followed the adaptive rule? (Inflation would have stayed high until a recession brought it down, as in 1981–82.)

What to remember from this chapter

  • The central bank sets an overnight nominal rate by supplying whatever money is demanded there. Because inflation is sticky, that sets the real rate: \(R = i - \pi\) (the MP curve).
  • Through the IS curve the real rate sets output; through the term structure it reaches long rates.
  • The Phillips curve, \(\Delta\pi = \bar{\nu}\widetilde{Y} + \bar{o}\): booms and price shocks raise inflation, and it stays up.
  • Lowering inflation takes a temporary recession of \((\pi_{-1} - \pi^T)/\bar{\nu}\) percent-years, however fast it is done: Volcker, 13.5% to 3.2%.
  • Oil shocks, a misread slowdown and a late response fed the Great Inflation, and in miniature the 2021–22 surge.

Ask each student to write one sentence answering the opening question: how does the central bank’s rate move output and inflation, and why does bringing inflation down take a recession? A good answer: with sticky inflation, a higher nominal rate is a higher real rate, which lowers spending and output; output below potential slows inflation; so inflation built into expectations can be lowered only by a period of output below potential.

For the policy students, three habits: judge policy by the real rate, not the nominal one; ask whether a rise in inflation is demand or a price shock; and ask how sure anyone is about potential output.
