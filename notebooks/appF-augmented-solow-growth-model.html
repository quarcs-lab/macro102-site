Learning goals

Can a model as simple as Solow’s explain why some countries are rich and others poor? In a famous paper, Mankiw, Romer and Weil (1992), “MRW” from now on, answered: almost, once the model counts human capital, the skills that workers build up through schooling, as well as machines. This appendix rebuilds their argument step by step, runs their regressions on their own data, and then asks whether the answer still holds with today’s data. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • derive the steady-state income equation of the textbook Solow model and turn it into a regression of income on the investment rate and population growth;
  • derive the steady state of the augmented Solow model, with physical and human capital, in its two forms: with the investment rate in human capital, and with its level;
  • estimate both models with OLS in statsmodels, test the restrictions that the theory puts on the coefficients, and recover the capital shares that the estimates imply;
  • explain conditional convergence, derive the speed of convergence of the augmented model, and estimate it from growth regressions;
  • use interactive simulations to see why human capital makes saving matter more and slows convergence;
  • rebuild MRW’s variables from the Penn World Table 11.0 and the World Bank, and check whether their results survive with new data, new countries and a new period.

F.2 Why augment the Solow model?

The Solow model of Chapter 5 says that a country’s income in the long run depends on a few numbers: how much it invests, how fast its population grows, how fast capital wears out, and its technology. Countries differ a lot in the first two. So a natural test of the model is to ask: do countries that invest more and whose populations grow more slowly have higher incomes, and by as much as the model says?

MRW ran this test on 98 countries in 1985. They found three things, which this appendix reproduces:

  1. The signs are right, the sizes are not. Countries that invest more are richer, and countries with fast population growth are poorer. But the effects in the data are about twice as large as the textbook model predicts with a capital share of one third.
  2. Human capital fixes the sizes. When workers’ skills are counted as a second kind of capital, the model predicts larger effects, and it explains about 80 percent of the differences in income across countries.
  3. Convergence is conditional. Poor countries do not grow faster than rich ones in general. But they do once we hold constant the things that set each country’s steady state, and at about the speed the augmented model predicts: some 2 percent of the gap closes each year.

The appendix fits between two others. Appendix D splits income gaps into capital, schooling and productivity without a theory of where capital comes from; here the Solow model supplies that theory. Appendix E studies convergence in depth; Section F.10 adds human capital to its speed of convergence.

F.3.1 Tools for regressions

We run many regressions in this appendix, each on three samples of countries, and we want to read them side by side, as in a published table. Four short functions do the work. reg_table puts several fitted regressions in one table: one column per regression, the coefficient with its standard error in parentheses, then the number of countries and the adjusted \(R^2\).

LABELS = {"Intercept": "Constant", "ln_s": "ln(s_K)", "ln_ngd": "ln(n+g+d)", "ln_school": "ln(SCHOOL)",
          "ln_s_ngd": "ln(s_K) − ln(n+g+d)", "ln_school_ngd": "ln(SCHOOL) − ln(n+g+d)",
          "ln_y60": "ln(y_1960)", "ln_hc": "ln(h)"}

def reg_table(models, names):
    """Coefficients (standard errors) of several regressions, side by side, plus N and adjusted R²."""
    rows = []
    for model in models:
        for term in model.params.index:
            if LABELS[term] not in rows:
                rows.append(LABELS[term])
    columns = {}
    for model, name in zip(models, names):
        cells = {}
        for term in model.params.index:
            cells[LABELS[term]] = f"{model.params[term]:.3f} ({model.bse[term]:.3f})"
        cells["N"] = str(int(model.nobs))
        cells["adjusted R²"] = f"{model.rsquared_adj:.2f}"
        columns[name] = cells
    return pd.DataFrame(columns).reindex(rows + ["N", "adjusted R²"]).fillna("")

Python note. A fitted statsmodels regression keeps its results as attributes: model.params holds the coefficients, model.bse their standard errors, model.nobs the number of observations and model.rsquared_adj the adjusted \(R^2\). params and bse are pandas Series indexed by the names of the variables, so model.params["ln_s"] is the coefficient on ln_s. zip(models, names) walks through two lists in step.

The other three tools test a restriction and turn coefficients into the economic parameters behind them. Each is explained in the section that first uses it.

def restriction_test(model, terms):
    """F-test that the coefficients on the listed terms add up to zero; returns F and its p-value."""
    test = model.f_test(" + ".join(terms) + " = 0")
    return float(np.squeeze(test.fvalue)), float(np.squeeze(test.pvalue))

def implied_shares(c_K, c_H=0.0):
    """Equations (3) and (7) turned around: the capital shares alpha and beta behind two coefficients."""
    alpha = c_K / (1 + c_K + c_H)
    beta = c_H / (1 + c_K + c_H)
    return alpha, beta

def implied_speed(b, T):
    """Equation (12): the speed of convergence λ and the half-life behind a slope b over T years."""
    speed = -math.log(1 + b) / T
    return speed, math.log(2) / speed

F.5 The textbook Solow model: from the steady state to a regression

This section derives the equation that MRW estimate first. It is the Solow model of Chapter 5, with population growth and technological progress. Bars mark parameters, and a hat marks a quantity per effective worker, as in Appendix E.

Assumptions

  1. Output is produced with a Cobb–Douglas function of capital and effective labour, \(AL\): \(Y=K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha}\), with capital share \(0<\alpha<1\).
  2. The labour force grows at rate \(\bar{n}\) and technology at rate \(\bar{g}\): \(L(t)=L(0)e^{\bar{n}t}\) and \(A(t)=A(0)e^{\bar{g}t}\).
  3. A constant share \(\bar{s}_K\) of output is invested in physical capital, which depreciates at rate \(\bar{d}\): \(\dot{K}=\bar{s}_K Y-\bar{d}K\). A dot over a variable is its change per unit of time.

MRW write \(\delta\) for depreciation and \(s\) for the investment rate. We keep the notation of the book.

Solving the model

Step 1: per effective worker. Define \(\hat{k}=K/(AL)\) and \(\hat{y}=Y/(AL)\). Divide the production function by \(AL\):

\[ \hat{y}=\frac{K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha}}{AL}=K^{\alpha}(AL)^{-\alpha}=\left(\frac{K}{AL}\right)^{\alpha}=\hat{k}^{\alpha}. \]

Step 2: the law of motion. The growth rate of a ratio is the growth rate of the numerator minus the growth rate of the denominator (Appendix C.5), so \(\dot{\hat{k}}/\hat{k}=\dot{K}/K-\bar{n}-\bar{g}\). With \(\dot{K}/K=\bar{s}_K Y/K-\bar{d}\) and \(Y/K=\hat{y}/\hat{k}\), multiplying through by \(\hat{k}\) gives

\[ \dot{\hat{k}}=\bar{s}_K\,\hat{k}^{\alpha}-(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\,\hat{k}. \tag{1} \]

Investment adds capital per effective worker. Depreciation, new workers and better technology all spread it more thinly.

Step 3: the steady state. At the steady state \(\dot{\hat{k}}=0\), so \(\bar{s}_K\hat{k}^{\alpha}=(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\hat{k}\). Divide by \(\hat{k}^{\alpha}\) and raise both sides to the power \(1/(1-\alpha)\):

\[ \hat{k}^*=\left(\frac{\bar{s}_K}{\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},\qquad \hat{y}^*=(\hat{k}^*)^{\alpha}=\left(\frac{\bar{s}_K}{\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}. \tag{2} \]

Step 4: back to income per worker. The data measure output per worker, \(Y/L=A\hat{y}\). Take logs, use \(\ln A(t)=\ln A(0)+\bar{g}t\), and the log of a power is the exponent times the log:

\[ \ln\frac{Y(t)}{L(t)}=\ln A(0)+\bar{g}t+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\ln\bar{s}_K-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\ln(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}). \tag{3} \]

What the model predicts

Equation (3) holds in each country’s steady state. Countries differ in \(\bar{s}_K\) and \(\bar{n}\), which we observe, and in \(A(0)\), which we do not: it reflects technology but also institutions, geography and climate. MRW write \(\ln A(0)=a+\varepsilon\), a common constant plus a country-specific term. In one year, equation (3) becomes a regression:

\[ \ln\frac{Y}{L}=a+c\,\ln\bar{s}_K-c\,\ln(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})+\varepsilon,\qquad c=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}. \]

The model makes three predictions about it:

Prediction Why
The coefficient on \(\ln\bar{s}_K\) is positive more investment, more capital per worker
The coefficient on \(\ln(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\) is negative, equal in size only the ratio \(\bar{s}_K/(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\) matters in equation (2)
Both are about 0.5 in size with \(\alpha=1/3\), the capital share in national accounts, \(c=(1/3)/(2/3)=0.5\)

Intuition. An elasticity of 0.5 means that a country that invests twice as much should be about \(2^{0.5}\approx1.41\) times as rich, all else equal. Diminishing returns keep the effect small: more capital raises output, but by less and less.

The regression is valid only under a strong assumption: the investment rate and population growth must be unrelated to \(\varepsilon\). MRW defend it as the standard assumption of the Solow model, in which saving and population growth are given from outside. Section F.14 returns to it.

The model in code

steady_state computes equation (2). We write it for the augmented model of Section F.6, which has a second capital share \(\beta\); with \(\beta=0\) it is exactly the textbook model. The baseline of every simulation in this appendix is \(\bar{s}_K=0.20\), \(\bar{s}_H=0.10\), \(\bar{n}=0.01\), \(\bar{g}=0.02\), \(\bar{d}=0.03\) and \(\alpha=\beta=1/3\).

def steady_state(s_K=0.20, s_H=0.10, n=0.01, alpha=1/3, beta=1/3, g=0.02, d=0.03):
    """Equations (2) and (6): k*, h* and y* per effective worker (beta = 0 is the textbook model)."""
    dilution = n + g + d
    k = (s_K ** (1 - beta) * s_H ** beta / dilution) ** (1 / (1 - alpha - beta))
    h = (s_K ** alpha * s_H ** (1 - alpha) / dilution) ** (1 / (1 - alpha - beta))
    return k, h, k ** alpha * h ** beta

simulate moves the economy forward one year at a time, with equation (1) and, in the augmented model, its twin for human capital. It starts from any capital stocks, so we can check that the economy really ends up at the steady state.

def simulate(s_K, s_H, k0, h0, n=0.01, alpha=1/3, beta=1/3, years=150, g=0.02, d=0.03):
    """Equations (1) and (5), year by year: paths of k, h and y per effective worker."""
    k = np.zeros(years + 1)
    h = np.zeros(years + 1)
    k[0], h[0] = k0, h0
    for t in range(years):
        y = k[t] ** alpha * h[t] ** beta
        k[t + 1] = k[t] + s_K * y - (n + g + d) * k[t]
        h[t + 1] = h[t] + s_H * y - (n + g + d) * h[t]
    return k, h, k ** alpha * h ** beta

Python note. np.zeros(years + 1) makes an array of zeros, one for each year, which the for loop fills in. k[t + 1] = ... writes next year’s value from this year’s. A function can return several results at once, and k, h, y = simulate(...) unpacks them.

F.7 The augmented Solow model: adding human capital

Workers are not all alike. A worker with a secondary education produces more than one without it, and schooling, like a machine, is built up by giving up consumption today. MRW therefore add a second kind of capital to the model: human capital \(H\), the stock of skills of the labour force.

Assumptions

  1. Output is produced with physical capital, human capital and effective labour: \[ Y=K^{\alpha}H^{\beta}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta},\qquad \alpha>0,\ \beta>0,\ \alpha+\beta<1. \tag{4} \] The condition \(\alpha+\beta<1\) means diminishing returns to all capital together: doubling both \(K\) and \(H\), with the same effective labour, less than doubles output.
  2. A share \(\bar{s}_K\) of output is invested in physical capital and a share \(\bar{s}_H\) in human capital.
  3. Both kinds of capital depreciate at the same rate \(\bar{d}\); \(L\) and \(A\) grow at \(\bar{n}\) and \(\bar{g}\), as before.

With \(\beta=0\) this is the textbook model.

Solving the model

Step 1: per effective worker. Divide equation (4) by \(AL\). The exponents of the denominator add up: \(AL=(AL)^{\alpha}(AL)^{\beta}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta}\), so

\[ \hat{y}=\frac{K^{\alpha}H^{\beta}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta}}{(AL)^{\alpha}(AL)^{\beta}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta}} =\left(\frac{K}{AL}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\frac{H}{AL}\right)^{\beta}=\hat{k}^{\alpha}\hat{h}^{\beta}. \]

Step 2: two laws of motion. The same steps that gave equation (1) give one equation for each kind of capital:

\[ \dot{\hat{k}}=\bar{s}_K\,\hat{y}-(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\,\hat{k},\qquad \dot{\hat{h}}=\bar{s}_H\,\hat{y}-(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\,\hat{h}. \tag{5} \]

Step 3: the ratio of the two capitals. At the steady state both changes are zero: \(\bar{s}_K\hat{y}=(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\hat{k}\) and \(\bar{s}_H\hat{y}=(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\hat{h}\). Divide the second by the first: \(\hat{h}/\hat{k}=\bar{s}_H/\bar{s}_K\). Each capital is in proportion to the share of output invested in it.

Step 4: solve for \(\hat{k}^*\). Put \(\hat{h}=(\bar{s}_H/\bar{s}_K)\hat{k}\) into the first condition, \(\bar{s}_K\hat{k}^{\alpha}\hat{h}^{\beta}=(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\hat{k}\):

\[ \bar{s}_K\,\hat{k}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{\bar{s}_H}{\bar{s}_K}\right)^{\beta}\hat{k}^{\beta}=(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\,\hat{k} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{k}^{1-\alpha-\beta}=\frac{\bar{s}_K^{1-\beta}\,\bar{s}_H^{\beta}}{\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}}. \]

Raising to the power \(1/(1-\alpha-\beta)\), and doing the same for \(\hat{h}\), gives the steady state:

\[ \hat{k}^*=\left(\frac{\bar{s}_K^{1-\beta}\,\bar{s}_H^{\beta}}{\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha-\beta}},\qquad \hat{h}^*=\left(\frac{\bar{s}_K^{\alpha}\,\bar{s}_H^{1-\alpha}}{\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha-\beta}}. \tag{6} \]

Step 5: steady-state income. Put both into \(\hat{y}^*=(\hat{k}^*)^{\alpha}(\hat{h}^*)^{\beta}\) and collect the exponent of each term. For \(\bar{s}_K\) it is \([\alpha(1-\beta)+\beta\alpha]/(1-\alpha-\beta)=\alpha/(1-\alpha-\beta)\); for \(\bar{s}_H\) it is \([\alpha\beta+\beta(1-\alpha)]/(1-\alpha-\beta)=\beta/(1-\alpha-\beta)\); for \(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}\) it is \(-(\alpha+\beta)/(1-\alpha-\beta)\). Back in income per worker, in logs:

\[ \ln\frac{Y}{L}=\ln A(0)+\bar{g}t+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\beta}\ln\bar{s}_K+\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha-\beta}\ln\bar{s}_H -\frac{\alpha+\beta}{1-\alpha-\beta}\ln(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}). \tag{7} \]

Step 6: the form with the level of human capital. We may observe the stock of human capital rather than the investment in it. Solve the condition for \(\hat{k}\) alone, for a given \(\hat{h}\): \(\hat{k}^*=\left(\bar{s}_K\hat{h}^{\beta}/(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\). Then \(\hat{y}=\hat{k}^{\alpha}\hat{h}^{\beta}\) has the exponent \(\alpha\beta/(1-\alpha)+\beta=\beta/(1-\alpha)\) on \(\hat{h}\), and

\[ \ln\frac{Y}{L}=\ln A(0)+\bar{g}t+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\ln\bar{s}_K-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\ln(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}) +\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\ln\hat{h}^*. \tag{8} \]

What the model predicts

  1. Saving matters more. With the same \(\alpha\), the effect of \(\bar{s}_K\) doubles. A country that invests more in machines is richer, so it also invests more in schooling (a fixed share of a larger income), which makes it richer still.
  2. Population growth matters much more. Fast population growth now dilutes both kinds of capital.
  3. The three coefficients of equation (7) add up to zero. This is the restriction we test in Section F.8.

F.11 Convergence: the theory and its speed

So far we have compared countries in their steady states. But a country that starts below its steady state grows faster than \(\bar{g}\) while it catches up: the principle of transition dynamics of Chapter 5. This section derives how fast the gap closes in the augmented model and turns the result into a regression.

Appendix E derives the speed of convergence of the textbook model and writes it as β. In this appendix β is the human capital share, as in MRW, so we write the speed as λ.

Solving the model

Step 1: logs. Write \(x=\ln\hat{k}\) and \(z=\ln\hat{h}\). Dividing each equation (5) by its capital, the growth rates are

\[ \dot{x}=\bar{s}_K\,e^{(\alpha-1)x+\beta z}-D,\qquad \dot{z}=\bar{s}_H\,e^{\alpha x+(\beta-1)z}-D, \qquad D\equiv\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}. \]

Step 2: straight lines near the steady state. As in Appendix E, replace each right-hand side by its tangent at the steady state \((x^*,z^*)\). At the steady state each exponential term equals \(D\), so the derivatives are simple:

\[ \begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}\\ \dot{z}\end{pmatrix}\approx D \begin{pmatrix}\alpha-1 & \beta\\ \alpha & \beta-1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}x-x^*\\ z-z^*\end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 3: the two speeds. A two-variable system like this one moves along two directions, each at its own speed, given by the eigenvalues of the matrix. Their sum is the trace, \(D(\alpha+\beta-2)\), and their product is the determinant, \(D^2[(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)-\alpha\beta]=D^2(1-\alpha-\beta)\). The two numbers with that sum and product are \(-D\) and \(-D(1-\alpha-\beta)\). The first, fast one, closes any gap in the ratio of the two capitals. The second, slow one, governs income once the ratio is right, and it sets the speed of convergence:

\[ \lambda=(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})(1-\alpha-\beta). \tag{9} \]

With \(\beta=0\) this is the textbook speed of Appendix E.

Step 4: the path of income. Log income per effective worker closes a share λ of its gap each year:

\[ \ln\hat{y}(t)-\ln\hat{y}^*=e^{-\lambda t}\left[\ln\hat{y}(0)-\ln\hat{y}^*\right], \tag{10} \]

and half of the gap is gone after \(\ln 2/\lambda\) years, the half-life.

What the model predicts

How fast? With \(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d}=0.06\) and \(\alpha=1/3\), the textbook model gives \(\lambda=0.06\times2/3=0.04\): half of the gap closes in about 17 years. With \(\alpha=\beta=1/3\), the augmented model gives \(\lambda=0.06\times1/3=0.02\) and a half-life of about 35 years. Human capital halves the speed, because diminishing returns to all capital together set in more slowly.

A regression. Subtract \(\ln\hat{y}(0)\) from both sides of equation (10) at \(t=T\), replace \(\ln\hat{y}^*\) by the right-hand side of equation (7), and move from effective workers to workers (the terms in \(A(0)\) and \(\bar{g}\) go into the constant \(a\)):

\[ \ln\frac{y(T)}{y(0)}=a+\left(1-e^{-\lambda T}\right)\left[\frac{\alpha\ln\bar{s}_K+\beta\ln\bar{s}_H-(\alpha+\beta)\ln(\bar{n}+\bar{g}+\bar{d})}{1-\alpha-\beta}-\ln y(0)\right]+\varepsilon. \tag{11} \]

Growth over \(T\) years depends on the determinants of the steady state and on initial income. The coefficient on \(\ln y(0)\) is \(b=-(1-e^{-\lambda T})\), which is negative. Turning it around gives the speed:

\[ \lambda=-\frac{\ln(1+b)}{T}. \tag{12} \]

This is what implied_speed computes.

Two kinds of convergence.

  • Unconditional (absolute) convergence: poor countries grow faster than rich ones, without any controls. The model predicts it only if all countries share the same steady state.
  • Conditional convergence: poor countries grow faster than rich ones with the same steady state, that is, holding constant \(\bar{s}_K\), \(\bar{s}_H\) and \(\bar{n}\). This is what the model predicts in general, and what equation (11) tests.

The model in code

The eigenvalue argument is an approximation near the steady state. We check it: start an economy below its steady-state income, with the two capitals in the steady-state ratio, simulate equation (5), and compare the year in which half of the log gap has closed with \(\ln 2/\lambda\). We try two starts: 80 percent of the steady state, and a quarter of it.

def start_below(fraction, alpha=1/3, beta=1/3, n=0.01):

def partial_out(d, column, controls):
    """The part of a column that the controls do not explain, plus its mean (Frisch-Waugh)."""
    if len(controls) == 0:
        return d[column]
    fit = smf.ols(column + " ~ " + " + ".join(controls), data=d).fit()
    return fit.resid + d[column].mean()

F.14.3 The regressions again

pwt_estimates runs, for one window, one sample and one measure of human capital, the restricted regressions of this appendix on the same set of countries: the textbook levels regression, the augmented levels regression (equation (7) with school, or equation (8) with the hc index) and the augmented convergence regression (equation (11)). It returns the implied α, β and λ and the fit. A sample with fewer than 15 economies is skipped.

HUMAN = {"ln_school": "School enrollment, eq. (7)", "ln_hc": "PWT hc index, eq. (8)"}
PWT_SAMPLES = {"MRW non-oil": "mrw_non_oil", "MRW intermediate": "mrw_intermediate", "MRW OECD": "mrw_oecd",
               "Full sample": "full"}
LABELS["ln_y0"] = "ln(initial income)"

def pwt_estimates(window, name, human):
    """Implied alpha, beta and λ in PWT 11.0, for one window, sample and measure of human capital."""
    d = pwt[(pwt["window"] == window) & (pwt[PWT_SAMPLES[name]] == 1)].dropna(subset=[human])
    row = {"window": window, "sample": name, "human capital": HUMAN[human], "N": len(d)}
    if len(d) < 15:
        return row
    terms = "ln_s_ngd + ln_school_ngd" if human == "ln_school" else "ln_s_ngd + ln_hc"
    textbook = smf.ols("ln_y ~ ln_s_ngd", data=d).fit()
    levels = smf.ols("ln_y ~ " + terms, data=d).fit()
    growth = smf.ols("growth ~ ln_y0 + " + terms, data=d).fit()
    c_K, c_H = levels.params[terms.split(" + ")[0]], levels.params[terms.split(" + ")[1]]
    if human == "ln_school":
        alpha, beta = implied_shares(c_K, c_H)
    else:
        alpha = c_K / (1 + c_K)                       # equation (8): c_K = alpha/(1-alpha), c_H = beta/(1-alpha)
        beta = c_H * (1 - alpha)
    row.update({"alpha, textbook": implied_shares(textbook.params["ln_s_ngd"])[0],
                "alpha": alpha, "beta": beta, "R², textbook": textbook.rsquared_adj,
                "R², augmented": levels.rsquared_adj,
                "speed (%)": 100 * implied_speed(growth.params["ln_y0"], int(window[-4:]) - int(window[:4]))[0]})
    return row

F.15 Go further

  • Is saving really unrelated to \(A(0)\)? The regressions assume that \(\bar{s}_K\) and \(\bar{n}\) are unrelated to the country’s technology and institutions. But a country with good institutions may both invest more and use its capital better, which would bias the coefficients up. Panel-data studies, such as Islam (1995), let each country have its own \(A(0)\) and find faster convergence and a smaller role for capital.
  • Better measures of human capital. SCHOOL measures the flow into secondary school, and the hc index the years of schooling, not what students learn. Hanushek and Woessmann (2012) use test scores and find that cognitive skills matter far more for growth than years in school.
  • How much is left for technology? Klenow and Rodríguez-Clare (1997) argue that MRW’s SCHOOL varies much more across countries than years of schooling do, which overstates the role of human capital. With better measures, differences in productivity explain about half of income differences, as the development accounting of Appendix D finds.
  • Is growth exogenous? Bernanke and Gürkaynak (2001) re-run MRW on later data and find that the levels results hold up, but that long-run growth rates are correlated with saving, which the Solow model, with its exogenous \(\bar{g}\), cannot explain. Chapter 6 turns to the models of ideas that can.

F.16 Concluding remarks

  • The textbook Solow model, equation (3), predicts the signs of the effects of saving and population growth on income, and they explain about 59 percent of income differences across countries in 1985. But the size of the effects implies a capital share of about 0.6, twice capital’s share of income.
  • The augmented model, equation (7), adds human capital. With \(\alpha=\beta=1/3\) it doubles the elasticity of income to saving (Fig. 2), and in MRW’s data it fits with α and β of about 0.3 each and explains almost 80 percent of income differences.
  • The model predicts conditional, not unconditional, convergence, at the speed of equation (9): about 2 percent a year with human capital, half the textbook speed (Figs. 5 and 7). MRW’s growth regressions, turned into speeds with equation (12), find 1.4 to 2.1 percent a year.
  • With PWT 11.0 and the WDI, for 1990–2023 and many more countries, the main results survive: a large textbook α, a β near 0.27 with MRW’s schooling measure, a fit of about three quarters and a speed of 1.3 to 2 percent. How we measure human capital matters, and PWT’s hc index gives implausibly large shares.
  • The model takes the investment rates, schooling and population growth as given, and leaves the technology term \(A(0)\) unexplained. Chapter 6 turns to the ideas behind technology, and Appendix D measures how much of income differences it accounts for.
