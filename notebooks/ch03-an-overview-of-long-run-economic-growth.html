3. An Overview of Long-Run Economic Growth

Learning goals

This chapter sets out the facts of economic growth and the tools we use to study them. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • describe how recent sustained growth is, and how unevenly it spread across countries;
  • define a growth rate and derive the constant growth rule \(y_t = y_0(1+\bar{g})^t\) by applying one year’s growth again and again, and explain why compounding makes the yearly increase grow;
  • take logs to show why a ratio scale draws constant growth as a straight line, and derive the exact doubling time and the Rule of 70 from it;
  • derive the formula for the average annual growth rate between any two years, explain why it is a geometric mean, and compute it from data;
  • derive the rules for the growth rates of products, ratios and powers from logarithms, apply them to the Cobb-Douglas production function, and read their comparative statics;
  • reshape, filter, combine and summarize data tables with pandas, and draw line charts, scatter plots and step charts with Plotly Express;
  • go beyond the textbook’s countries: pick any of about 170 countries and compare data from two sources.

Model overview

This chapter is about economic growth: the steady rise in income per person that has made people in many countries far richer than their great-grandparents. It presents facts rather than an economic model. But reading those facts takes a small toolkit: what a growth rate is, what a constant growth rate does to a number over many years, why a ratio scale turns that growth into a straight line, how long a variable takes to double, and how growth rates combine when variables are multiplied, divided or raised to a power. Every later chapter uses these tools, so this section derives each of them step by step.

There are four blocks, from the basic definition to the results built on it: growth rates and the constant growth rule; logarithms, the ratio scale and the Rule of 70; the average annual growth rate; and the rules for the growth rates of products, ratios and powers. At the end, ### The model in code turns the tools into Python functions and checks every result with numbers. The sections after it apply the tools to data: growth over two thousand years, U.S. growth on a ratio scale, growth across countries, poverty counted by people, and the growth rules in U.S. data.

A bar over a letter marks a value we hold constant and take as given, such as a constant growth rate \(\bar{g}\) for income or \(\bar{n}\) for population. A subscript is the year: \(y_t\) is the value of \(y\) in year \(t\), and \(y_0\) its value in the starting year. A growth rate without a bar, such as \(g_x\), may differ from one year to the next. In the code we simply write g and n.

Growth rates and the constant growth rule

Definitions

Let \(y_t\) be any variable that we measure once a year, such as GDP per person or the population. Its growth rate between year \(t\) and year \(t+1\) is its percentage change: the change in the variable divided by its value at the start (Appendix C.2),

\[g_t \equiv \frac{y_{t+1} - y_t}{y_t} = \frac{\Delta y_{t+1}}{y_t}.\]

A growth rate of \(0.02\) means 2 percent a year. Two features of this definition matter below. First, a growth rate has no units: GDP measured in dollars or in euros, or population counted in people or in billions, gives the same growth rate. Second, it measures change relative to the starting level. A country that adds $1,000 to an income of $10,000 grows by 10 percent; a country that adds the same $1,000 to an income of $50,000 grows by only 2 percent.

Deriving the results

Step 1: one year ahead. Suppose the growth rate takes the same value \(\bar{g}\) every year. Multiply both sides of the definition by \(y_t\), which gives \(y_{t+1} - y_t = \bar{g}\,y_t\), and then add \(y_t\) to both sides:

\[y_{t+1} = y_t + \bar{g}\,y_t = y_t\,(1+\bar{g}). \tag{1}\]

Next year’s value is this year’s value times the growth factor \(1+\bar{g}\). The 1 carries over everything that is already there, and the \(\bar{g}\) adds the new part, which is proportional to the current level. For a population \(L_t\) that grows at the rate \(\bar{n}\), equation (1) reads \(L_{t+1} = L_t(1+\bar{n})\): everyone alive this year is still counted next year, and \(\bar{n}L_t\) newcomers join them.

Step 2: one year after another. Start from \(y_0\) and apply equation (1) repeatedly, each time substituting the result of the previous year:

\[y_1 = y_0(1+\bar{g}), \qquad y_2 = y_1(1+\bar{g}) = y_0(1+\bar{g})(1+\bar{g}) = y_0(1+\bar{g})^2, \qquad y_3 = y_2(1+\bar{g}) = y_0(1+\bar{g})^2(1+\bar{g}) = y_0(1+\bar{g})^3.\]

Each year contributes one more factor \((1+\bar{g})\), and a product of equal factors is a power (Appendix C.3). After \(t\) years there are \(t\) factors:

\[y_t = y_0\,(1+\bar{g})^t. \tag{2}\]

This is the constant growth rule. The pattern is not a coincidence of the first three years. If equation (2) holds in some year \(t\), then equation (1) gives \(y_{t+1} = y_0(1+\bar{g})^t(1+\bar{g}) = y_0(1+\bar{g})^{t+1}\), so it also holds in year \(t+1\), and therefore in every year.

With the starting values of Fig. 3, a population of \(L_0 = 6\) billion that grows at \(\bar{n} = 0.02\), equation (2) gives \(L_{35} = 6 \times 1.02^{35} \approx 12.0\) billion and \(L_{70} = 6 \times 1.02^{70} \approx 24.0\) billion.

Step 3: why the yearly increase keeps getting bigger. Equation (1) says that the increase in a year is \(\Delta y_{t+1} = \bar{g}\,y_t\), a fixed percentage of a base that keeps growing. So the absolute increase is larger every year, and a chart of \(y_t\) against \(t\) bends upward. Compare this with simple growth, which adds the same amount \(\bar{g}\,y_0\) every year, so that \(y_t = y_0(1+\bar{g}t)\). Multiplying out the growth factor for two years shows where the two part ways:

\[(1+\bar{g})^2 = 1 + 2\bar{g} + \bar{g}^2.\]

The terms \(1 + 2\bar{g}\) are simple growth. The extra \(\bar{g}^2\) is growth earned in the second year on the growth of the first year. This is compounding, and over many years such extra terms pile up. At 2 percent for 35 years, simple growth multiplies the starting value by \(1 + 35 \times 0.02 = 1.7\), while compound growth doubles it: \(1.02^{35} \approx 2.0\).

Intuition. Under a constant growth rate, the growth itself grows. Each year’s increase becomes part of the base on which next year’s growth is earned.

What the results predict

  • Paths that bend upward. Under constant growth the level rises by a larger amount every year. That is why the population of Fig. 3 and U.S. GDP per person in Fig. 2 both curve upward on an ordinary scale, even though their growth rates hardly change.
  • Small differences in growth rates become large differences in levels. Take two economies that start at the same level \(y_0\). Writing equation (2) for both and dividing, \(y_0\) cancels: \(y^A_t/y^B_t = \left[(1+\bar{g}_A)/(1+\bar{g}_B)\right]^t\). Over 100 years, growth of 1 percent a year multiplies income by \(1.01^{100} \approx 2.7\), growth of 2 percent by \(1.02^{100} \approx 7.2\), and growth of 3 percent by \(1.03^{100} \approx 19.2\). Comparative statics. Taking logs of equation (2) (Appendix C.4) gives \(\ln y_t = \ln y_0 + t\ln(1+\bar{g})\). Each coefficient is an elasticity:
A change in … changes \(y_t\) by … reason
the starting value \(y_0\), up 1% \(+1\%\), in every year \(\ln y_0\) enters with coefficient 1
the growth factor \(1+\bar{g}\), up 1% about \(+t\) percent \(\ln(1+\bar{g})\) enters with coefficient \(t\)
the growth rate \(\bar{g}\), up 0.01 (one percentage point) \(\ln y_t\) rises by about \(0.01\,t/(1+\bar{g})\) the derivative of \(\ln(1+\bar{g})\) is \(1/(1+\bar{g})\) (Appendix C.7)
the horizon \(t\), longer any gap in growth rates matters more \(t\) multiplies \(\ln(1+\bar{g})\)

Raising growth from 2 to 3 percent for 100 years raises \(\ln y_t\) by about \(100 \times 0.01/1.02 \approx 0.98\), which multiplies the final level by about \(e^{0.98} \approx 2.7\) (exactly, \(1.03^{100}/1.02^{100} \approx 2.65\)). A change in the starting level shifts the whole path by the same percentage forever. A change in the growth rate has an effect that grows every year.

Logarithms, the ratio scale and the Rule of 70

Definitions

A ratio scale is a vertical axis whose equally spaced tick marks carry numbers with a constant ratio, such as 6, 12, 24, 48 (each twice the one before) or 1, 10, 100, 1,000 (each ten times the one before). On such an axis the height of a point is proportional to the logarithm of its value, not to the value itself. Moving up by one tick multiplies the value by the same factor, wherever on the axis you start. Plotly draws this kind of axis with log_y=True or type="log".

Deriving the results

Step 1: constant growth is a straight line in logs. Take the natural logarithm of both sides of equation (2). The log of a product is the sum of the logs, and the log of a power is the exponent times the log (Appendix C.4):

\[\ln y_t = \ln y_0 + t\,\ln(1+\bar{g}). \tag{3}\]

The right-hand side is a straight line in \(t\), with intercept \(\ln y_0\) and slope \(\ln(1+\bar{g})\). For small growth rates \(\ln(1+\bar{g}) \approx \bar{g}\) (Appendix C.6); for instance \(\ln 1.02 = 0.0198\). Between any two years \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) the slope, rise over run in logs, is

\[\frac{\ln y_{t_2} - \ln y_{t_1}}{t_2 - t_1} = \ln(1+\bar{g}) \approx \bar{g}.\]

So on a ratio scale, constant growth draws a straight line, and the slope of that line is (very nearly) the growth rate.

Step 2: the doubling time. How many years does it take until \(y_t = 2y_0\)? Put \(y_t = 2y_0\) into equation (2) and divide both sides by \(y_0\). The starting level drops out:

\[2y_0 = y_0(1+\bar{g})^t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2 = (1+\bar{g})^t.\]

The unknown sits in the exponent, so take logs of both sides, \(\ln 2 = t\,\ln(1+\bar{g})\), and divide by \(\ln(1+\bar{g})\):

\[t_{\text{double}} = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln(1+\bar{g})}. \tag{4}\]

Step 3: the Rule of 70. Equation (4) is exact, but hard to evaluate in your head. Two approximations make it easy: \(\ln 2 \approx 0.693\), and \(\ln(1+\bar{g}) \approx \bar{g}\). Write the growth rate in percent as \(g = 100\,\bar{g}\), so that \(\bar{g} = 0.02\) means \(g = 2\). Then

\[t_{\text{double}} \approx \frac{0.693}{\bar{g}} = \frac{69.3}{g} \approx \frac{70}{g}. \tag{5}\]

Why 70 and not 69.3? The log of \(1+\bar{g}\) is a little smaller than \(\bar{g}\) itself, about \(\bar{g} - \bar{g}^2/2\). Dividing by \(\bar{g}\) instead of by \(\ln(1+\bar{g})\) therefore makes the doubling time come out slightly too short, and rounding 69.3 up to 70 makes up for it at growth rates of a few percent. (It also divides evenly by 1, 2, 5, 7 and 10.) At 2 percent the exact answer is 35.0 years, \(69.3/2\) gives 34.7 and \(70/2\) gives 35.

The same steps give the time to grow by any factor \(m\): \(t = \ln m/\ln(1+\bar{g})\). And after \(k\) doubling times a variable is \(2^k\) times its starting value. At 2 percent, 70 years are two doubling times, so the variable grows by a factor of \(2^2 = 4\): the 6 billion people of Fig. 3 become 24 billion after 70 years.

Intuition. Logs turn multiplying into adding. Constant growth multiplies by the same factor every year, so in logs it adds the same amount every year, and that is a straight line.

What the results predict

  • The doubling time depends only on the growth rate. The starting level cancelled in step 2. A poor country and a rich country that grow at the same rate double their incomes in the same number of years.
  • Faster growth shortens the doubling time in proportion. By the Rule of 70 the doubling time is inversely proportional to the growth rate, an elasticity of \(-1\). Doubling the growth rate halves the doubling time; growth that is 10 percent faster, 2.2 instead of 2.0 percent a year, shortens it by about 9 percent, from 35 to about 32 years. How to read a ratio-scale chart.
On a ratio scale you see … it means … because …
a straight line constant growth equation (3) is linear in \(t\)
a steeper straight line faster growth the slope is \(\ln(1+\bar{g}) \approx \bar{g}\)
a line that bends upward (flattens) growth that speeds up (slows down) the slope changes

The average annual growth rate

Definitions

Real data rarely grow at a constant rate. Suppose we know a variable’s value \(y_0\) in a starting year and \(y_t\) in a year \(t\) years later. Its average annual growth rate \(\bar{g}\) is the constant growth rate that would take it from \(y_0\) to \(y_t\) in exactly \(t\) years.

Deriving the results

Step 1: turn the constant growth rule around. In equation (2) we knew \(y_0\) and \(\bar{g}\) and computed \(y_t\). Now \(y_0\), \(y_t\) and \(t\) are known and \(\bar{g}\) is the unknown. Divide both sides of equation (2) by \(y_0\):

\[\frac{y_t}{y_0} = (1+\bar{g})^t.\]

Step 2: undo the power. Raise both sides to the power \(1/t\). Since \(\left((1+\bar{g})^t\right)^{1/t} = (1+\bar{g})^{t/t} = 1+\bar{g}\) (Appendix C.3), we get \((y_t/y_0)^{1/t} = 1+\bar{g}\).

Step 3: subtract 1 from both sides:

\[\bar{g} = \left(\frac{y_t}{y_0}\right)^{1/t} - 1. \tag{6}\]

This is the rule for computing growth rates. For \(t = 1\) it gives \(y_1/y_0 - 1 = (y_1 - y_0)/y_0\), the definition of a growth rate we started from. Applied to the population path of Fig. 3, which goes from 6 billion to \(6 \times 1.02^{100} \approx 43.5\) billion in 100 years, it gives back \(\bar{g} = 7.245^{1/100} - 1 = 0.02\).

Step 4: the same rule in logs. Take logs of the equation in step 1 and divide by \(t\):

\[\ln(1+\bar{g}) = \frac{\ln y_t - \ln y_0}{t}.\]

The right-hand side is the slope, on a ratio scale, of the straight line that joins the first and the last point. The dashed line in Fig. 5 uses the same idea, but fits all the years at once.

Step 5: an average of growth factors. If the yearly growth rates \(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_t\) differ, applying equation (1) once for each year gives

\[y_t = y_0\,(1+g_1)(1+g_2)\cdots(1+g_t).\]

Compare this with equation (2): \((1+\bar{g})^t\) must equal the product of the yearly growth factors. So \(1+\bar{g}\) is their geometric mean, not the plain average of the growth rates. Here is why the difference matters. A variable that rises by 10 percent and then falls by 10 percent has a plain average growth rate of zero, yet it ends lower, because \(1.10 \times 0.90 = 0.99\). Equation (6) gets this right: \(0.99^{1/2} - 1 \approx -0.5\) percent a year.

Intuition. The average annual growth rate is the one steady pace that starts and ends at the same two points as the data.

What the results predict

  • Only the end points matter. Equation (6) uses \(y_0\), \(y_t\) and \(t\) and nothing in between. Booms and slumps inside the period count only through the level at which they leave the variable at the end. Comparative statics. From the log version, \(\ln(1+\bar{g}) = (\ln y_t - \ln y_0)/t\):
A change in … changes \(\ln(1+\bar{g})\), close to \(\bar{g}\), by …
the final value \(y_t\), up 1% about \(+0.01/t\)
the starting value \(y_0\), up 1% about \(-0.01/t\)
the length \(t\), longer, with the same ratio \(y_t/y_0\) less: the same total growth is spread over more years

Long-run growth rates are robust. Over 100 years, a 1 percent error in the final level moves the average growth rate by only about 0.01 percentage points. Over a single year, the same error moves it by a full percentage point. That is one reason why the growth rates of Fig. 5 and Fig. 7 are more reliable than any single year’s number.

Growth rates of products, ratios and powers

Definitions

Suppose two variables \(x\) and \(y\) grow at the rates \(g_x\) and \(g_y\). We want the growth rate \(g_z\) of a variable \(z\) built from them. One fact does all the work: the change in the log of a variable is approximately its growth rate. From the definition, \(v_{t+1}/v_t = 1+g_v\) for any variable \(v\), so

\[\Delta\ln v_{t+1} \equiv \ln v_{t+1} - \ln v_t = \ln\frac{v_{t+1}}{v_t} = \ln(1+g_v) \approx g_v.\]

Deriving the results

The product rule. Let \(z_t = x_t \times y_t\). Taking logs gives \(\ln z_t = \ln x_t + \ln y_t\). This holds in every year, so we can write it for year \(t+1\) and for year \(t\) and subtract the second from the first: \(\Delta\ln z = \Delta\ln x + \Delta\ln y\). Replacing each change in logs by the growth rate it approximates (Appendix C.5):

\[z = x \times y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_z = g_x + g_y. \tag{7}\]

The ratio rule. Let \(z_t = x_t/y_t\). Now \(\ln z_t = \ln x_t - \ln y_t\), and the same subtraction gives

\[z = \frac{x}{y} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_z = g_x - g_y. \tag{8}\]

The power rule. Let \(z_t = x_t^a\) for any number \(a\): positive, negative or a fraction. Now \(\ln z_t = a\ln x_t\), so \(\Delta\ln z = a\,\Delta\ln x\) and

\[z = x^a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_z = a \times g_x. \tag{9}\]

For a whole-number power you can also see it from the product rule: \(x^2 = x \times x\) grows at \(g_x + g_x = 2g_x\). Each rule replaces an operation by a simpler one, which is exactly what logs do: multiplying becomes adding, dividing becomes subtracting, and a power becomes a multiple.

How exact are the rules? With yearly data the product rule holds exactly for growth factors: \(1+g_z = (1+g_x)(1+g_y)\), so \(g_z = g_x + g_y + g_x g_y\). The leftover term is the product of two small numbers; at 2 and 1 percent it is \(0.02 \times 0.01 = 0.0002\). The same is true of the ratio rule, \(1+g_z = (1+g_x)/(1+g_y)\), and of the power rule, \(1+g_z = (1+g_x)^a\). The rules hold exactly for growth rates measured as changes in logs, or in continuous time.

Combining the rules. Joining (7) and (9), a variable \(z = x^a y^b\) grows at \(g_z = a\,g_x + b\,g_y\). The most important case in this book is the Cobb-Douglas production function of Chapter 4, \(Y_t = A_t K_t^{1/3} L_t^{2/3}\). The product rule (7), used twice for the three factors, gives \(g_Y = g_A + g_{K^{1/3}} + g_{L^{2/3}}\). The power rule (9) turns the last two terms into multiples:

\[g_Y = g_A + \tfrac{1}{3}\,g_K + \tfrac{2}{3}\,g_L. \tag{10}\]

Output per person is \(y = Y/L\), so the ratio rule (8) subtracts \(g_L\):

\[g_y = g_Y - g_L = g_A + \tfrac{1}{3}g_K + \tfrac{2}{3}g_L - g_L = g_A + \tfrac{1}{3}\left(g_K - g_L\right) = g_A + \tfrac{1}{3}\,g_k,\]

where the last step uses the ratio rule once more for capital per person, \(k = K/L\).

Intuition. Output grows as fast as productivity, plus the growth of each input weighted by its exponent. Per person, only productivity growth and the growth of capital per person are left, and capital per person counts only one third. Chapters 4 to 6 build on this decomposition.

What the results predict

  • Equal growth rates keep a ratio constant. If \(g_x = g_y\), the ratio rule gives \(g_{x/y} = 0\). On a ratio scale, two countries growing at the same rate draw parallel lines, as the United States and the United Kingdom do after 1950 in Fig. 6.
  • GDP, population and GDP per person. Total GDP is GDP per person times the population, \(Y = y \times L\), so \(g_Y = g_y + g_L\), or equivalently \(g_y = g_Y - g_L\). In the U.S. data of Fig. 9, GDP per person grew about 1.9 percent a year since 1870 and the population about 1.4 percent, so total GDP grew about 3.3 percent. Fast GDP growth with equally fast population growth leaves income per person where it was. productivity \(A\) 1 point 1 point
capital \(K\) 1/3 point 1/3 point
labor \(L\) 2/3 point \(-1/3\) point (the same capital is spread over more workers)

The model in code

We now turn the tools into Python functions. Each function’s description names the equation it computes. The cells after it check every result above with numbers.

Python note. def creates a function: a named recipe that takes inputs (the arguments) and gives back a result with return. The text in triple quotes is a short description of what the function does. ** means “to the power of”, and np.log is the natural logarithm.

def constant_growth(y0, g, t):
    """Value after t years of constant growth at rate g: y_t = y0 (1 + g)^t   (equation 2)."""
    return y0 * (1 + g) ** t


def growth_rate(y0, yt, t):
    """Average annual growth rate between two values t years apart: (yt / y0)^(1/t) - 1   (equation 6)."""
    return (yt / y0) ** (1 / t) - 1


def doubling_time(g):
    """Exact number of years it takes to double at growth rate g: ln 2 / ln(1 + g)   (equation 4)."""
    return np.log(2) / np.log(1 + g)

Python note. group_colors gives the countries their colors one at a time. def group_colors(names, groups, fixed=None):
    """Give each country a color that no other country in its groups uses, as far as the palette allows.

    groups: the lists of countries shown together (menu views); fixed: {country: color} for the book's countries."""
    colors = dict(fixed or {})
    for name in names:
        if name in colors:
            continue
        used = []                                       # the colors of the countries shown with `name`
        for group in groups:
            for other in group:
                if name in group and other in colors:
                    used.append(colors[other])
        free, unused = [], []
        for c in PALETTE:
            if c not in used:
                free.append(c)
                if c not in colors.values():
                    unused.append(c)                    # no country has this color yet: the best choice
        least = PALETTE[-1]
        for c in reversed(PALETTE):                     # no free color: the one used least, from the end
            if used.count(c) < used.count(least):
                least = c
        colors[name] = (unused + free + [least])[0]
    return colors

The mathematics of growth

Constant growth produces a curve that bends upward, which is hard to read. These figures show why, and how a ratio scale turns that curve into a straight line.

Fig. 3: Population over time

Goal. See what constant growth looks like when we plot it.

Suppose the world population starts at \(L_0 = 6\) billion and grows at a constant rate \(\bar{n} = 2\%\) a year. Each year the population is multiplied by \((1+\bar{n})\):

\[L_{t+1} = L_t\,(1+\bar{n})\]

This is equation (1) written for the population. Applying it year after year gives \(L_t = L_0(1+\bar{n})^t\) (equation 2). The cell below checks this with a loop: it multiplies by \(1.02\) one hundred times and compares the result with the formula.

Fig. 4: Population over time, revisited

Goal. Learn to read a ratio scale, on which constant growth is a straight line.

At 2 percent a year, the population doubles about every 35 years (Rule of 70: \(70/2 = 35\)). It goes from 6 to 12, 24 and 48 billion. On a ratio scale, the vertical axis is labeled 6, 12, 24, 48: each step doubles the one before. Equal distances then mean equal percentage changes, so a constant growth rate draws a straight line.

Python note. make_subplots(rows=1, cols=2, ...) makes a figure with two panels side by side, and row=1, col=2 sends a trace to the second one. np.arange(0, 106, T) gives 0, T, 2T, … up to 105. On a ratio-scale axis, the range is given in powers of ten: np.log10(150).

Rules for growth rates

Last, three handy rules: the growth rate of a product is a sum, of a ratio a difference, and of a power a multiple. We check them with U.S. data.

Fig. 9: U.S. population, GDP and per capita GDP

Goal. See the rules for growth rates at work.

Three rules make growth rates easy to work with (equations 7 to 9). If \(x\) grows at rate \(g_x\) and \(y\) at rate \(g_y\), then

  • the product \(z = x \times y\) grows at \(g_x + g_y\);
  • the ratio \(z = x / y\) grows at \(g_x - g_y\);
  • the power \(z = x^a\) grows at \(a \times g_x\).

Total GDP is GDP per person times population, so its growth rate is the sum of the two growth rates. To see this, the chart divides each series by its value in the first year, so all three lines start at 1. For the United States we use the official series of the textbook figure. For any other country we compute total GDP from the Maddison Project’s GDP per person and population.

Python note. start, end = period unpacks a pair of years into two variables. Dividing the table table.loc[start:end] by its first row divides every column by its own first value. Concluding remarks

  • Sustained growth in living standards is very recent. It began about 200 years ago and spread unevenly, which produced the Great Divergence in incomes across countries.
  • A growth rate is a percentage change. Growing at a constant rate, a variable is multiplied by \(1+\bar{g}\) each year, equation (1), so after \(t\) years it equals \(y_t = y_0(1+\bar{g})^t\), the constant growth rule, equation (2). Compounding makes the yearly increase grow, and small differences in growth rates become large gaps in levels.
  • In logs, constant growth is a straight line, equation (3), so on a ratio scale the slope is the growth rate. A variable doubles in exactly \(\ln 2/\ln(1+\bar{g})\) years, equation (4), or in about \(70/g\) years, the Rule of 70, equation (5). U.S. GDP per person has grown at close to 2 percent a year for more than 150 years.
  • The average annual growth rate between two years, equation (6), is the one constant rate that links the two end points: a geometric mean of the yearly growth factors.
  • Growth rates differ a lot across countries, and small differences compound into huge income gaps. Counting people rather than countries, poverty has fallen dramatically, mainly because of growth in China and India.
  • Growth rates of products are sums, of ratios are differences, and of powers are multiples, equations (7) to (9). Applied to the production function, they split output growth into productivity growth plus weighted input growth, equation (10).
  • The next chapter builds a first model to explain why some countries are so much richer than others.
