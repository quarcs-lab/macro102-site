10. The Great Recession and the Covid-19 Pandemic: A First Look

Learning goals

Two shocks hit the world economy in less than 15 years: the global financial crisis of 2008–09 and the Covid-19 pandemic of 2020. This chapter looks at both in the data. By the end of it you will be able to:

  • tell the story of the financial crisis with data: the housing bubble, the Fed’s interest rate, the spreads between interest rates and the fall of the stock market;
  • compare the Covid-19 recession across countries, in lives lost and in GDP lost;
  • measure how deep the two recessions were (employment, short-run output, unemployment, Table 10.1) and describe the inflation that followed the pandemic;
  • derive the measurement tools behind the figures: average growth rates, the climb back after a crash, short-run output, the cumulative GDP loss, and the gap between log changes and percent changes;
  • use a balance sheet to derive how leverage magnifies returns, how small a fall in asset prices wipes out net worth, and why losses force asset sales; solve a simple model of a bank run;
  • read an interest rate spread as lenders’ fear of default, split a fall in GDP into the contributions of its components, and read the comparative statics of each result;
  • in Python: find peaks and troughs with idxmax and idxmin, shade recessions with one small function, reshape a table with melt, compare two vintages of the same data, and turn log changes into percent changes;
  • go beyond the textbook’s countries: pick any of up to 61 economies from long (tidy) data files, and spot breaks, gaps and changes of definition in international data.

Model overview

This chapter has no new model to solve. It follows two recent recessions through the data: the global financial crisis of 2007–09, which began in the housing market and spread through the banks to the whole economy, and the Covid-19 pandemic of 2020, a sudden stop that was deeper but far shorter. What the chapter does add is a set of analytical tools. Some are measurement tools that every figure relies on: how far a series fell, how fast it grew, how long it took to climb back. Others come from financial economics. They explain how a fall in house prices of about a third could bring large banks to the edge of collapse: balance sheets, leverage, bank runs and interest rate spreads.

This section writes out each tool as a small framework, in the same three steps we use for a model: the definitions it starts from, the algebra that turns them into a result, and what the result predicts. We move from measurement to finance to macroeconomic outcomes: (i) booms, busts and recoveries; (ii) balance sheets and leverage; (iii) bank runs; (iv) interest rate spreads; (v) short-run output and the cost of a slump; (vi) the components of GDP in a recession; and (vii) the value of the lives the pandemic took. Measuring booms, busts and recoveries

Definitions

Let \(X_t\) be any series observed at date \(t\): a price index, GDP, employment. Most figures compare an earlier date \(s\) with a later date \(t\). The percent change between them is

\[\%\Delta X = 100\times\left(\frac{X_t}{X_s}-1\right). \tag{1}\]

  • A peak is the highest value inside a window of dates, and a trough is the lowest value after that peak. The fall from peak to trough is equation (1) with \(s\) the peak and \(t\) the trough, so it is a negative number.
  • The drawdown at date \(t\) is the percent change from the highest value reached so far, \(100\times\left(X_t/\max_{s\le t}X_s - 1\right)\). It is zero at every new record (Fig. 1).
  • For a series that is itself a rate, such as the unemployment rate \(u_t\), an interest rate or an inflation rate, we report the difference \(u_t - u_s\) in percentage points. Unemployment that goes from 4 to 6 percent has risen by 2 points, but by 50 percent.
  • The NBER dates each recession by a peak, the last month of the expansion, and a trough, the last month of the recession. The number of months between two dates is \(12\times(\text{difference in years}) + (\text{difference in months})\).

Solving: four results we use again and again

Step 1: the average growth rate over many years. Suppose \(X\) grew at the same rate \(g\) in each of \(T\) years. Then \(X_T = X_0(1+g)^T\). Divide both sides by \(X_0\) and raise them to the power \(1/T\), using \(\left((1+g)^T\right)^{1/T} = 1+g\) (Appendix C.3):

\[g = \left(\frac{X_T}{X_0}\right)^{1/T}-1. \tag{2}\]

Logs give a handy shortcut. Since \(\ln(X_T/X_0) = T\ln(1+g)\) and \(\ln(1+g)\approx g\) when \(g\) is small (Appendix C.6), \(g \approx \ln(X_T/X_0)/T\). In our data, real U.S. house prices were 1.72 times higher in 2006 than in 1996 (Fig. 1). Equation (2) gives \(1.72^{1/10}-1 \approx 5.6\) percent a year, and the shortcut gives \(\ln 1.72/10 \approx 5.4\) percent. By the rule of 70, a price that grows at that pace doubles in about \(70/5.6 \approx 12.5\) years.

Step 2: the climb back after a fall. If a series falls by the fraction \(f\) from its peak, the trough is \(X_{\text{trough}} = (1-f)X_{\text{peak}}\). To get back to the peak, the series must be multiplied by \(X_{\text{peak}}/X_{\text{trough}} = 1/(1-f)\). Written as a percentage, and putting both terms over the common denominator \(1-f\), the rise needed is

\[\text{rise needed} = 100\times\left(\frac{1}{1-f}-\frac{1-f}{1-f}\right) = 100\times\frac{f}{1-f}. \tag{3}\]

After a small fall, the climb back is about as large as the fall. After a large fall it is much larger: a series that has lost half its value must double to recover.

Step 3: why a ratio scale. Figs. 1 and 4 use a ratio (log) scale. On it, the vertical distance between two values is \(\ln X_t - \ln X_s = \ln(X_t/X_s)\) (Appendix C.4), which depends only on their ratio. A fall by one half and a rise of 100 percent cover the same distance, \(\ln 2 \approx 0.69\). That is why busts of different decades, at very different price levels, can be compared by eye.

Step 4: log changes and percent changes. The inflation data of Fig. 10 measure the 12-month change in the price level \(P_t\) as a log change, \(\pi^{\log}_t = 100\ln(P_t/P_{t-12})\), while the usual inflation rate is the percent change \(\pi_t = 100\times(P_t/P_{t-12}-1)\). To go from the first to the second, divide the log change by 100 and undo the log with the exponential function, \(P_t/P_{t-12} = e^{\pi^{\log}_t/100}\). Substituting this ratio into the percent change gives

\[\pi_t = 100\times\left(e^{\pi^{\log}_t/100}-1\right). \tag{4}\]

How large is the difference? The exponential function can be written as \(e^x = 1 + x + x^2/2 + \dots\), so \(\pi_t \approx \pi^{\log}_t + (\pi^{\log}_t)^2/200\). The gap grows with the square of inflation: about 0.02 points at 2 percent inflation, but about 0.4 points at 9 percent.

  • Percent changes do not add up over time, but log changes do. A fall of 50 percent followed by a rise of 50 percent leaves a series 25 percent below its start, and indeed \(\ln 0.5 + \ln 1.5 = \ln 0.75\).
  • When inflation is low, log and percent changes are interchangeable. When it is high, as in 2022, the choice moves the peaks by about half a point (Fig. 10).

Balance sheets and leverage

Definitions and assumptions

A balance sheet is a snapshot, on one date, of what an institution owns and what it owes. It works the same way for a bank, a firm, a household or a government.

  • Assets \(A\) are what it owns (in this chapter \(A\) stands for assets, not for productivity). For a bank: loans, securities and other investments, and cash and reserves.
  • Liabilities \(D\) (for debt) are what it owes to others. For a bank: deposits, short-term debt and long-term debt.
  • Net worth \(E\), also called equity or capital, is what is left for the owners: assets minus liabilities. Because net worth is defined as that difference, the two sides of a balance sheet always match. The leverage ratio \(\ell\) counts the dollars borrowed per dollar of the owners’ own money:

\[A = D + E, \qquad \ell \equiv \frac{D}{E}. \tag{5}\]

Regulators watch two more ratios. The capital ratio \(E/A\) is the share of assets paid for by the owners, and a capital requirement sets a floor for it, for example 6 percent. The reserve ratio is cash and reserves divided by deposits, and a reserve requirement sets a floor for it, for example 3 percent.

The experiments below rest on one assumption: liabilities are promises to repay fixed dollar amounts, while the market value of assets moves with house prices, stock prices and defaults.

Our running example is the hypothetical bank of the textbook’s Table 10.3, in billions of dollars. Its assets are loans of 1,000, investments of 900 and cash and reserves of 100, so \(A = 2{,}000\). It owes 1,000 in deposits, 400 in short-term debt and 400 in long-term debt, so \(D = 1{,}800\) and \(E = 200\). Equation (5) gives \(\ell = 1{,}800/200 = 9\). Its capital ratio is \(200/2{,}000\) and its reserve ratio \(100/1{,}000\), both 10 percent, above the two floors.

Solving

Step 1: leverage and the capital ratio carry the same information. Divide both sides of \(A = D + E\) by \(E\):

\[\frac{A}{E} = \frac{D}{E} + 1 = 1 + \ell, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{E}{A} = \frac{1}{1+\ell}.\]

With \(\ell = 9\), every 10 dollars of assets rest on 1 dollar of equity. A capital requirement is therefore a ceiling on leverage: \(E/A \ge 0.06\) means \(1+\ell \le 1/0.06\), or \(\ell \le 15.7\).

Step 2: leverage magnifies returns. Let the value of the assets change by \(\Delta A\). The liabilities do not move, \(\Delta D = 0\), so the change in \(A = D + E\) is \(\Delta E = \Delta A\): every dollar gained or lost lands on the owners. Divide by \(E\), then multiply and divide by \(A\):

\[\frac{\Delta E}{E} = \frac{\Delta A}{E} = \frac{A}{E}\times\frac{\Delta A}{A} = (1+\ell)\,\frac{\Delta A}{A}. \tag{6}\]

The owners’ percent return is \(1+\ell\) times the percent return on all the assets. For our bank \(1+\ell = 10\), so a 1 percent gain on its assets, 20 billion dollars, raises its equity by 10 percent. When only one item changes, measure its change relative to total assets: a gain of 100 billion on the investments is \(100/2{,}000 = 5\) percent of all assets, and so 50 percent for the owners. Households work the same way. A buyer who pays 20 percent of the price of a house and borrows the other 80 percent has \(E/A = 0.2\), so \(1+\ell = 5\): each 1 percent change in the price of the house moves the owner’s equity by 5 percent, up or down.

Intuition. Lenders are paid first, and they are paid a fixed amount. The owners keep whatever is left: a thin layer on top of a large pile of debt. A small change in the size of the whole pile is a large change in the thickness of that layer.

Step 3: how small a fall wipes out the owners. Let the value of the assets fall by the fraction \(f\), so that \(A\) becomes \((1-f)A\) while \(D\) stays the same. Net worth becomes

\[E' = (1-f)A - D = (A - D) - fA = E - fA.\]

The owners are wiped out, \(E' \le 0\), as soon as \(fA \ge E\), that is, once the fall reaches

\[f^* = \frac{E}{A} = \frac{1}{1+\ell}. \tag{7}\]

Beyond that point the bank is insolvent, or bankrupt: even if it sold everything at market prices, it could not repay what it owes. With \(\ell = 9\), \(f^*\) is 10 percent. Bear Stearns had a leverage of about 35 when it collapsed in 2008, so \(f^* = 1/36\), about 2.8 percent.

Step 4: losses force sales (deleveraging). After a loss of \(x\) dollars, assets are \(A - x\) and equity is \(E - x\), so leverage jumps to \(D/(E-x)\), above \(\ell\). Suppose the bank wants, or its regulators require, to bring leverage back to \(\ell\), and it cannot raise new equity. Then its assets must shrink to \((1+\ell)(E-x)\): it sells assets and uses the cash to repay debt. The sales needed are the assets left after the loss minus that target. Expanding, and using \(A = (1+\ell)E\) from Step 1:

\[(A - x) - (1+\ell)(E - x) = A - x - (1+\ell)E + (1+\ell)x = \ell\,x. \tag{8}\]

Each dollar of losses forces \(\ell\) dollars of asset sales: 9 for our bank, 35 for Bear Stearns.

Intuition. When many leveraged banks sell the same kind of assets at the same time, the prices of those assets fall further. That causes new losses, and equation (8) turns them into new sales. Through this loop, losses on subprime mortgages, small next to the total wealth of the United States, could shake the whole financial system.

What the model predicts

Higher leverage \(\ell\) means … formula sign at \(\ell = 9\) at \(\ell = 35\)
a bigger multiplier on the owners’ returns \(1+\ell\), equation (6) \(+\) 10 36
a smaller fall that wipes out equity \(1/(1+\ell)\), equation (7) \(-\) 10 percent 2.8 percent
more forced sales per dollar of losses \(\ell\), equation (8) \(+\) 9 35
  • As an elasticity, equation (6) says that net worth responds to the value of assets with elasticity \(A/E = 1+\ell\). Taking logs of equation (7), \(\ln f^* = -\ln(1+\ell)\): doubling \(1+\ell\) halves the cushion.
  • The multiplier works in both directions. When asset prices rise, a 5 percent gain becomes 50 percent for the owners; when they fall, the same multiplier turns small losses into bankruptcies.
  • Households are exposed in the same way. With an 80 percent mortgage, \(\ell = 4\), and equation (7) says a fall of 20 percent in the price of the house wipes out the owner’s equity; any further fall leaves the house worth less than the mortgage. Lost housing wealth helps explain why consumption fell in the Great Recession (see the components of GDP below).
  • Who holds the losses matters. Losses spread over millions of unleveraged shareholders, like the dot-com crash of 2000–03, hurt but do not bankrupt anyone; similar losses concentrated in a few highly leveraged banks do (Fig. 4).

Bank runs and liquidity crises

Assumptions

  • The bank’s assets come in two kinds. Liquid assets, cash and reserves \(R\), can be paid out at full value at once. Illiquid assets, the loans and most investments, are worth their full value if the bank keeps them. Sold in a hurry, each dollar of them brings only \(1-\delta\) dollars of cash, where the fire-sale discount \(\delta\) is between 0 and 1.
  • Depositors may take their money out at any time. Suppose they withdraw \(W\) dollars.

Solving the model

If \(W \le R\), the bank pays out of its cash and nothing else happens. If \(W > R\), it needs \(W - R\) more cash, so it sells illiquid assets with a book value \(S\) such that \((1-\delta)S = W - R\). Solving for \(S\) gives \(S = (W-R)/(1-\delta)\). The bank gives up assets worth \(S\) but receives only \((1-\delta)S\) in cash, so it loses the difference \(\delta S\):

\[\text{loss} = \delta S = \frac{\delta\,(W-R)}{1-\delta}. \tag{9}\]

The loss comes out of net worth, which falls to \(E - \delta(W-R)/(1-\delta)\). The run bankrupts the bank when the loss is larger than \(E\). We can solve this condition in two directions.

  • How large a discount bankrupts the bank in a full run? If every depositor leaves, \(W\) is equal to total deposits. Set the loss equal to \(E\) and solve for \(\delta\): \(\delta(W-R) = E(1-\delta)\), so \(\delta(W-R+E) = E\) and \[\delta^* = \frac{E}{E + W - R}.\] For our bank, \(\delta^* = 200/(200 + 1{,}000 - 100) = 200/1{,}100\), about 18 percent.
  • How large a run bankrupts the bank at a given discount? Solve the same condition for \(W\) instead: \(W^* = R + E(1-\delta)/\delta\). With a discount of one quarter, \(W^* = 100 + 200\times 0.75/0.25 = 700\). A run on 70 percent of the deposits is enough.

What the model predicts

  • Runs can be self-fulfilling. At normal prices the bank is solvent. But if each depositor expects the others to run, running first is the best thing to do, and the forced sales then make the bank insolvent. The same bank survives or fails depending only on what depositors expect.
  • Illiquid is not the same as insolvent. Without a run, \(\delta\) never matters. The fire-sale discount is what turns a liquidity problem into a solvency problem.
  • Comparative statics. A run is harder to trigger, \(W^*\) is larger, when the bank holds more liquid assets \(R\), has more equity \(E\) or faces a smaller discount \(\delta\). Each policy works on one of these: capital requirements raise \(E\), reserve requirements raise \(R\), and a central bank that lends against illiquid assets, the lender of last resort, spares the bank the fire sale. Deposit insurance, run by the FDIC in the United States since 1933, takes away the reason to run in the first place.
  • In 2007–08 the runs hit mostly the short-term debt of banks and investment banks. Unlike insured deposits, those loans could simply not be renewed. When lenders pull back across a whole market at once, the volume of lending dries up: a liquidity crisis.

Interest rate spreads: a thermometer of fear

Definitions

An interest rate spread is the difference between the interest rate on a risky loan, \(i\), and the rate on a safe one, \(i^f\), measured in percentage points. The TED spread of Fig. 3 is the 3-month LIBOR rate, at which banks lent to one another, minus the 3-month Treasury bill rate, at which the U.S. government borrows.

Solving: what a spread measures

A lender with one dollar can buy a Treasury bill, which pays \(1 + i^f\) for sure. Or it can lend to another bank, which pays \(1+i\) if the borrower survives, with probability \(1-p\), and in the simplest case nothing if it fails. A lender who cares only about the expected payoff is willing to lend to the bank when the two options pay the same on average:

\[(1-p)(1+i) = 1+i^f.\]

Divide by \(1-p\), subtract \(1+i^f\) from both sides and factor out \(1+i^f\):

\[i - i^f = \frac{1+i^f}{1-p} - (1+i^f) = (1+i^f)\left(\frac{1}{1-p}-1\right) = \frac{(1+i^f)\,p}{1-p} \approx p. \tag{10}\]

The approximation holds when \(p\) and \(i^f\) are both small, so that \(1+i^f \approx 1\) and \(1-p\approx 1\). The spread is then roughly the probability of default, as lenders see it. A spread of a third of a point, typical of the calm years before 2007, corresponds to a perceived default risk of about 0.3 percent a year. The monthly peak of the TED spread, about 3.35 points in October 2008, corresponds to about 3.2 percent: ten times more fear.

What the model predicts

  • The spread rises almost one for one with perceived default risk: its slope with respect to \(p\) is \((1+i^f)/(1-p)^2\), close to 1.
  • If lenders get part of their money back when a borrower fails, the spread is smaller for the same \(p\). If they dislike risk, they ask for more than equation (10). Either way the spread moves with the fear of default.
  • Flight to safety. When fear rises, lenders leave interbank loans for Treasury bills. That pushes \(i^f\) down and \(i\) up, so the spread widens from both sides.
  • Banks that worry about their own funding also hoard cash rather than lend it, which adds a liquidity premium on top of the default premium. A spread that jumps is a sign of a liquidity crisis (Fig. 3).

Short-run output and the cost of a slump

Definitions

Potential output \(\bar{Y}_t\) is the GDP the economy would produce with its resources in normal use; for the United States the Congressional Budget Office estimates it. Short-run output (Chapter 9) is the percent gap between actual GDP \(Y_t\) and potential:

\[\tilde{Y}_t \equiv \frac{Y_t - \bar{Y}_t}{\bar{Y}_t}, \tag{11}\]

shown in percent in the charts. Rearranged, \(Y_t = \bar{Y}_t(1+\tilde{Y}_t)\): a value of \(-0.05\) means that GDP is 5 percent below potential. The OECD calls the same measure the output gap.

Solving

Step 1: in logs. Take logs of \(Y_t = \bar{Y}_t(1+\tilde{Y}_t)\) and use \(\ln(1+x)\approx x\) (Appendix C.6):

\[\ln Y_t = \ln\bar{Y}_t + \ln(1+\tilde{Y}_t) \approx \ln\bar{Y}_t + \tilde{Y}_t.\]

Short-run output is approximately log GDP minus log potential output.

Step 2: growth and the gap. Write this equation for two consecutive years and subtract. A difference in logs is a growth rate (Appendix C.5), so

\[g_{Y,t} \approx \bar{g}_t + \Delta\tilde{Y}_t,\]

where \(\bar{g}_t\) is the growth rate of potential output. To close a negative gap, GDP must grow faster than potential. Closing a gap of 5 percent in five years takes growth one point a year above potential, every year. Growth only a little above potential closes the gap slowly, which is why \(\tilde{Y}\) stayed negative for most of the 2010s (Fig. 8).

Step 3: the cumulative loss. Fig. 5 measures the pandemic’s GDP loss against the pre-pandemic level \(Y_0\) of 2019Q4, not against potential. Write real GDP in quarter \(q\) as an index, \(I_q = 100\,Y_q/Y_0\). Quarterly GDP is reported at an annual rate, so a quarter produces one fourth of that rate. The output lost in quarter \(q\), as a share of one year’s GDP, is therefore \((Y_0 - Y_q)/(4Y_0) = (100 - I_q)/400\), or \((100 - I_q)/4\) in percent. Quarters above the old level count as no loss. Adding up from 2020Q1:

\[\text{GDP loss} = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{q}\max\left(0,\;100 - I_q\right), \tag{12}\]

in percent of one year’s GDP. The same sum with \(-100\,\tilde{Y}_q\) in place of \(100 - I_q\) measures the loss against potential output instead.

What it predicts

  • Depth times duration. If output drops by \(d\) percent at once and climbs back in a straight line over \(T\) years, the sum in equation (12) is close to the area of a triangle, \(\tfrac{1}{2}dT\). A slump twice as deep costs twice as much, and so does a slump twice as long.
  • So a deep but short slump can cost less than a moderate but long one. Against potential, the Great Recession pushed \(\tilde{Y}\) to about \(-5\) percent and kept it below zero for about nine years: a triangle of about \(\tfrac{1}{2}\times5\times9 \approx 22\) percent of a year’s GDP. The 2020 slump, near \(-9\) percent but over within about a year, gives a triangle of about 4. ### The model in code computes the exact sums with the data.
  • Potential output is an estimate, and GDP is revised for years. Both change \(\tilde{Y}\), as the two vintages of Fig. 8 show.

The components of GDP in a recession

Definitions

The national income identity of Chapter 2 splits GDP into consumption, investment, government purchases, exports and imports:

\[Y_t = C_t + I_t + G_t + EX_t - IM_t.\]

The share of a component \(X\) in GDP is \(X/Y\), measured at the peak of the recession.

Solving

Between the peak and the trough the identity holds in changes: \(\Delta Y = \Delta C + \Delta I + \Delta G + \Delta EX - \Delta IM\). Divide both sides by \(Y\), and write each term as a share times a percent change, for example \(\Delta C/Y = (C/Y)\times(\Delta C/C)\):

\[\frac{\Delta Y}{Y} = \frac{C}{Y}\frac{\Delta C}{C} + \frac{I}{Y}\frac{\Delta I}{I} + \frac{G}{Y}\frac{\Delta G}{G} + \frac{EX}{Y}\frac{\Delta EX}{EX} - \frac{IM}{Y}\frac{\Delta IM}{IM}. \tag{13}\]

Each term is a contribution to GDP growth, in percentage points: the component’s share times its own percent change. Table 10.1 reports the percent changes; the contributions show which of them moved GDP the most.

What it predicts

  • A small component that swings a lot can matter more than a large one that barely moves. Investment is only about a sixth of GDP, but it fell by almost 30 percent between the quarterly peak and trough of the Great Recession. Its contribution, close to \(-5\) points, is larger than the whole fall in GDP. Consumption is two thirds of GDP, so even its fall of about 2 percent subtracted about 1.5 points.
  • Imports enter with a minus sign. A fall in imports adds to GDP growth, because part of what households and firms stopped buying had been made abroad.
  • In most recessions consumption hardly moves, because households smooth consumption: they base it on their long-run (permanent) income rather than on this year’s income (Chapter 16). In 2008–09 the falls in house and stock prices cut household wealth, and with it permanent income, so consumption fell. In 2020 people stayed away from shops, restaurants and travel, and consumption led the fall.
  • Real GDP is measured in chained dollars, which do not add up exactly, so the contributions miss GDP growth by a small residual.

Lives lost and their value

Definitions

  • Deaths are counted per million people, \(m\). The mortality rate is \(m/10^6\), and “one out of every \(X\) people” means \(X = 10^6/m\).
  • The value of a statistical life \(V\) is the amount society is willing to pay, on average, to avoid one death. Agencies such as the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency use it to weigh safety rules against their cost. A person of middle age has about \(T\) years of life ahead.

Solving

The simplest approach spreads \(V\) evenly over the remaining years, so a year of life is worth \(v = V/T\). Losing \(\Delta\) years of life expectancy then costs about \(\Delta\times v\) per person (the key numbers of Fig. 5).

Years far in the future usually count for less (Appendix C.12). If \(V\) is the present value of \(T\) equal yearly values \(v\), discounted at the rate \(r\), then by the sum of a geometric series

\[V = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{v}{(1+r)^t} = v\,\frac{1-(1+r)^{-T}}{r}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad v = \frac{r\,V}{1-(1+r)^{-T}}.\]

With \(V = 10\) million dollars, \(T = 40\) years and \(r = 0.03\), a year of life is worth about 433,000 dollars, against 250,000 without discounting.

What it predicts

  • The value of a year of life rises with \(V\) and falls with \(T\). Discounting raises it: later years count for less, so for the same \(V\) each year must be worth more.
  • Even without discounting, the value of the life expectancy lost in the pandemic is many times the lost output of equation (12), which for the United States was a few percent of one year’s income per person.

def percent_change(new, old):
    """Percent change from old to new: 100 (new / old - 1)   (equation 1)."""
    return 100 * (new / old - 1)


def peak_to_trough(series, start, end):
    """The highest point between start and end, the lowest point after it, and the fall in percent."""
    window = series[start:end]
    peak = window.idxmax()                      # date of the highest value
    trough = window[peak:].idxmin()             # date of the lowest value after the peak
    return peak, trough, percent_change(window[trough], window[peak])


def low_to_high(series, start, end):
    """The opposite measure: the highest point between start and end, the lowest point before it, and the rise."""
    window = series[start:end]
    high = window.idxmax()                      # date of the highest value
    low = window[:high].idxmin()                # date of the lowest value before it
    return low, high, window[high] - window[low]

For a series that rises in a recession, such as the unemployment rate, low_to_high measures the rise from the lowest point to the highest one, in the units of the series (percentage points for a rate). Two more functions compute equations (2) and (3).

Python note. ** means “to the power of”, and np.log is the natural logarithm. An f-string such as f"g = {x:.2f}" writes the value of x into the text; :.2f shows two decimals and :+.1f one decimal with its sign.

def annual_growth(factor, years):
    """Average growth rate per year when a series is multiplied by `factor` in `years` years (equation 2)."""
    return factor ** (1 / years) - 1


def rise_needed(fall):
    """Percent rise needed to undo a fall by the fraction `fall`: 100 f / (1 - f)   (equation 3)."""
    return 100 * fall / (1 - fall)

def run_loss(withdrawals, cash, discount):
    """Loss from selling illiquid assets at a discount to pay withdrawals beyond the cash (equation 9)."""
    if withdrawals <= cash:
        return 0.0
    return discount * (withdrawals - cash) / (1 - discount)

def pick(panel, names, column):
    """Rows of the chosen countries that have a value in `column`, and the chosen countries without data."""
    chosen = panel[panel.country.isin(names) & panel[column].notna()]
    missing = []
    for name in names:
        if name not in set(chosen.country):
            missing.append(name)
    return chosen, missing


def no_data_note(missing):
    """A second, smaller title line that names the chosen countries without data."""
    if missing:
        return "<br><sup>No data: " + ", ".join(missing) + "</sup>"
    return ""
def largest_fall(chosen, column, start, end):
    """Among the chosen countries, the largest peak-to-trough fall between start and end."""
    worst = None
    for name, rows in chosen.groupby("country"):
        series = rows.set_index("date")[column]
        if len(series[start:end]) > 1:
            peak, trough, change = peak_to_trough(series, start, end)
            if worst is None or change < worst[3]:
                worst = (name, peak, trough, change)
    return worst                                # (country, peak date, trough date, percent change)


def largest_rise(chosen, column, start, end):

BUSTS is a dictionary: each name (the key) gives a pair of dates (the value), and BUSTS[bust] looks one up. start, end = ... unpacks the pair into two variables. px.line draws the index as a line, and log_y=True gives the ratio scale. fig.add_vrect shades the bust, and fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(...)) adds the two black dots for the peak and the trough. In an f-string, {peak:%b %Y} prints a date as “Dec 2005”, and {change:+.1f} a number with its sign. On a ratio (log) axis, Plotly wants the axis range as powers of ten, so we pass np.log10(...). With xref="paper" and yref="paper", a label’s position is a share of the plot area (0 = left or bottom, 1 = right or top), not a date or a value.

    """Turn a log change 100 ln(P_t / P_t-12) into a percent change 100 (P_t / P_t-12 - 1)."""
    return 100 * (np.exp(log_change / 100) - 1)

def axis_top(value, tops=(12, 25, 50, 100, 150)):
    """The smallest of a few fixed axis tops that is at least `value`."""

(a) The spread was \(4.50 - 4.22 = 0.28\) points, so \(p \approx 0.28\) percent a year. Lenders saw almost no risk of a Greek default.

(b) Divide \((1-p)(1+i) = 1+i^f\) by \(1+i\), then solve for \(p\):

\[1 - p = \frac{1+i^f}{1+i} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = 1 - \frac{1+i^f}{1+i} = \frac{i - i^f}{1+i}.\]

(c) With recovery, the expected payoff of the loan must still equal the safe payoff:

\[(1-p)(1+i) + pR(1+i) = 1+i^f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (1+i)\,\big[1 - p(1-R)\big] = 1+i^f.\]

Solving as in (b), \(p(1-R) = 1 - (1+i^f)/(1+i) = (i - i^f)/(1+i)\), so

\[p = \frac{i - i^f}{(1+i)(1-R)}.\]

With \(R = 0\) this is the result of (b). To see the spread, solve the same condition for \(1+i = (1+i^f)/[1 - p(1-R)]\) and subtract \(1+i^f\):

\[i - i^f = \frac{(1+i^f)\,p(1-R)}{1 - p(1-R)} \approx p(1-R)\]

when \(p\) and \(i^f\) are small. The spread prices the expected loss: the chance of a default times the share of the money lost in it. For the same \(p\), a higher recovery means a smaller spread, which the Model overview stated in words.

\[\frac{c}{100}\,y = \frac{m}{10^6}\,\Delta L\,v \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m^* = \frac{10^6\,c\,y}{100\,\Delta L\,v}.\]

Without discounting, \(m^* = 10^6\times2{,}400/(10\times250{,}000) = 960\) deaths averted per million; with \(r = 3\) percent, \(m^* = 555\). On the sliders, the title reads “break-even” at 960 when \(r = 0\), and it turns from “not worth it” to “worth it” between 550 and 555 when \(r = 0.03\). The break-even rises in proportion to the cost \(c\) (and to income \(y\)), falls in proportion to the years saved \(\Delta L\), and falls with \(r\), because discounting raises the value of each year, \(v\).

(c) Only what the lockdown itself changes should count. If people would have stayed home anyway, much of the lost output would have been lost without the lockdown, so its own cost is smaller than the whole fall in GDP. For the same reason, staying home on one’s own would have averted some deaths, so the deaths averted by the lockdown itself are fewer. Both sides of the comparison shrink, and the answer depends on which shrinks more. The calculator also leaves out illness that does not kill, lost schooling, who pays the cost and who gains the life-years, and the uncertainty about \(m\) and \(\Delta L\) when the decision has to be made. If most of the deaths averted are of older people, who have fewer years ahead, try 5 years of life per death: the break-even doubles.
